**Bottom line:** Cultural humility is a stance, not a body of facts about groups. Stay curious about how this client defines their identity, notice your own assumptions, name power differences, and repair missteps openly. Clients' ratings of their therapist's cultural humility and competence track outcomes, while therapists' self-ratings do not, so ask the client instead of trusting your own sense of being competent.

## The principle

Tervalon and Murray-García proposed cultural humility as a better training goal than "competence" understood as mastery of a finite body of knowledge. It involves lifelong self-evaluation and self-critique, redressing power imbalances in the clinical relationship, and building respectful partnerships [Tervalon-MurrayGarcia-1998]. Hook and colleagues defined it for psychotherapy as an interpersonal stance that is other-oriented, respectful and free of superiority toward the client's cultural background. In four studies, clients who rated their therapist as more culturally humble reported a stronger therapeutic alliance and more improvement, and the alliance mediated that link [Hook-etal-2013].

The American Psychological Association (APA) Multicultural Guidelines say that identity is fluid and intersectional, that clinicians are cultural beings whose biases shape their formulations, and that clinicians should follow the client's own self-definition and terms [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017]. In practice, cultural humility has three working parts:

1. **Self-awareness.** Know your own identities, assumptions and blind spots.
2. **Other-oriented curiosity.** Treat what you know about a group as a hypothesis to check with this person.
3. **Power and repair.** Name the difference in power and position, invite correction, and repair missteps without defensiveness.

## Guidance

- If you are meeting any new client → ask how they describe themselves and which parts of their background matter for this problem, then use their words in notes and formulation. Because the guidelines advise following the client's self-definition rather than categorical assumptions [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].
- If you want to check your blind spots before or after a session → scan Hays's ADRESSING factors for you and the client: age and generation, disability, religion, ethnicity, social status, sexual orientation, Indigenous heritage, national origin and gender. Because the model was built to expose a counselor's biases and gaps in experience and to weigh which identities are salient [Hays-1996].
- If race, culture or another identity may bear on the problem but the client has not raised it → broach it once, early, as an open invitation the client can decline. Because broaching means considering how sociopolitical factors such as race shape the client's concerns, and it is described as strengthening the alliance [Day-Vines-etal-2007].
- If the explanation of illness, help-seeking or symptom expression seems shaped by culture → use the Cultural Formulation Interview. Because it is a set of 16 questions built for this, with supplementary modules for children and adolescents, older adults, and immigrants and refugees [APA-CFI-Site].
- If you share a background or language with the client → still ask rather than assume. Because language and culture can be experienced differently even when shared [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].
- If a remark of yours lands badly or you made an assumption → name it, apologize briefly, ask what it was like, and return to the client's agenda; do not defend your intent. Because microaggressions, often unintended, harm the alliance [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].
- If you are unsure whether the work feels culturally safe to the client → ask directly at the end-of-session feedback point. Because client-rated multicultural competence correlated r = 0.38 with outcomes across 15 studies, but therapist self-ratings correlated only r = 0.06 [Soto-etal-2018].
- If a belief or practice is unfamiliar to you → learn from the client first, then from consultation or community sources, and never ask the client to educate you about a whole group. Because guidelines ask clinicians to move beyond formulations built on limited knowledge [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].

**What to record:** identity and background in the client's own words, which identities the client says matter for the problem, language preference, any cultural explanation of the problem, and any rupture and how it was repaired. For tool designers: make identity fields optional and free text, and never infer identity from a name, photo or accent.

## Example phrasings

- "People describe their background in lots of different ways. How do you describe yours, and does any of it matter for what brings you here?"
- "I'm aware we come from different backgrounds. If anything I say doesn't fit your experience, I want you to tell me. You're the expert on your life."
- "Some people wonder whether a therapist can understand experiences like racism or being treated differently. If that comes up for you here, I'd like to hear it."
- "I made an assumption just now, and I think I got it wrong. Can you tell me how it actually is for you?"
- "What do people in your family or community think is causing this? What do they think would help?"
- Avoid: "I don't see color. I treat everyone the same."
- Avoid: "As a [group member], you must find it hard to..."

## Evidence

The link between client-perceived humility and outcome comes from correlational studies [Hook-etal-2013] and a meta-analysis of 15 studies of therapist multicultural competence [Soto-etal-2018]. No randomized trial has shown that humility training improves client outcomes, so the specific behaviors above rest on guideline recommendations and expert consensus [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017; Day-Vines-etal-2007].
