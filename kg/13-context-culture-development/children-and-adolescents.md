**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) works for young people, most clearly for anxiety, but it must be pitched at the child's cognitive and emotional level: more concrete, behavioral and visual for younger children, and more autonomy and confidentiality for adolescents. Involve parents as coaches when the child is young or parent behavior maintains the problem; adding parents by default does not reliably improve outcomes.

## The principle

Effects are real but smaller than in adults. A Cochrane review of 87 trials found remission of the primary anxiety disorder in 49.4% after CBT versus 17.8% on waitlist (number needed to treat, 3), with little to no evidence that CBT beats usual care or alternative treatments, and no trials at all in children with intellectual impairment [James-etal-2020]. Across 447 youth trials the mean effect was 0.46: 0.61 for anxiety and 0.29 for depression [Weisz-etal-2017]. For depression, therapy effects were g = 0.35 in children and g = 0.55 in adolescents, against g = 0.77 in middle-aged adults [Cuijpers-etal-2020b]. In a large anxiety trial for ages 7 to 17, 14 sessions of Coping Cat based CBT (with weekly parent check-ins and two parent-only sessions) led to 59.7% much improved, versus 80.7% for CBT plus sertraline and 23.7% for placebo [Walkup-etal-2008].

Guidelines in the United Kingdom (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, NICE) say to adapt therapy to developmental level. For 12 to 18 year olds with moderate to severe depression, offer individual CBT for at least 3 months; for 5 to 11 year olds, individual CBT is one option alongside family-based interpersonal psychotherapy, family therapy and psychodynamic therapy [NICE-NG134]. For social anxiety, individual CBT is 8 to 12 sessions of 45 minutes, or group CBT 8 to 12 sessions of 90 minutes, with psychoeducation and skills training for parents, especially of young children [NICE-CG159].

## Guidance

- If the child is young or thinks concretely → lead with behavioral methods (graded exposure, rewards, parent coaching), use pictures, feelings faces and rating thermometers, and teach short coping thoughts rather than weighing evidence. Because NICE asks for treatment that takes account of cognitive and emotional maturity [NICE-CG159], and effects are larger in older children and adolescents [Reynolds-etal-2012].
- If the child cannot or will not speak to you → accept information from parents, but give the child a chance to answer by writing, drawing or speaking through the parent. Because socially anxious children may be reluctant to speak to strangers [NICE-CG159].
- If parent anxiety, reassurance or accommodation maintains the problem → work with the parent as a coach for exposure and coping. Because, for children aged 5 to 12, brief guided parent-delivered CBT (up to 8 sessions, about 5 hours of therapist time) did as well as a comparison therapy at lower cost [Creswell-etal-2017].
- If you are deciding whether to add parent sessions → decide from the formulation, not by default. Because parent involvement was not associated with better outcomes in a meta-analysis [Reynolds-etal-2012], and trials of added parent components give inconsistent results [Breinholst-etal-2012].
- If the client is an adolescent → let them set their own goals, explain confidentiality and its limits at the start, and agree with them what is shared with parents. Because NICE asks clinicians to respect young people's rights to confidentiality and explain why information is shared [NICE-CG159].
- If a young person with social anxiety disorder is about 15 or older and can manage an adult protocol → consider the adult version. Because the NICE social anxiety guideline suggests adult interventions for young people, typically 15 and older, with that capacity [NICE-CG159].
- If a young person under 16 in England or Wales wants therapy without parental involvement → assess Gillick competence: understanding, reasoning, risks and freedom from pressure. Because that is the legal test for consenting without a parent [NSPCC-Gillick]. In the United States, minor consent rules vary by state; see `14-ethics-law.informed-consent`.
- If the young person reports self-harm, suicidal thoughts or abuse → assess risk the same day, follow your service's risk and safeguarding procedures (see `02-risk.safeguarding`), involve parents or carers unless that would increase risk, tell the young person who needs to know and why, and share crisis contacts: 988 or 911 in the United States; 999, NHS 111 (the National Health Service helpline) or Samaritans on 116 123 in the United Kingdom. Because safety and safeguarding duties do not wait for the next session.

**What to record:** who attended, the young person's own goals, the confidentiality agreement, parent role, and ratings from both young person and parent, since informants often disagree [Weisz-etal-2017].

## Example phrasings

- To a young child: "Worry is like a smoke alarm. It's meant to keep you safe, but sometimes it beeps when there's only toast. Let's find out when your alarm is too loud."
- To an adolescent: "What you tell me stays between us, unless I'm worried about your safety or someone else's. If that happens, I'll talk with you first about who needs to know and how."
- To a parent: "This week your job is coach, not rescuer. When she gets scared, remind her of her brave plan and praise her for trying."
- Avoid: "Your mom says you're being difficult. Why is that?"

## Evidence

Supported by randomized trials and Cochrane review for anxiety [James-etal-2020]; NICE recommends CBT for adolescent depression [NICE-NG134], though youth depression effects are modest [Weisz-etal-2017]. Developmental tailoring is guideline-recommended but rests largely on expert consensus.
