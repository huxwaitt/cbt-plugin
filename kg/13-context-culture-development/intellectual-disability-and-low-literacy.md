**Bottom line:** People with mild to moderate intellectual disability benefit from adapted cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and behavioral activation, and low literacy is common in the general population. Use plain spoken language, pictures and simple rating scales, short chunks checked with teach-back, more doing than verbal reasoning, measures built for the population, and a supporter for between-session practice when the client agrees.

## The principle

Mental health problems are common in people with intellectual disability (called learning disabilities in the United Kingdom): about 40% of adults at any time, or 28% excluding problem behaviors. Symptoms are often wrongly attributed to the disability or a physical problem [NICE-NG54]. Psychological therapies show a moderate overall effect (g = 0.682), and CBT is effective for anger and depression [Vereenooghe-Langdon-2013]. In a trial of 161 adults with mild to moderate intellectual disability and depression, behavioral activation and guided self-help both produced within-group improvements at 12 months, with no difference between them [Jahoda-etal-2017].

The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends following the condition guideline, tailoring the intervention to the person's understanding, strengths, impairments and communication needs, agreeing goals, pace and measures with the person and their supporters, and offering structured help with practice between sessions. It names adapted CBT for depression in milder intellectual disability, relaxation for anxiety and graded exposure for anxiety and phobias [NICE-NG54].

Low literacy is often hidden. In 2023, 28% of United States adults aged 16 to 65 scored at or below Level 1, the two lowest literacy bands, up from 19% in 2017, though the survey cautions about comparing the two rounds [NCES-PIAAC-2024].

## Guidance

- If the client cannot understand the nature of therapy, what will be shared or what the options are → adapt the consent explanation with simple words and pictures, involve a supporter if the client wants, and follow your local law on capacity (see `14-ethics-law.informed-consent`). Because NICE asks that information about treatment match the person's language and communication needs [NICE-NG222].
- If you are giving any questionnaire or worksheet → offer to go through it aloud together as a routine choice, not a test. Because many adults read at a basic level and may not say so [NCES-PIAAC-2024].
- If you are measuring outcomes for someone with intellectual disability → use tools developed or adapted for this group, and interpret standard tools with caution. Because NICE recommends this [NICE-NG54].
- If you have explained something → ask the client to say it back in their own words, one chunk at a time. Do not ask "Do you understand?" Because yes/no checks miss misunderstanding, while teach-back confirms it [AHRQ-TeachBack].
- If the client tends to agree with whatever you suggest → use open or picture-based either/or questions rather than yes/no or leading questions, and check answers against observed behavior. Because yes/no questions invite agreement regardless of meaning (expert consensus).
- If abstract thought work is hard → teach a three-part model (thoughts, feelings, actions) with pictures, use feelings faces or a traffic-light scale, and weight the plan toward activity scheduling, relaxation and graded exposure. Because NICE recommends relaxation and graded exposure for anxiety in this group [NICE-NG54], and behavioral activation helps depression [Jahoda-etal-2017].
- If the client agrees to involve a supporter → agree goals, how the person shows distress, pace and measures with them, and ask them to prompt practice between sessions. Because NICE notes people may need more structured support to apply new skills [NICE-NG54].
- If behavior or mood changes → assess physical health, pain and medication before assuming a psychological cause or putting it down to the disability. Because NICE warns of misattribution [NICE-NG54].
- If you are assessing suicide or self-harm risk → ask directly in concrete words, check understanding with teach-back, and gather a supporter's observations with consent; escalate as for any client (United States: 988, call or text, or 911; United Kingdom: 999, NHS 111 (the National Health Service helpline) or Samaritans on 116 123). Because NICE asks clinicians to assess all potential psychopathology, not only the first-reported symptoms [NICE-NG54].
- If the client has low literacy without intellectual disability → keep adult content and pace, but swap writing for drawings, voice notes, phone photos or a recorded session summary. Because NICE asks that information match the person's language and communication needs [NICE-NG222].
- If communication is very limited → use the person's usual method (sign language, symbols or a communication aid) and ask a family member or carer about symptoms as well. Because NICE recommends both routes [NICE-NG222].

**What to record:** communication method, reading support needed, supporter role and the client's consent to it, adapted measures used, and teach-back checks of key plans.

## Example phrasings

- "Lots of people find these forms tricky. Would you like to go through it out loud together?"
- "I want to check I explained that clearly. Can you tell me what you'll try this week?"
- "Is your worry big like a red light, middle like an amber light, or small like a green light?"
- Avoid: "Do you understand?"
- Avoid: talking to the supporter about the client as if the client were not in the room.

## Evidence

Supported by meta-analysis [Vereenooghe-Langdon-2013] and one large randomized trial without a no-treatment control [Jahoda-etal-2017]; NICE recommendations are mostly "consider" level [NICE-NG54]. A Cochrane review of youth anxiety found no trials in children with intellectual impairment, so child evidence is a gap [James-etal-2020]. Teach-back comes from health literacy practice, not therapy trials [AHRQ-TeachBack].
