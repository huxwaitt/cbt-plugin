**Bottom line:** Diagnostic overshadowing is attributing a new problem to a condition or identity the client already has. Treat every change from the client's own baseline as something to assess: mental health, physical health, medication, pain and life events. Autism, intellectual disability, age and minority identity raise the risk of treatable problems; they do not explain them away.

## What it looks like

- An autistic adult becomes more withdrawn and rigid, and this is called "more autistic" rather than assessed for depression.
- A person with intellectual disability starts self-injuring or shouting, and it is logged as "challenging behavior" without checking for pain, illness, trauma or low mood.
- An older adult's poor sleep, low energy and memory complaints are filed under "normal aging" or "early dementia" without considering depression, delirium or medication.
- A transgender client's panic attacks are assumed to be "about the transition," or, the opposite error, gender-related stress is never asked about.
- A refugee's nightmares are put down to "cultural differences."

## Why it happens

- The existing label is salient and offers a ready explanation.
- Communication differences make standard interviews harder, so clinicians rely on labels.
- Beliefs that low mood is expected in later life or in disability.
- Lack of a baseline for comparison.

The consequences are serious. National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance notes that symptoms in people with intellectual disability are sometimes wrongly attributed to the disability or a physical problem rather than a change in mental health [NICE-NG54]. In one clinic cohort of adults newly diagnosed with Asperger syndrome, 66% reported lifetime suicidal ideation [Cassidy-etal-2014]. Depression in older adults responds to therapy about as well as in midlife [Cuijpers-etal-2020b], so missing it denies effective care.

## The fix

- If behavior or mood changes → compare with the person's own baseline, using family, carers or past records with consent. Because NICE asks clinicians to assess all potential psychopathology, not just the first-reported symptoms [NICE-NG54].
- If cognition seems to decline → check for reversible causes such as delirium, depression, hearing or sight loss and medicines with anticholinergic effects before accepting a dementia explanation. Because NICE asks for these to be investigated before referral for dementia diagnosis [NICE-NG97].
- If a transgender or gender nonconforming client presents with a mental health concern → assess it on its own terms and ask about minority stress. Because guidelines say concerns may or may not relate to gender identity [APA-TGNC-Guidelines-2015].
- If communication is limited → use adapted tools, observation and informants rather than concluding nothing can be assessed. Because NICE advises tools developed or adapted for people with learning disabilities, interpreted with care when they are not [NICE-NG54].
- If physical causes are possible (pain, constipation, infection, medication change) → refer for medical review (see `12-medical-pharmacology.when-to-refer-medically`).
- If risk is possible → ask about suicide and self-harm directly in an accessible format and, if there is a plan, intent or recent preparation, arrange same-day emergency assessment (see `02-risk.crisis-response`); crisis contacts are 988 or 911 in the United States and 999, NHS 111 (the National Health Service helpline) or Samaritans on 116 123 in the United Kingdom.

## Sources note

The misattribution warning is from NICE guidance [NICE-NG54]; NICE describes the problem without using the term "diagnostic overshadowing", which comes from intellectual disability research not cited here. Its frequency in older, autistic and gender-diverse clients is plausible but less studied.
