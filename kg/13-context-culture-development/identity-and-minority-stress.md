**Bottom line:** Stigma adds ongoing stress for members of minority groups: outside events such as discrimination, violence and rejection, and inside processes such as expecting rejection, concealing identity and internalized stigma. In cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), map these as real stressors and as maintaining processes, validate the reality of discrimination, and target the avoidance, rumination and self-blame that follow. The identity itself is never the treatment target.

## Components

Meyer's minority stress model explains the higher rates of mental disorder in lesbian, gay and bisexual people through stigma, prejudice and discrimination. Its stress processes are prejudice events, expectations of rejection, hiding and concealing, and internalized homophobia, offset by coping and community [Meyer-2003]. Hatzenbuehler's mediation framework adds that stigma-related stress raises general emotion dysregulation, social and interpersonal problems and unhelpful cognitive processes, and these mediate the link to disorder [Hatzenbuehler-2009].

The same logic applies beyond sexual orientation. Across 293 studies, reported racism was associated with poorer mental health (r = -0.23) [Paradies-etal-2015]. Guidelines for transgender and gender nonconforming (TGNC) people describe stigma, discrimination and violence as drivers of poor health [APA-TGNC-Guidelines-2015]. Microaggressions, brief everyday slights, carry cumulative stress for people of minority status [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].

| Layer | Examples | Where it sits in a CBT formulation |
|---|---|---|
| Distal stressors | Discrimination, harassment, rejection, structural barriers | Situations and context |
| Proximal processes | Vigilance, expected rejection, concealment, internalized stigma | Thoughts, beliefs, safety behaviors |
| General processes | Rumination, emotion dysregulation, isolation, substance use | Maintaining cycles |
| Resources | Community, affirming relationships, pride, coping skills | Strengths and protective factors |

## How it keeps the problem going

- Vigilance and expected rejection lead to avoidance and safety behaviors, which block disconfirming experiences and shrink support.
- Concealment costs effort, adds suppression and limits closeness.
- Internalized stigma feeds core beliefs such as "Something is wrong with me".
- Rumination after an incident deepens low mood.
- Substance use and other avoidance give short relief and long-term harm [Pachankis-etal-2015].

Some fear is accurate. Where danger is real, the task is safety planning and problem solving, not disputation.

## Drawing it with the client

Draw the client's usual five-part cycle, then add an outer ring for stigma-related events and the messages the client absorbed. Ask: "Where did you first learn that about yourself?" Separate what happened (the discrimination) from how mind and body have learned to respond. One adaptation in the Effective Skills to Empower Effective Men (ESTEEM) protocol was to help clients attribute distress to minority stress rather than to personal failure [Pachankis-etal-2015].

## Treatment targets it implies

ESTEEM adapted the Unified Protocol over 10 sessions: strengths, the impact of minority stress, its emotional, behavioral and cognitive effects, cognitive restructuring, an avoidance hierarchy, graded behavioral experiments and assertiveness. In 63 young gay and bisexual men, it reduced depression, alcohol problems and sexual risk behavior against a 3-month waitlist; the effect on anxiety was only marginal [Pachankis-etal-2015]. Targets therefore include:

- Internalized stigma beliefs (restructure these, not reports of discrimination).
- Avoidance driven by expected rejection, tested only where the client judges consequences tolerable.
- Assertiveness and coping with incidents.
- Connection to affirming community and identity strengths [APA-SexualMinority-Guidelines-2021].

## Guidance

- If the client describes discrimination → validate it as real before anything else. Because distal stressors are real events in the model [Meyer-2003]. Avoid: "Are you sure it was about your race?"
- If the client asks you to change their sexual orientation → do not offer it; explore the distress, values and conflicts behind the request and offer affirming support. Because American Psychological Association (APA) guidelines state that efforts to change sexual orientation cause harm [APA-SexualMinority-Guidelines-2021].
- If a TGNC client presents with anxiety or depression → assess it fully without assuming gender identity is the cause or that it is irrelevant. Because mental health concerns may or may not relate to gender identity and minority stress [APA-TGNC-Guidelines-2015].
- If the client wants to test whether a feared rejection will happen → plan the experiment with them in settings they judge physically and socially safe, and agree in advance what they will do if rejection does occur. Because ESTEEM used graded behavioral experiments and assertiveness training for minority stress only in safe situations [Pachankis-etal-2015].
- If suicidal thoughts are present → complete a full risk assessment and safety plan, escalate to emergency care if there is a plan, intent or recent preparation, and share crisis contacts: in the United States, 988 (call or text) or 911, and for lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer and other sexual and gender minority (LGBTQ+) young people the Trevor Project at 1-866-488-7386 or text START to 678-678 [Trevor-Project-Site]; in the United Kingdom, 999, NHS 111 (the National Health Service helpline) or Samaritans on 116 123. Because a plan, intent or preparation signals acute risk that needs same-day action (see `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`).
