**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) works for depression and anxiety in later life; effects for depression are similar to those in midlife, and effects for anxiety are somewhat smaller. Adapt the delivery (pace, sensory access, memory aids, booster contacts, coordination with medical care) more than the content. Check cognition and physical health early, and challenge ageist beliefs, including your own.

## The principle

Psychotherapy for depression showed g = 0.66 in adults aged 55 to 75 and g = 0.97 in those over 75, not significantly different from middle-aged adults [Cuijpers-etal-2020b]. Meta-analyses of CBT in older people found it better than waitlist or usual care for depression [Gould-etal-2012a] and anxiety [Gould-etal-2012b], but not clearly better than active controls. For generalized anxiety disorder, effects were g = 0.55 in older people and g = 0.94 in working-age adults; the difference was not significant, but older-adult protocols rarely drew on gerontological evidence to make them age-appropriate [Kishita-Laidlaw-2017].

In mild to moderate dementia, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises considering psychological treatment for mild to moderate depression or anxiety and not routinely offering antidepressants [NICE-NG97]. A Cochrane review found that CBT-family therapies (CBT, behavioral activation, problem-solving therapy) probably reduce depressive symptoms slightly in dementia and mild cognitive impairment (standardized mean difference -0.23) [Orgeta-etal-2022]. The American Psychological Association's updated guidelines (adopted 2024) set out 21 guidelines covering attitudes, aging, intersecting identities, clinical issues, assessment, intervention, consultation and training, with more emphasis on diversity and telehealth [APA-OlderAdults-Guidelines-2024].

## Guidance

- If you are planning the first session → ask about hearing, vision, reading and preferred format, then seat the client facing you in a quiet, well-lit room and give large-print written summaries. Because standard protocols were rarely adapted to older clients' needs [Kishita-Laidlaw-2017]. (Specific adjustments here are expert consensus.)
- If memory or thinking has changed recently → screen briefly, refer for cognitive assessment, and look for reversible causes such as delirium, depression, hearing or sight loss, or medicines with anticholinergic effects. Because NICE asks for these to be investigated before referral to a dementia diagnostic service [NICE-NG97]. Sudden confusion over hours or days may be delirium: arrange same-day medical review (expert consensus).
- If the client has mild dementia → simplify, lean on behavioral activation and problem solving, use written prompts, and involve a carer with consent. Because the evidence in dementia is mainly for behavioral and problem-solving approaches [Orgeta-etal-2022].
- If the client has several health conditions or medicines → coordinate with primary care and consider a collaborative care model. Because NICE recommends collaborative care particularly for older people and those with significant physical illness [NICE-NG222].
- If anxiety is being treated with benzodiazepines → raise it with the prescriber, and never advise the client to stop or cut down on their own. Because their risks in later life include falls, hip fractures and memory problems [Stanley-etal-2009], and dose changes need medical supervision (see `12-medical-pharmacology.anxiolytics-and-hypnotics`).
- If travel, mobility or caring roles limit attendance → offer home, community or telephone sessions and planned booster calls. Because NICE suggests community and primary care settings particularly for older people [NICE-NG222], and a primary care trial added telephone boosters at 4, 7, 10 and 13 months [Stanley-etal-2009].
- If the client says "It's normal to feel like this at my age" or "I'm too old to change" → treat it as a belief to test, not a fact. Because depression responds to treatment in late life as in midlife [Cuijpers-etal-2020b].
- If losses dominate (bereavement, retirement, health) → separate realistic grief from depressive interpretations and rebuild valued activity within current capacity. Because normal grief is not a treatment target, but depressive interpretations and lost activity are (expert consensus).
- If generational or cultural background shapes views of therapy → use the older adults module of the Cultural Formulation Interview. Because the module explores how age and generation shape views of distress and help [APA-CFI-Site].
- If the client mentions hopelessness, being a burden or wanting to die → ask directly about suicide, complete a risk assessment and safety plan, and share crisis contacts: 988 or 911 in the United States; 999, NHS 111 (the National Health Service helpline) or Samaritans on 116 123 in the United Kingdom. Because these statements can signal suicidal thinking the client may not name (expert consensus).

**What to record:** sensory and format needs, cognitive screen result and referral, medical conditions and medicines, carer involvement and consent, and agreed adaptations.

## Example phrasings

- "Some people like a written copy of what we cover. Would that help, and what size print is easiest?"
- "You said you're too old to change. Shall we treat that as an idea to test? What would we expect to see if it were true?"
- "Let's do one small step this week that fits your energy, and I'll call you midweek to see how it went."
- Avoid: "Well, at your age, some low mood is to be expected."

## Evidence

Supported by randomized trials and meta-analyses for depression and anxiety [Cuijpers-etal-2020b; Gould-etal-2012a; Gould-etal-2012b]; evidence in dementia is of low to moderate certainty [Orgeta-etal-2022]. Sources disagree on anxiety: one meta-analysis suggests lower efficacy than in working-age adults [Gould-etal-2012b], another found no significant difference and noted that no older-adult trial used an intention-to-treat analysis [Kishita-Laidlaw-2017]. Delivery adaptations are expert consensus.
