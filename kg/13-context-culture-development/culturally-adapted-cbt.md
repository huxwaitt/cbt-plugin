**Bottom line:** Cultural adaptation means systematically changing how cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is delivered (language, metaphors, explanation of the problem, goals, methods and context) while keeping its active ingredients. Adapted interventions outperform unadapted versions of the same therapy in several meta-analyses, though effects shrink after correction for publication bias and one large youth meta-analysis found standard therapy worked as well for minority samples. Adapting the explanation of the illness so it fits the client's own beliefs appears to matter most.

## When to use

- The client's preferred language, idioms of distress or literacy differ from those the materials assume.
- The client explains the problem in spiritual, somatic, moral or social terms that the standard cognitive model does not address.
- Family or community roles shape decisions, goals or what counts as a good outcome.
- Mistrust of services, stigma or practical barriers keep the client from engaging.
- A service is building a program for a defined community.

Group membership alone is not a reason to change the protocol. Assess first; adapt to the person.

## How it works

Bernal and colleagues' ecological validity framework names eight dimensions a treatment can be made culturally congruent on: language, persons (the client and therapist match and relationship), metaphors, content, concepts, goals, methods and context [Bernal-etal-1995]. Hwang's psychotherapy adaptation and modification framework offers a similar route, illustrated with recent Asian American immigrants [Hwang-2006]. Arundell and colleagues group adaptations into therapist-related, content-related and organization-level changes; organization-level changes such as outreach and access may add the most [Arundell-etal-2021].

The likely mechanism is credibility and engagement. In a direct-comparison meta-analysis, adapting the "illness myth", the shared explanation of what is wrong and why the treatment will help, was the only moderator of the advantage of adapted over unadapted therapy [Benish-etal-2011].

## Steps

1. **Assess before adapting.** Ask about language preference, the client's explanation of the problem, what their family and community think, and expectations of a helper (see `10-diagnosis.cultural-formulation-interview`).
2. **Name the active ingredients you will keep.** For example, exposure for anxiety, activation for depression, and testing of key beliefs.
3. **Bridge the explanations.** Present the CBT model alongside the client's explanation, not instead of it: "Both can be true. Stress and worry can also keep this going, and that part we can work on together."
4. **Translate language and metaphors.** Use the client's words for distress, proverbs and examples from their life. Have materials translated by a qualified translator and use measures validated in that language.
5. **Fit goals and methods.** Include family if the client wants it, allow a more directive style if expected, and build homework around existing roles, routines and practices.
6. **Fit the context.** Offer sessions outside working hours and in community settings, with bilingual therapists or professional interpreters, and actively reach groups who face stigma or access barriers. Because the UK depression guideline asks services to build these into care pathways [NICE-NG222].
7. **Measure and review.** Track outcomes and ask for feedback on fit. Drop adaptations that do not help.

## Guidance

- If the client says "This is God's will" or "It's my nerves" → explore that explanation with respect and look for the shared ground before introducing the cognitive model. Because adapting the illness explanation is the adaptation most linked to better outcomes [Benish-etal-2011].
- If you are tempted to drop exposure or another core technique because it "doesn't suit the culture" → keep the technique and adapt its rationale, pace and examples instead. Because the framework adapts delivery dimensions, not the treatment's change processes [Bernal-etal-1995].
- If you are adapting for one client → base each change on what this client told you. Because studied moderators of adaptation (age, language match, ethnicity) are inconsistent across meta-analyses [Rathod-etal-2018].
- If the client prefers a language other than yours → offer a qualified interpreter or bilingual therapist rather than simplifying the protocol, and use measures validated in that language. Because language access is the first adaptation guidelines name [NICE-NG222] (see `13-context-culture-development.working-with-interpreters`).
- If outcomes are not improving after an adaptation → review the formulation and the client's feedback before adding further cultural changes. Because adapted and unadapted therapy both fail some clients, and adaptation is not a fix for a wrong formulation (expert consensus).

## Common problems

- **Surface-only adaptation.** Translated worksheets, unchanged assumptions. Fix: adapt concepts and goals, not just words.
- **Stereotyped adaptation.** Assuming every member of a group is collectivist or religious. Fix: assess the individual.
- **Adapting away the active ingredient.** Fix: keep the change process, adapt its packaging.
- **No evaluation.** Fix: measure outcomes in the client's language.

## Evidence

Hall and colleagues pooled 78 studies with 13,998 participants, 95% of them not European American. Adapted interventions beat other conditions (g = 0.67) and unadapted versions of the same intervention (g = 0.52), with 4.68 times the odds of remission [Hall-etal-2016b]. Benish and colleagues found adapted therapy beat unadapted bona fide therapy by d = 0.32 [Benish-etal-2011]. Soto and colleagues pooled 99 studies (d = 0.50, falling to 0.35 after correcting for publication bias) [Soto-etal-2018]. Against non-adapted active treatments for ethnic minority groups, g = -0.43 (favoring adaptation) across 30 trials [Arundell-etal-2021]. A review of meta-analyses reported effects from 0.23 to 0.75 [Rathod-etal-2018].

Not all evidence agrees. A 447-study meta-analysis of youth therapy found that standard therapy effects did not differ between Caucasian and minority samples [Weisz-etal-2017]. Evidence strength: supported by randomized trials and meta-analyses, with high heterogeneity, frequent waitlist comparisons and few studies isolating which adaptation helps.
