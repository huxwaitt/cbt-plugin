**Bottom line:** Perinatal depression and anxiety are common, and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a guideline-recommended first-line treatment [Vigod-etal-2025; NICE-CG192]. Ask every perinatal client about unwanted thoughts of harm to the baby: they are very common, and in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) they do not signal danger [Collardeau-etal-2024; Fairbrother-etal-2022]. Postpartum psychosis is a psychiatric emergency [RCPsych-Postpartum-Psychosis]. Medication decisions belong to the prescriber, and safeguarding referrals rest on evidence of risk, not on intrusive thoughts alone.

## The principle

Perinatal depression affects about 11.9% of women [Woody-etal-2017]. A diagnosed anxiety disorder affects about 15.2% in pregnancy and 9.9% in the first 24 postnatal weeks [Dennis-etal-2017]. OCD affected 2.07% in pregnancy and 2.43% postpartum, against 1.08% in the general female population [Russell-etal-2013b].

In a cohort of 763 women, 95.8% had unwanted postpartum thoughts of accidental harm to the infant and 53.9% of intentional harm, mostly fading by six months [Collardeau-etal-2024]. Neither intentional-harm thoughts nor OCD was linked to self-reported physical aggression toward the infant [Fairbrother-etal-2022].

Postpartum psychosis newly affects 0.89 to 2.6 per 1000 women [VanderKruik-etal-2017] and usually starts in the first two weeks after birth [RCPsych-Postpartum-Psychosis].

## Guidance

- If you see a pregnant or postnatal client → ask the two Whooley depression questions and the two-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-2); follow a positive answer with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) or Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), and a GAD-2 of 3 or more with the GAD-7. Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) suggests this [NICE-CG192]; the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) guideline adds bipolar disorder, psychosis and suicidality [ACOG-CPG4-2023].
- If you score the EPDS → treat 11 or more as a positive screen (sensitivity 0.81, specificity 0.88), use 13 or more when false positives are costly, and confirm by interview. Because of pooled accuracy data [Levis-etal-2020a].
- If depression is subthreshold or mild to moderate → consider facilitated self-help; for moderate or severe depression consider high-intensity CBT or medication via the prescriber; assess within 2 weeks of referral and start therapy within 1 month of assessment. Because NICE sets these options and timescales [NICE-CG192], and Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) guidance lists CBT, interpersonal therapy (IPT), behavioral activation and mindfulness-based therapies as first-line [Vigod-etal-2025].
- If a pregnant or postpartum client has risk factors (past depression, subthreshold symptoms, low income, recent partner violence, adolescent or single parenthood) → refer for preventive CBT or IPT. Because the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) gives this grade B; counseling cut perinatal depression by 39% [USPSTF-Perinatal-2019].
- If a parent hints at "bad thoughts" about the baby → ask directly and calmly, normalize, then assess: unwanted, horrifying thoughts with checking, reassurance seeking or avoidance suggest anxiety or OCD; intent without distress, hallucinations, delusions or confusion suggest psychosis or real risk. Because CANMAT advises separating intrusive thoughts, which come with checking, reassurance seeking and avoidance, from intent [Vigod-etal-2025].
- If it is perinatal OCD → offer CBT with exposure and response prevention (ERP), including dropping avoidance of baby care. Because CANMAT recommends it (second-line, as perinatal evidence is limited) [Vigod-etal-2025], and intensive CBT reduced postpartum OCD symptoms with controlled effect sizes of 1.31 to 1.90 [Challacombe-etal-2017].
- If you are planning sessions with a new parent → let the baby attend, offer video sessions, and ask about the bond with the baby. Because NICE asks clinicians to assess the mother-baby relationship at all postnatal contacts [NICE-CG192].
- If mania, psychosis or confusion starts suddenly after birth, especially with a history of bipolar disorder or postpartum psychosis → treat it as an emergency: arrange same-day emergency psychiatric assessment (US: crisis team or emergency department; UK: perinatal or crisis team), and call 911 or 999 if anyone is in immediate danger. Because NICE expects assessment within 4 hours of referral [NICE-CG192], and postpartum psychosis carries a risk of maternal suicide and harm to the infant [Vigod-etal-2025].
- If medication comes up in pregnancy or breastfeeding → do not advise starting, stopping or changing it; route it to the prescriber and help the client prepare questions. Because NICE asks for a discussion of the higher threshold for medication [NICE-CG192], CANMAT advises against reducing or stopping antidepressants near delivery [Vigod-etal-2025], and prescribing is outside most therapists' scope.
- If you are weighing a safeguarding referral → refer on evidence of abuse, neglect, intent, psychosis or inability to care for the baby, following local safeguarding or US state mandated-reporting procedures; not on intrusive thoughts alone. Because NICE directs suspected maltreatment to safeguarding protocols [NICE-CG192], and CANMAT names these as the signs to assess [Vigod-etal-2025].
- If there are suicidal thoughts, including an EPDS answer endorsing self-harm → assess risk the same day, follow `02-risk.crisis-response`, and share contacts: US 988 (call or text) or 911, plus the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline, 1-833-TLC-MAMA (1-833-852-6262); UK 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because these are the current routes [Lifeline-988; MMHLA-Hotline-2023; NHS-Urgent-Help; Samaritans-Site].

## Example phrasings

- "Lots of new parents get sudden, horrible thoughts about the baby getting hurt, even by them. Has anything like that popped into your head?"
- "The fact that this thought horrifies you tells me how much you care. Thoughts are not actions."
- Avoid: "I'm going to have to report that thought."

## Evidence

Psychological treatment of perinatal depression: 43 studies, g = 0.67, number needed to treat 4.39, high heterogeneity [Cuijpers-etal-2023b]. Preventive counseling is supported by randomized trials [USPSTF-Perinatal-2019]. Perinatal OCD treatment rests on a pilot trial of 34 mothers [Challacombe-etal-2017], and intrusive-thought advice on cohort data [Fairbrother-etal-2022] and expert consensus.
