**Bottom line:** Socioeconomic deprivation goes with more mental health problems and somewhat poorer therapy outcomes, yet low-income clients benefit from evidence-based cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) when services remove barriers. Ask about money, housing, work and food as routinely as about sleep, put real stressors in the formulation, fix logistics, use problem solving and small acts of advocacy for practical problems, and keep cognitive restructuring for interpretations, not for the hardship itself.

## The principle

A systematic review of 17 studies (165,574 patients) found that 12 reported significant links between socioeconomic indicators and psychological therapy outcomes; unemployment had a small negative effect in a sensitivity analysis [Finegan-etal-2018]. In England's national talking therapies data, poorer areas had more mental health problems and lower recovery rates; adjusting service benchmarks for deprivation cut the share of areas judged underperforming from 72.5% to 43.1% [Delgadillo-etal-2016]. Debt is strongly linked to mental disorder (odds ratio 3.24), depression (2.77) and suicide (7.9), though causality is unclear [Richardson-etal-2013].

Low-income clients do benefit from evidence-based care, but logistical, attitudinal and systemic barriers keep many from getting it [Santiago-etal-2013]. Depressed low-income women are hard to engage, and many interventions do not address their barriers [Levy-OHara-2010]. The American Psychological Association (APA) guidelines ask clinicians to examine their own social class bias, respect the client's priorities, reduce barriers, consider small acts of advocacy, and understand the effects of unemployment [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019].

## Guidance

- If you are doing an intake → ask about income adequacy, debt, housing stability, food, work hours and shifts, caring duties, transport, and phone and data access. Because survival needs and logistics shape whether therapy is usable [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019].
- If daytime sessions are impossible → offer evening or weekend times, telephone or video sessions, or community settings. Because flexible scheduling and alternative delivery are recommended [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019; NICE-NG222].
- If the client misses sessions → assume practical barriers before low motivation, and problem-solve them together (reminders, travel help, childcare, shorter sessions). Because barriers include transport, waiting lists and intake processes that need a permanent phone or address [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019]. Avoid: labeling the client "noncompliant".
- If an urgent practical crisis dominates (eviction, no food, unpaid bills) → make it the agenda, use structured problem solving, and link to housing, benefits, food or debt advice services. Because writing support letters and coordinating care can strengthen the alliance and count as intervention [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019].
- If the thought is accurate ("I can't pay the rent this month") → problem-solve the situation and work on the added meaning ("so I'm a failure"), not on the fact. Because disputing real hardship invalidates the client (see `13-context-culture-development.pitfall-disputing-realistic-adversity`).
- If the client is in serious debt → ask about suicidal thoughts, and if they are present complete a risk assessment and safety plan (see `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`) alongside debt advice. Because debt is strongly associated with suicide [Richardson-etal-2013]. Crisis contacts: 988 or 911 in the United States; 999, NHS 111 (the National Health Service helpline) or Samaritans on 116 123 in the United Kingdom.
- If homework needs money, time, privacy or a printer → design free activities that fit the client's shifts and space, on paper or phone as the client prefers. Because barriers of cost and time apply between sessions as well as to attendance [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019].
- If cost blocks access → consider sliding-scale fees and plan for gaps in insurance. Because APA lists fee flexibility among useful advocacy steps [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019].
- If the client is unemployed or underemployed → include work goals and link to employment support where available. Because APA guidelines highlight the mental health effects of unemployment [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019].

**What to record:** current material stressors, barriers to attendance, adjustments offered, referrals and letters made, and how stressors appear in the formulation.

## Example phrasings

- "Money, housing and work stress can affect mood a lot. Is anything like that weighing on you right now?"
- "You've missed a couple of sessions. I'm guessing something practical got in the way. What would make it easier to get here, or should we switch to phone?"
- "Not being able to pay the rent is a real problem, and we'll plan for it. I'm also curious about the thought that came after: that it means you've failed."
- Avoid: "If you really wanted to get better, you'd find a way to come."

## Evidence

The link between deprivation and outcomes comes from observational studies, and the review authors call it preliminary [Finegan-etal-2018]. Practice recommendations are guideline-based and expert consensus [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019]. United Kingdom guidance likewise asks services to offer out-of-hours and community-based care (recommendation 1.16.4) and to address groups facing access barriers, including people who are homeless, refugees and asylum seekers (recommendation 1.16.5) [NICE-NG222].
