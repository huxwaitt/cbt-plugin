**Bottom line:** Some negative thoughts are accurate. When a thought describes real discrimination, poverty or danger, validate it, then problem-solve the situation and examine only the extra meanings the client adds, such as self-blame or hopelessness. Disputing the fact itself invalidates the client and can blame them for their circumstances.

## What it looks like

- Client: "They didn't hire me because I'm Black." Therapist: "What's the evidence for and against that?"
- Client: "We could be evicted next month." Therapist labels it "catastrophizing" and sets a thought record.
- A gay client's fear of being attacked in a hostile neighborhood is treated as a safety behavior to drop.
- The client goes quiet, says "You don't get it," or stops attending.

## Why it happens

- Applying cognitive restructuring to every negative thought by habit.
- Not separating the situation from the appraisal in the formulation.
- Discomfort with structural problems the therapist cannot fix.
- Social distance: the therapist has not lived the adversity and underestimates it.

Discrimination and deprivation are real stressors with measurable effects. Minority stress theory locates the source of excess distress in stigma, prejudice and discrimination [Meyer-2003], and reported racism is associated with poorer mental health across 293 studies [Paradies-etal-2015]. Low-income clients face real survival needs and barriers [Santiago-etal-2013].

## The fix

- If the thought describes a real event or risk → validate first: "That sounds unfair, and it makes sense you're angry." Because minority stressors are external events, not distortions [Meyer-2003].
- If the situation is real → switch to problem solving, assertiveness, safety planning or practical help. Because guidelines ask clinicians to respect the client's priorities within their real circumstances [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019].
- If the client adds a meaning to the fact ("so I'm worthless", "it will always be like this", "it's my fault") → target that meaning with standard cognitive methods. Because an adaptation in the Effective Skills to Empower Effective Men (ESTEEM) protocol was helping clients attribute distress to minority stress rather than personal failure [Pachankis-etal-2015].
- If fear leads to avoidance → sort situations with the client into unsafe (keep protective behavior), uncertain (gather information), and safe-but-avoided (test with behavioral experiments). Because the minority stress-adapted protocol tested avoidance only in situations judged safe [Pachankis-etal-2015].
- If the client describes current violence, threats or abuse → treat it as a safety issue: assess risk, make a safety plan, and follow safeguarding or emergency routes (see `02-risk.harm-to-others` and `02-risk.safeguarding`; in an emergency call 911 in the United States or 999 in the United Kingdom). Because real danger calls for protection, not cognitive work (expert consensus).
- If you are unsure whether a thought is accurate → ask the client how they know and what others in their situation experience, without implying they imagined it.
- If you notice the client withdraw after a challenge → name it and repair. "I think I questioned something that really happened to you. I'm sorry. Can we go back to it?"

Example sorting question: "Which part of this is the situation itself, and which part is what your mind says it means about you?"

## Sources note

The evidence that discrimination and poverty harm mental health is strong but largely observational [Paradies-etal-2015]. The fix steps follow from minority stress-adapted cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [Pachankis-etal-2015] and practice guidelines [APA-LowIncome-Guidelines-2019]; no trial compares disputing versus validating realistic thoughts.
