**Bottom line:** Do not let family, friends, children or untrained staff interpret therapy except as a brief emergency stopgap. Ad hoc interpreters make more clinically serious errors, cannot be neutral, and may be the very people the client cannot speak freely in front of. Book a qualified interpreter and document any exception.

## What it looks like

- An adult son or a teenage daughter interprets a parent's assessment.
- Answers come back shorter than the questions, or the relative answers without asking the client.
- Questions about suicide, alcohol, sex, abuse or family conflict get "No, nothing like that."
- The client looks at the relative before answering.
- A bilingual receptionist interprets because "it's quicker."

## Why it happens

- No interpreter was booked, or booking feels slow or expensive.
- The client or family asks for it, and refusing feels rude.
- The clinician underestimates how much is lost or changed.

The cost is real. In 13 recorded pediatric encounters, interpreters made a mean of 31 errors per visit; omission was the commonest type, and 77% of ad hoc interpreters' errors (the ad hoc interpreters included nurses, social workers and an 11-year-old sibling) had potential clinical consequences, against 53% for hospital interpreters [Flores-etal-2003]. Professional interpreters bring communication and outcomes close to those of patients without language barriers [Karliner-etal-2007]. Professional standards require interpreting everything without adding, omitting or substituting, and staying impartial, which a relative cannot reliably do [NCIHC-Standards-2005]. In therapy the stakes include unreported risk and abuse, especially when the interpreter is part of the family system.

## The fix

- If a relative or friend offers to interpret → thank them, explain that a professional interpreter is standard for everyone, and book one (telephone or video if needed today). Because National Health Service (NHS) England primary care guidance says patients should always be offered a registered interpreter [NHSE-Interpreting-2018].
- If the client asks for the relative → ask privately with a qualified interpreter present, and document the request. Because United States rules for covered health programs allow an accompanying adult, outside an imminent-threat emergency, only on the client's private request with a qualified interpreter present, the adult's agreement and documentation [CFR-45-92-201]; NHS England guidance requires consent sought independently in the client's language and recorded [NHSE-Interpreting-2018].
- If the only option is a child → do not use them except as a temporary measure in an emergency, then confirm everything with a qualified interpreter. Because United States rules allow a minor child only in an imminent-threat emergency [CFR-45-92-201], and NHS England guidance rules out anyone under 16 except when immediate and necessary treatment is required [NHSE-Interpreting-2018].
- If a family member has been interpreting → re-ask risk and safeguarding questions with a professional interpreter and the family member out of the room. Because abuse or coercion by the person interpreting cannot be disclosed in front of them (expert consensus).
- If a bilingual colleague offers → use them only if they are qualified to interpret. Because covered programs must not rely on unqualified staff [CFR-45-92-201].
- If a risk emergency happens with no interpreter → act on safety with whatever communication is available, call 988 or 911 in the United States or 999 in the United Kingdom as needed, and get a qualified interpreter as soon as possible. Because the United States rule permits an ad hoc interpreter only as a stopgap in an imminent-threat emergency, with a qualified interpreter confirming the communication afterward [CFR-45-92-201].

## Sources note

Error rates come from a small transcript study in pediatrics [Flores-etal-2003] and a systematic review of medical care [Karliner-etal-2007]; no trial has tested family interpreters in psychotherapy. The United States rule applies to entities covered by Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act; check which rules apply to your setting.
