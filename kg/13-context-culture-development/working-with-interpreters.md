**Bottom line:** Use a trained, qualified interpreter, not family, friends, children or untrained staff. In one service comparison, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for refugees delivered through interpreters worked as well as CBT without them. Book about double time, brief the interpreter beforehand, speak directly to the client in short segments, use professionally translated materials, debrief afterward, and record the language, interpreter and any decisions about who interprets.

## When to use

- The client has limited English proficiency, even if they manage everyday conversation. Events may be encoded in the language spoken when they happened and carry more emotion in that language, though some clients use a second language for distance, so follow the client's lead [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].
- The client uses a signed language or another communication method [NICE-NG222].
- Interpreting is needed for assessment, risk discussions, consent and homework, not just the first session.

## Steps

1. **Book well.** Record language and dialect. Book a qualified interpreter (in person, video or telephone), meet any reasonable preference for interpreter gender, and request the same interpreter across sessions. Allow extra time, typically double a standard appointment, and record the language need as a visible alert [NHSE-Interpreting-2018]. Check the interpreter does not know the client socially.
2. **Brief the interpreter (a few minutes, by expert consensus).** Explain CBT's structure, the session plan, that distressing content may come up, confidentiality, and that everything said must be interpreted without adding, omitting or substituting [NCIHC-Standards-2005]. Ask which CBT terms ("anxiety", "thought", "belief") lack a direct equivalent and agree wording.
3. **Set up the room.** Seat the three of you in a triangle so you face the client. Speak to the client as "you", and expect the interpreter to encourage you and the client to address each other directly [NCIHC-Standards-2005].
4. **Run the session.** Use short sentences, one question at a time, and pause for interpretation. Avoid idioms and jargon, simplify Socratic questions, and ask the client to summarize key points back.
5. **Handle materials.** Use worksheets and measures translated by a qualified translator and, for measures, validated in that language. If machine translation is used for important content, a qualified human translator must review it (United States regulation) [CFR-45-92-201].
6. **Handle risk.** Ask about suicide and harm as usual, in plain words. Interpreters are expected to interpret everything and, under National Health Service (NHS) England primary care guidance, to be trained annually in safeguarding [NHSE-Interpreting-2018]. If risk is imminent, act on safety at once with whatever communication is available (United States: 988, call or text, or 911; United Kingdom: 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans 116 123), and confirm with a qualified interpreter as soon as one is reachable; see `02-risk.crisis-response`.
7. **Debrief briefly.** Ask about cultural meanings you may have missed, any part that was hard to interpret, and how the interpreter is coping with distressing material. The interpreter is expected to flag significant cultural misunderstandings [NCIHC-Standards-2005].

## What to record

Language and dialect, interpreter name or badge number and agency, mode (in person, video, telephone), any interpreter preference, terms agreed for key concepts, cultural issues raised in debrief, and every decision about who interpreted and why, including any client request to use a family member or friend.

## Guidance

- If the client asks for a relative or friend to interpret → offer a professional interpreter first and ask about the request privately. Because United States health programs covered by Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act may rely on an accompanying adult only as an imminent-threat emergency stopgap, or on the client's private, documented request made with a qualified interpreter present and the adult's agreement [CFR-45-92-201]; NHS England guidance requires independent consent in the client's language, recorded [NHSE-Interpreting-2018].
- If only a child is available → do not use them except as a temporary measure in an emergency until a qualified interpreter arrives. Because United States rules allow a minor child only as a stopgap in an imminent-threat emergency [CFR-45-92-201], and NHS England guidance bars anyone under 16 except when immediate and necessary treatment is required [NHSE-Interpreting-2018].
- If the interpreter starts summarizing, explaining or answering for the client → pause and ask for everything to be interpreted as said. Because accuracy and completeness are core interpreter standards [NCIHC-Standards-2005].
- If a bilingual colleague offers to help → use them only if they are qualified to interpret. Because covered programs must not rely on unqualified staff [CFR-45-92-201].
- If you use video or telephone interpreting → make sure the audio is clear and, for video, both faces are visible. Because these are legal requirements for remote interpreting in covered programs [CFR-45-92-201].

## Common problems

- **Talking to the interpreter about the client.** Fix: address the client directly.
- **Long speeches.** Fix: two or three sentences, then pause.
- **A new interpreter each time.** Fix: request continuity at booking.
- **Interpreter distress after trauma work.** Fix: debrief and signpost support.

**Evidence.** Professional interpreters improve communication, use of services, outcomes and satisfaction, bringing care close to that for patients without language barriers [Karliner-etal-2007]. Ad hoc interpreters' errors were more often clinically consequential (77% versus 53%) [Flores-etal-2003]. In one non-randomized service comparison, refugees treated with CBT for posttraumatic stress disorder through interpreters did as well as those treated without [DArdenne-etal-2007]. The step-by-step practices are expert consensus and professional standards. United States federal language-access policy has shifted since 2025; check current enforcement guidance for your setting.
