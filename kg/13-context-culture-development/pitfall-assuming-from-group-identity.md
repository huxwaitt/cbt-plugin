**Bottom line:** Group knowledge is a starting hypothesis, never a description of the person in front of you. Ask before you assume, use the client's own words for their identity, and when you get it wrong, say so and repair. The opposite error, ignoring culture entirely, fails clients in the same way.

## What it looks like

- The therapist opens with a statement about the client's group: "I'm sure it's been really hard to deal with people's reactions to your disability." The American Psychological Association (APA) guidelines use this example to show how a well-meant remark can miss the client's actual experience and focus [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].
- Family involvement, a directive style or faith-based content is added because of the client's ethnicity, without asking.
- An older client is offered a slower, simplified version of therapy they did not need.
- The formulation names "cultural factors" in general terms, not the client's own account.
- The mirror image: "I treat everyone the same," so identity-related stress never enters the formulation.
- The client gives short answers, corrects the therapist, stops sharing, or does not return.

## Why it happens

- Training that teaches facts about groups without teaching how to check them with individuals.
- Time pressure and the wish to show cultural knowledge.
- Discomfort raising race, faith or sexuality, which pushes the therapist toward either stereotype or silence.
- Overconfidence. Therapists' own ratings of their multicultural competence are almost unrelated to outcomes (r = 0.06), unlike clients' ratings (r = 0.38) [Soto-etal-2018].
- Treating adaptation research as a rule. Group-level moderators of adapted treatment are inconsistent across meta-analyses [Rathod-etal-2018], so they cannot predict what one person needs.

## The fix

- If you notice a prediction forming from a group label → turn it into a question. "Some people from similar backgrounds find family very involved in decisions like this. How is it for you?" Because the guidelines ask clinicians to move beyond categorical assumptions and follow the client's self-definition [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].
- If you are unsure which identities matter → scan Hays's ADRESSING factors (age, disability, religion, ethnicity, social status, sexual orientation, Indigenous heritage, national origin, gender), then ask which ones the client sees as relevant. Because the model aims to surface clinician bias and weigh salience for each client [Hays-1996].
- If a remark lands badly → name it and repair. "I made an assumption there. Can you tell me how it actually is for you?" Because clients who see their therapist as culturally humble report stronger alliances and more improvement [Hook-etal-2013].
- If you have been avoiding culture altogether → broach it once, openly, and let the client decide its relevance. Because the guidelines say attending to what the client finds salient about their identity, rather than the therapist's assumptions, matters for process and outcome [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017].
- If you supervise → ask trainees what they assumed about each client and how they checked it. Because therapists' self-ratings of cultural competence do not track outcomes [Soto-etal-2018].

## Sources note

The disability example and the definition of microaggressions come from the APA Multicultural Guidelines [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017]. Outcome links are correlational [Hook-etal-2013; Soto-etal-2018]. That assumption-driven practice causes dropout is plausible and consistent with these data but has not been tested directly.
