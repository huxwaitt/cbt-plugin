**Bottom line:** Ask every client whether faith or spirituality matters to them, and if it does, how it bears on the problem and on coping. When a client wants it, weaving their beliefs and practices into cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) works about as well as standard CBT for symptoms and adds spiritual wellbeing. Respect the belief itself, and test only the unhelpful appraisals and behaviors around it.

## The principle

Religion is one of the cultural influences Hays lists for clinicians to consider in themselves and their clients [Hays-1996], and clinicians are encouraged to learn language that affirms a client's religious identity [APA-Multicultural-Guidelines-2017]. A meta-analysis of 97 outcome studies (7,181 clients) found that therapy tailored to clients' religious or spiritual beliefs improved psychological functioning (g = 0.74 against no treatment; g = 0.33 against alternative secular therapies). In the more rigorous additive designs, it matched standard therapy on distress (g = 0.13, not significant) and improved spiritual wellbeing (g = 0.34) [Captari-etal-2018]. In a randomized pilot trial of 132 adults with major depression and chronic medical illness (ten sessions, mostly by telephone), religiously integrated CBT and conventional CBT were equivalent overall, with a slight advantage for religious CBT in more religious participants [Koenig-etal-2015].

So faith integration is a matter of client preference and engagement, not a stronger treatment for everyone.

## Guidance

- If you are taking a history → ask a brief, open spiritual history: whether the client has a faith or source of meaning, how important it is, whether they belong to a community, and how they would like it addressed in care. Because these are the four domains of the FICA spiritual history tool (faith, importance, community, address in care) [GWish-FICA].
- If the client's explanation of the problem is spiritual → explore it respectfully and look for the overlap with the CBT model before introducing your own. Because cultural explanations of illness shape presentation and care [APA-CFI-Site].
- If the client wants their faith included → use their texts, practices and community as resources: scripture or teachings as evidence in thought work, prayer or ritual as scheduled valued activity, and faith community as social support. Because religiously tailored therapy is at least as effective and improves spiritual wellbeing [Captari-etal-2018].
- If the client does not want faith discussed → respect that and keep therapy secular. Because the benefit depends on client preference and religiosity [Koenig-etal-2015].
- If a belief is central to the client's faith → do not dispute the belief. Examine the appraisal built on it instead, such as "God is punishing me because I'm worthless", and ask what their tradition says about mercy, forgiveness or suffering. Because the goal is to change unhelpful appraisals, not faith (expert consensus).
- If religious themes appear as obsessions or compulsions (scrupulosity) → assess and treat as obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), plan exposures that do not require the client to break their own religious rules, and, with consent, consult a trusted religious leader. Because scrupulosity is OCD, and exposures that respect the faith keep treatment acceptable (expert consensus; see `ocd.treatment`).
- If you are unsure whether an unusual belief is a shared religious belief or a symptom → ask whether others in the client's community hold it, and gather collateral information with consent. Because a belief widely held in the client's community is not by itself a symptom (expert consensus).
- If faith-based guilt or a belief about punishment comes with hopelessness or thoughts of death → ask directly about suicide and follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`; a moral or religious objection to suicide can be protective but does not rule risk out. Crisis contacts: 988 (call or text) or 911 in the United States; 999, NHS 111 (the National Health Service helpline) or Samaritans on 116 123 in the United Kingdom. Because risk assessment applies whatever the explanation of distress (expert consensus).
- If your own beliefs differ strongly from the client's → notice the reaction, keep it out of the work, and use supervision. Because clinicians are asked to examine their own biases [Hays-1996].

**What to record:** the client's faith or meaning system in their own words, its importance, community supports, how they want it handled in therapy, and any faith-based resources used in the plan.

## Example phrasings

- "Some people find faith or spirituality important, and some don't. Is it part of your life?"
- "How does your faith make sense of what you're going through? Does it help, add pressure, or both?"
- "You said God must be angry with you. What does your tradition teach about how God responds to people who are struggling?"
- Avoid: "Let's leave religion out of this. We'll focus on the evidence."
- Avoid: "Is there really any proof that God is involved?"

## Evidence

Supported by a large meta-analysis [Captari-etal-2018] and a pilot randomized trial [Koenig-etal-2015]. The symptom benefit over standard therapy disappears in the most rigorous additive designs, so the case for integration rests on preference, engagement and spiritual wellbeing. Practices for scrupulosity and for telling religious belief from symptom rest on expert consensus.
