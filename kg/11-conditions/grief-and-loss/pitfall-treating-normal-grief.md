**Bottom line:** Grief is not a disease, and most bereaved people adjust without professional psychological help [Stroebe-etal-2007]. Preventive interventions offered to all bereaved people have not shown benefit [Wittouck-etal-2011]. Diagnose prolonged grief disorder (PGD) only after the minimum time since the death and only when grief clearly exceeds the client's own cultural and religious norms; until then, support, normalize and monitor.

## What it looks like

- A client seen 6 weeks after a death is told they have "complicated grief" and is booked for a 16-session protocol.
- A high score on a grief scale at 4 months is written up as PGD.
- A widow observing her community's year of mourning is described as "stuck" and urged to "move on."
- Everyone bereaved in a service is routinely referred for counseling regardless of need.
- Sadness, crying and longing in the first months are disputed with thought records as if they were distortions.

## Why it happens

- Intense early grief looks like a disorder: acute grief brings strong emotion and preoccupation with the deceased, and integration usually takes around 6 to 12 months [Szuhany-etal-2021].
- Clinicians apply their own cultural timetable for mourning.
- A wish to help leads to offering treatment rather than watchful support.
- Scale scores are read without the time and norms requirements.

## The fix

- If an adult's loss is under 12 months old (the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision, DSM-5-TR) or 6 months or less old (the International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision, ICD-11) → do not diagnose PGD under that system; offer support and a planned review. Because DSM-5-TR chose 12 months to avoid diagnosing grief too soon [Prigerson-etal-2021], and ICD-11 requires more than 6 months [WHO-ICD11-2022].
- If mourning is long but fits the client's cultural or religious norms → treat it as normal bereavement. Because ICD-11 gives no diagnosis for grief within a normative mourning period [WHO-ICD11-2022], and accepted mourning periods vary, for example the year of mourning generally accepted in societies with a dominant Christian tradition [Killikelly-Maercker-2018].
- If a service wants to offer help to every bereaved person → offer information and access, and reserve treatment for those with persistent impairing grief, depression or posttraumatic stress. Because preventive interventions were not effective while treatment for complicated grief was [Wittouck-etal-2011], and intervention is best targeted at high-risk people [Stroebe-etal-2007].
- If early grief is intense → normalize, check for suicide risk, depression and trauma, and arrange a follow-up. Because high or moderate early grief often declines without intervention [Szuhany-etal-2021].

Example phrasing: "What you are feeling is what grief is like for many people at this stage. Let's check in again in a couple of months and see how things are moving."

Avoid: "Your grief is abnormal; we need to work through it now." It pathologizes a normal response and can increase distress.

## Sources note

Timing and norms rules come from DSM-5-TR as reported by its developers [Prigerson-etal-2021] and from ICD-11 [WHO-ICD11-2022]. The case against routine preventive intervention rests on a meta-analysis of 14 randomized trials [Wittouck-etal-2011] and a major review [Stroebe-etal-2007]. Evidence strength: meta-analysis and diagnostic manual rules. Always check risk even in normal grief (`grief-and-loss.pitfall-missing-suicide-risk-in-grief`).
