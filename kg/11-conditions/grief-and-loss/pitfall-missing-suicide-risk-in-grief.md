**Bottom line:** Prolonged grief disorder (PGD) raises suicide risk on its own: complicated grief, its earlier research name, predicted high suicidality beyond depression and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [Latham-Prigerson-2004]. "I just want to be with her" can be longing, a wish to die, or both. Ask directly every time, assess fully, plan for safety, and keep asking as treatment revisits the death.

## What it looks like

- The client says "I want to be where he is" and the therapist reflects the love and moves on.
- "Life is meaningless without her" is treated as a grief symptom to restructure, with no risk question.
- Risk is screened at intake and not again, even during sessions that revisit the death.
- A client bereaved by suicide is not asked about their own suicidal thoughts because "they know what it does to a family."

## Why it happens

- Yearning to be with the deceased is a core grief experience, so its suicidal meaning is easy to miss. A wish to join the deceased is associated with PGD [Szuhany-etal-2021].
- Therapists fear that asking will seem insensitive to the loss.
- Attention goes to depression measures, which may not capture grief-related suicidal thinking.

The risk is real: in 309 bereaved adults, complicated grief carried about 6.6 times the odds of high suicidality about 6 months after the loss and predicted later high suicidality (about 8.2 times the odds) after controlling for depression and PTSD [Latham-Prigerson-2004]. A PGD diagnosis predicted later suicidality in a United States (US) sample [Prigerson-etal-2021], and 20% to over 50% of treatment-seekers report suicidal ideation [Szuhany-etal-2021].

## The fix

- If the client voices a wish to be with the deceased, or that life is meaningless → ask directly about thoughts of dying or suicide in that session. Because complicated grief predicted suicidality independently of depression and PTSD [Latham-Prigerson-2004].
- If the death was by suicide → ask about the client's own suicidal thoughts at every session. Because suicide-bereaved young adults had higher odds of a suicide attempt than those bereaved by sudden natural death (adjusted odds ratio 1.65) [Pitman-etal-2016].
- If suicidal thoughts are present → assess intent and plans (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`), build a safety plan that includes restricting access to lethal means, involve supports with consent, increase contact, and consult your supervisor. Because a wish to join the deceased can carry suicidal intent that standard grief work does not address [Szuhany-etal-2021].
- If risk is raised → keep grief treatment going alongside the safety plan rather than stopping it. Because suicidal ideation fell more in adults given grief treatment than in those given medication or placebo alone [Shear-etal-2016].
- If there is imminent danger → do not leave the client alone and contact emergency services. In the US, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911; in the United Kingdom (UK), call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because these are the 24-hour crisis routes in each country [Lifeline-988; NHS-Urgent-Help; Samaritans-Site].

Example phrasing: "When you say you want to be with him, some people mean they miss him terribly, and some mean they have thought about dying so they could be with him. Is either true for you?"

Avoid: "You wouldn't do anything to yourself, would you?" It invites a reassuring "no."

Record the words used, the direct question and answer, the risk formulation, the safety plan and who was informed.

## Sources note

Risk associations come from cohort and validation studies [Latham-Prigerson-2004; Prigerson-etal-2021], a clinical review [Szuhany-etal-2021] and a UK-wide cross-sectional survey of 3,432 bereaved young adults [Pitman-etal-2016]. Evidence that grief treatment lowers suicidal ideation comes from one large randomized trial [Shear-etal-2016]. Evidence strength: observational studies, one randomized trial and expert consensus.
