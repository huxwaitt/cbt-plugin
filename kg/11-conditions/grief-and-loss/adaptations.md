**Bottom line:** Keep the core of grief-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): facing the loss, working with beliefs and rebuilding life. Adapt the frame. Culture and faith define normal mourning, so judge grief against the client's own norms and work with their rituals [WHO-ICD11-2022; Killikelly-Maercker-2018]. Violent and suicide losses, young people, older adults and comorbidity change the plan; suicidal thinking changes the priority.

## The principle

Grief is universal, but how it is expressed varies [Stelzer-etal-2020]. Mourning periods differ, such as the year of mourning generally accepted in societies with a dominant Christian tradition, like the German "mourning year" (Trauerjahr). Display rules differ: in Taiwan, widows are discouraged from crying in front of the recently deceased. The meaning of experiences differs: for Cambodian refugees, seeing the dead in dreams or visions was part of normal bereavement [Killikelly-Maercker-2018]. Standard items can miss bodily distress after loss, and Chinese bereaved people seldom share grief with others [Stelzer-etal-2020]. Prolonged grief disorder (PGD) requires grief that clearly exceeds the person's own cultural and religious norms [WHO-ICD11-2022].

## Guidance

- If mourning is long but fits the client's cultural or religious norms → support rather than diagnose or treat. Because grief within a normative mourning period is normal bereavement [WHO-ICD11-2022].
- If the client sees, hears or senses the deceased → explore what it means to them and their community before treating it as a symptom of psychosis. Because such experiences can be normal in some cultures [Killikelly-Maercker-2018; Stelzer-etal-2020].
- If funeral or mourning rituals were disrupted (distance, migration, a pandemic, no body recovered) → discuss culturally appropriate rituals and how they could still be completed or adapted. Because World Health Organization (WHO) Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) guidance, as summarized by Killikelly and Maercker, recommends discussing culturally appropriate adjustment and mourning rituals when managing grief [Killikelly-Maercker-2018].
- If faith shapes how the client understands the death → work inside that frame and, with consent, involve faith leaders (`13-context-culture-development.religion-and-spirituality`). Because religious beliefs and practices may account for some of the variation in grieving [Stelzer-etal-2020].
- If the client is a child or adolescent → apply the 6-month threshold and use a youth protocol with parallel caregiver sessions. Because the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) sets 6 months for young people [Prigerson-etal-2021], and 9 child sessions plus 5 parent sessions of CBT beat supportive counseling in 134 young people [Boelen-etal-2021].
- If the client is an older adult → offer the standard protocol without lowering expectations. Because complicated grief treatment achieved 70.5% response versus 32.0% for interpersonal psychotherapy in adults aged 50 or older [Shear-etal-2014].
- If the death was violent, sudden or by suicide → screen for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and plan to treat both (`ptsd.treatment`). Because PGD affects an estimated 49% after unnatural losses [Djelantik-etal-2020].
- If the client was bereaved by suicide → ask about suicidal thoughts at every session and address stigma. Because young adults bereaved by suicide had higher odds of a suicide attempt than those bereaved by sudden natural death (adjusted odds ratio 1.65), a difference no longer significant after adjusting for perceived stigma [Pitman-etal-2016].
- If suicidal thoughts are present → assess fully (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`) and complete a safety plan, including restricting access to lethal means, before revisiting the death; for imminent danger in the United States, call or text 988 or call 911, and in the United Kingdom (UK), call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because revisiting is emotionally demanding and these are the 24-hour crisis routes [Lifeline-988; NHS-Urgent-Help; Samaritans-Site].
- If depression is severe → refer to a prescriber to consider medication alongside grief treatment. Because adding citalopram to grief treatment reduced depressive symptoms but not grief [Shear-etal-2016].
- If attending in person is hard → consider guided online delivery that centers on exposure. Because therapist-guided internet exposure reduced grief versus a waiting list in a trial of 47 mourners, while online activation had 59% dropout [Eisma-etal-2015].
- If a UK client wants bereavement support between sessions → give the Cruse Bereavement Support helpline, 0808 808 1677, free from UK landlines and mobiles. Because it offers bereavement support by phone, but it keeps daytime hours (one evening a week) and is not a crisis line, so pair it with the crisis routes above [Cruse-Helpline].

## Example phrasings

- "How would people in your family and community expect you to be grieving at this point?"
- "Some people sense their loved one nearby. Has that happened to you, and what does it mean to you?"
- "Were you able to say goodbye in the way that matters in your tradition?"
- Avoid: "It has been a year; you should be moving on by now." It imposes the therapist's norms and invalidates the bond.

## Evidence

Cultural guidance rests on expert consensus and cross-cultural studies [Killikelly-Maercker-2018; Stelzer-etal-2020]. The youth and older adult adaptations are each supported by a randomized trial [Boelen-etal-2021; Shear-etal-2014]. Online delivery has emerging evidence from a small trial [Eisma-etal-2015].
