**Bottom line:** Most bereaved people grieve intensely and then adapt without professional help [Stroebe-etal-2007]. Prolonged grief disorder (PGD) describes the minority whose longing for or preoccupation with the person who died stays intense, daily and impairing long after the death: at least 12 months for adults in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR), and more than 6 months in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11) [Prigerson-etal-2021; WHO-ICD11-2022]. It responds to grief-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [Komischke-Konnerup-etal-2024] and carries an independent suicide risk, so always ask [Latham-Prigerson-2004].

## Diagnostic essentials

DSM-5-TR requirements, paraphrased [Prigerson-etal-2021]:

| Criterion | Requirement |
|---|---|
| A. Timing | Death of a close person at least 12 months ago (at least 6 months for children and adolescents) |
| B. Core grief | Intense yearning for the person, or preoccupation with thoughts or memories of them (in young people, possibly with the circumstances of the death), nearly every day for at least the last month |
| C. Symptoms | At least 3 of 8, nearly every day for at least the last month: identity disruption, marked disbelief, avoiding reminders that the person is dead, intense emotional pain (such as anger, bitterness or sorrow), difficulty reengaging with life, emotional numbness, feeling life is meaningless, intense loneliness |
| D. Impairment | Clinically significant distress or impairment |
| E. Norms | Duration and severity clearly exceed the person's social, cultural or religious norms |
| F. Exclusion | Not better explained by depression, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), another disorder, a substance or medical condition |

United States (US) code: F43.81 in the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) [CDC-ICD-10-CM-2027].

**ICD-11 (6B42).** After the death of a partner, parent, child or other close person, there is persistent, pervasive longing for or preoccupation with the deceased with intense emotional pain (for example sadness, guilt, anger, difficulty accepting the death, numbness). It lasts atypically long (more than 6 months at a minimum), clearly exceeds the person's cultural and religious norms, and impairs functioning. Longer grief that stays within a normative mourning period is normal bereavement and receives no diagnosis [WHO-ICD11-2022].

**Normal versus prolonged grief.** Acute grief, with intense emotion and preoccupation with the deceased, usually becomes integrated grief, more in the background, by around 6 to 12 months [Szuhany-etal-2021].

## Course and prevalence

- Trajectories vary. Stable low grief is the most common path (about 26% to 45%); many start high or moderate and decline without help; about 7% to 10% show stable high grief, the likely PGD group [Szuhany-etal-2021].
- Pooled prevalence after non-violent bereavement in adults: 9.8% (95% confidence interval 6.8 to 14.0) across 14 studies [Lundorff-etal-2017]. An analysis of 34 samples from 16 countries averaged 13% [Comtesse-etal-2024].
- After unnatural deaths (suicide, homicide, accident, disaster) pooled prevalence is 49%, higher after violent killing or the death of an only child and lower after natural disasters [Djelantik-etal-2020].
- Risk factors include characteristics of the death, older age, female sex, lower socioeconomic status, anxious attachment, prior depression, high grief before the death, emotional dependence on the deceased, trauma exposure and poor social support [APA-PGD-Site].
- In the US, Dutch and British DSM-5-TR validation samples, 4.4% to 15.3% met criteria 12 to 24 months after the loss, and the diagnosis was stable over about 7 months [Prigerson-etal-2021].

## Differential and comorbidity

- **Major depressive disorder:** sadness and withdrawal are generalized, with broad loss of pleasure. In PGD, pain centers on the lost person, with yearning and seeking closeness to them [Szuhany-etal-2021]. Both can coexist (`depression.overview`).
- **PTSD:** fear dominates and intrusions replay the event. In PGD, yearning and sadness dominate, and avoidance targets reminders of the loss rather than threat [Szuhany-etal-2021]. Violent deaths often produce both.
- **Normal or culturally expected mourning:** no diagnosis, however long, if it fits the person's norms [WHO-ICD11-2022].
- **Losses other than a death** (divorce, migration, health) do not qualify, because both systems require a death [Prigerson-etal-2021; WHO-ICD11-2022]; consider adjustment disorder.
- Comorbidity is the rule: 75% of 206 people with PGD had a current comorbid disorder [Szuhany-etal-2021].

## CBT approach in brief

Formulate with the cognitive behavioral model of prolonged grief (`grief-and-loss.cbt-model`), then offer grief-focused CBT or prolonged grief disorder therapy, both supported by randomized trials and meta-analysis [Komischke-Konnerup-etal-2024] (`grief-and-loss.treatment`). Do not offer routine treatment to everyone bereaved [Stroebe-etal-2007].

## Risk notes

- Complicated grief predicted high suicidality after controlling for depression and PTSD, with about 6.6 times the odds about 6 months after the loss and 8.2 times the odds of high suicidality at follow-up, in 309 bereaved adults [Latham-Prigerson-2004]. Complicated grief is the earlier research term for what is now PGD. Among treatment-seekers, 20% to over 50% report suicidal ideation [Szuhany-etal-2021].
- A wish to join the deceased is associated with PGD [Szuhany-etal-2021]. Ask whether it includes thoughts of dying or suicide, assess fully (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`) and build a safety plan that includes restricting access to lethal means.
- Bereavement also raises the risk of physical illness and death, especially early on [Stroebe-etal-2007]; check health and self-care.
- Immediate danger: do not leave the person alone; in the US call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911 [Lifeline-988]; in the United Kingdom (UK) call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123 [NHS-Urgent-Help; Samaritans-Site].
