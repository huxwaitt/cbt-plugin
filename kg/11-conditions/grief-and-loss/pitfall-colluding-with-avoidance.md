**Bottom line:** Warm, supportive listening is necessary but not enough for prolonged grief disorder (PGD). Anxious avoidance of the reality of the death maintains the disorder [Boelen-etal-2006], and treatments that include exposure outperform supportive counseling [Boelen-etal-2007]. If the therapist avoids the death story too, therapy repeats the client's avoidance and grief stays stuck.

## What it looks like

- Many sessions of reminiscing about good times, with the day of the death never discussed.
- The therapist changes the subject when the client cries about the death.
- Revisiting is planned but postponed every week because the client "isn't ready."
- Homework is limited to self-care; avoided places, objects and people stay off limits.
- Grief scale scores are unchanged after 12 or more sessions.

## Why it happens

- Therapists fear causing pain or harm to someone already suffering.
- The client's avoidance is persuasive: "I can't go there."
- Talking about the deceased feels like progress, and rumination about the loss can look like processing while working as avoidance [Eisma-etal-2013].
- Revisiting is demanding for clients. In a National Health Service (NHS) Talking Therapies pilot, clients described revisiting sessions and home practice as the hardest parts to engage with, but powerful [King-etal-2025].

## The fix

- If several sessions pass without the death being discussed → share the formulation again and agree a start date for revisiting. Because exposure alone outperformed cognitive restructuring alone, and starting with exposure gave better results than starting with restructuring [Boelen-etal-2007].
- If the client says they are not ready → explore the feared outcome ("If you let yourself tell it fully, what do you fear would happen?") and set revisiting up as a test of that prediction. Because catastrophic beliefs about grief reactions motivate avoidance [Boelen-etal-2006].
- If revisiting has been dropped from the plan → put it back as a core component. Because adding exposure to group cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) reduced the share of completers with PGD at 6 months from 37.9% to 14.8% [Bryant-etal-2014].
- If you are offering only supportive or interpersonal work → switch to a protocol with revisiting. Because complicated grief treatment, which includes revisiting, achieved 51% response versus 28% for interpersonal psychotherapy [Shear-etal-2005].
- If the client is overwhelmed in revisiting → stay with them, slow down and reflect, then process together after the exposure, rather than abandoning it; if distress brings up thoughts of dying, pause and assess risk (`grief-and-loss.pitfall-missing-suicide-risk-in-grief`). Because exposure to the death memory, not supportive counseling alone, drove the added benefit in trials [Bryant-etal-2014; Boelen-etal-2007].

Example phrasing: "We've spent good time remembering him. The part we've both been stepping around is the day he died. The research and your own experience suggest that is where the grief is stuck. Shall we plan how to approach it together?"

Avoid: "Let's leave that for when you feel stronger." It signals that the memory is dangerous and keeps avoidance going.

## Sources note

Comparative trials show the added value of exposure [Boelen-etal-2007; Bryant-etal-2014] and of a revisiting-based protocol over interpersonal psychotherapy [Shear-etal-2005]. The maintaining role of avoidance comes from the cognitive behavioral model [Boelen-etal-2006] and a longitudinal study of rumination [Eisma-etal-2013]. Evidence strength: supported by randomized trials.
