**Bottom line:** Exposure done while the client keeps a safety behavior teaches "I was safe because of the safety behavior", not "the catastrophe does not happen". Dropping safety behaviors during exposure reduced catastrophic beliefs and anxiety more than the same exposure with them [Salkovskis-etal-1999]. Ask about safety behaviors before, during and after every task.

## What it looks like

- The client walks to the supermarket each week, but only with a phone in hand, a water bottle, or a partner waiting outside.
- In-session anxiety falls, yet the belief "I would have fainted if I hadn't sat down" stays at 70.
- The client uses slow breathing, muscle tensing or distraction "to get through" each task.
- An as-needed benzodiazepine is carried "just in case" and never mentioned.
- Gains vanish in a new place or when the companion is absent.

## Why it happens

- Safety behaviors are often subtle and the client may not see them as relevant: holding a trolley, standing near the door, checking the pulse, carrying a lucky object.
- Therapists sometimes teach breathing or relaxation as a way to stop attacks, which turns a skill into a rescue. Muscle relaxation components were linked to lower efficacy in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) packages for panic [Pompoli-etal-2018].
- In Clark's model, the client credits the safety behavior with averting disaster, so the catastrophic belief is protected [Clark-1986].
- Both therapist and client want tasks to "succeed", and keeping the safety net feels kinder.

## The fix

1. **Inventory first.** "When you're in the shop, what do you do to stop the worst from happening? What do you carry? Who needs to be around?"
2. **Ask the key question** for each item: "If you didn't do that, what do you think would happen?" That reveals the prediction to test.
3. **Run paired experiments** where helpful: the same task with and then without the safety behavior, rating belief and anxiety each time [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
4. **Fade in steps:** phone in pocket, then in a bag, then left in the car; partner in the shop, then outside, then at home.
5. **Treat medication carefully.** Map an as-needed benzodiazepine in the formulation and plan any change with the prescriber. Evidence that benzodiazepines add to psychotherapy for panic is inadequate [Watanabe-etal-2009], and the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises against them for panic disorder [NICE-CG113].
6. **Remove safety signals and vary contexts** so the learning does not depend on them [Craske-etal-2014].

Example phrasing: "You did the walk, which took courage. You also mentioned holding the railing. What do you think would have happened if you'd let go? Shall we test that next?" Avoid: "Great, you're cured of that one."

## Sources note

The experimental evidence comes from one randomized experiment in panic disorder with agoraphobia [Salkovskis-etal-1999]. Whether some safety behaviors can be allowed early to make exposure acceptable is still debated. Gradual fading is a practical compromise (expert consensus), but the end point is exposure without safety signals [Craske-etal-2014].
