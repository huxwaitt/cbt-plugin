**Bottom line:** Interoceptive exposure means deliberately bringing on the body sensations a client fears, such as dizziness or a pounding heart, and staying with them without safety behaviors so the predicted catastrophe can be tested. It is the cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) component most consistently linked to better outcomes in panic disorder [Pompoli-etal-2018]. Choose exercises that reproduce the client's own attack sensations.

## When to use

After the client understands the panic cycle, usually from session 4 onward, and before or alongside situational exposure. Use it whenever sensations, not only places, are feared, including fear of exercise, heat, caffeine or feeling unreal.

## How it works

Panic attacks condition fear to internal cues [Bouton-etal-2001]. Producing those cues on purpose, in a controlled way, gives the client new learning: the sensations are uncomfortable, they pass, and the disaster does not happen. Expectancy violation drives this learning more than the fall in anxiety itself [Craske-etal-2014]. Doing it without safety behaviors matters, because safety behaviors preserve the catastrophic belief [Salkovskis-etal-1999].

## Steps

1. **Check health first.** Ask about heart, lung and seizure conditions, pregnancy, and recent injuries. If any are present, get medical advice on which exercises are safe and adapt them. A medical work-up should precede treating symptoms as panic [Tunnell-etal-2024]. Agree a stop rule: a symptom unlike the client's usual attacks, such as chest pain that does not settle with rest, ends the exercise and goes to a doctor. (Exercise selection and stop rules are expert consensus.)
2. **Run a test battery in session.** Demonstrate each exercise first, then do it together. Common exercises: overbreathing, spinning in a chair, shaking the head, breathing through a narrow straw, holding the breath, running in place or step-ups, head between the knees then standing quickly, tensing the body, staring at a light or mirror for unreality [Antony-etal-2006; Lee-etal-2006].
3. **Rate each exercise** 0 to 100 for intensity, similarity to real attacks, and fear. Keep the ones with real similarity; one trial kept exercises rated at least 30 of 100 for similarity [Lee-etal-2006]. Spinning, overbreathing, straw breathing and the tongue depressor produced the most fear in one study [Antony-etal-2006].
4. **Write the prediction** before each trial: "If I spin for a minute, I will collapse." Belief 0 to 100.
5. **Do it fully.** Continue past the first sensations, then keep going a little longer. Then sit with the sensations without breathing control, sitting down, or checking the pulse.
6. **Review.** "What did you predict? What happened? What does that say about the sensations?" Re-rate the belief.
7. **Assign home practice**: at least one exercise each day, recording fear each time [Lee-etal-2006], working through the client's ranked list.
8. **Make it naturalistic:** coffee, saunas, exercise classes, climbing stairs, horror films, then sensations inside avoided situations.

## Guidance

- If the client stops the exercise early → validate, then ask what they feared would happen if they continued, and repeat with that prediction written down. Because stopping early leaves the expectancy untested [Craske-etal-2014].
- If fear stays at zero → pick a more similar exercise, extend it, or remove a subtle safety behavior. Because tasks that do not reproduce the feared sensations give little new learning [Lee-etal-2006].
- If the client wants the therapist to say it is safe → say what you checked, then let the experiment answer. "We have checked with your doctor. Let's see what your body does when you spin." Avoid: "Don't worry, nothing bad will happen." Because seeing the prediction fail drives the learning [Craske-etal-2014].
- If cardiorespiratory sensations are the main fear → add vigorous exercise. Because no standard task reproduced cardiorespiratory sensations well in one study [Lee-etal-2006], and in a trial with 72 sedentary adults, 12 weeks of walking with brief sprints reduced panic severity more than relaxation [Muotri-etal-2025]. Check fitness for vigorous exercise first.

## Common problems

- Treating the exercises as a coping drill to "get used to it" instead of a test of a prediction.
- Doing them only in the clinic, so learning does not generalize. Vary place and time [Craske-etal-2014].
- Hidden safety behaviors, such as stopping to drink water or staying near the therapist.

## Evidence

In a component network meta-analysis of 72 randomized trials (4,064 participants), interoceptive exposure was associated with better efficacy and acceptability [Pompoli-etal-2018]. People with panic disorder fear the symptom induction exercises more than controls [Antony-etal-2006]. In one 10-session group CBT study, fear of neurological-type sensations fell when hyperventilation, breath holding, spinning or chest breathing worked [Lee-etal-2006]. Evidence strength: supported by randomized trials as a component of CBT.
