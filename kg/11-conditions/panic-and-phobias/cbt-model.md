**Bottom line:** In Clark's cognitive model, panic attacks come from catastrophically misinterpreting normal bodily sensations as signs of imminent disaster, which raises anxiety and the sensations further in a fast vicious circle. Body scanning, safety behaviors and avoidance keep the misinterpretation alive by blocking disconfirmation. Phobias follow the same logic: avoidance and safety behaviors stop the person from learning that the feared outcome does not happen or can be tolerated.

## Components

**Clark's vicious circle of panic** [Clark-1986; Pompoli-etal-2016]:
1. **Trigger:** external (a crowded shop) or internal (a skipped heartbeat, a thought, a feeling of unreality).
2. **Perceived threat** and **apprehension**.
3. **Body sensations** of anxiety: racing heart, breathlessness, dizziness.
4. **Catastrophic misinterpretation:** "I'm having a heart attack", "I'll faint", "I'm going mad".
5. The interpretation raises threat and anxiety, which raises the sensations, closing the loop.

**Maintaining processes:**
- **Selective attention to the body:** scanning for sensations makes normal fluctuations noticeable and triggers attacks.
- **Safety behaviors:** sitting down, holding on, slow breathing, carrying water or pills. The client credits them with preventing the catastrophe. Dropping them during exposure reduced catastrophic beliefs and anxiety more than exposure with them [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- **Avoidance** of situations and of activities that produce sensations (exercise, caffeine, heat).

**Learning theory account.** Panic attacks condition anxiety to internal and external cues (interoceptive conditioning), and conditioned anticipatory anxiety makes further panic more likely [Bouton-etal-2001]. **Anxiety sensitivity**, the fear of arousal sensations, measured by the Anxiety Sensitivity Index-3 (ASI-3), is a vulnerability factor with physical, cognitive and social facets [Taylor-etal-2007].

**Phobias.** The person expects a feared outcome (bitten, trapped, falling, fainting, panicking). Avoidance and safety signals prevent the learning that would violate that expectancy [Craske-etal-2014]. In blood-injection-injury phobia, an excessive vasovagal response can cause real fainting [Barnhill-2026c], so the model includes a physiological target, not only a belief [Ayala-etal-2009].

## How it keeps the problem going

| Process | What the client concludes |
|---|---|
| Body scanning | "My body is unpredictable and dangerous." |
| Safety behavior | "I only survived because I sat down." The belief stays intact. |
| Escape | Relief teaches that leaving was necessary. |
| Avoidance | No new evidence; the fear generalizes to more places. |
| Anticipatory anxiety | Arousal before entering raises the chance of an attack. |

Cultural meanings can add catastrophic content, for example the fear in khyâl attacks that wind and blood will burst neck vessels [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2010]. Put the client's own meaning in the interpretation box.

## Drawing it with the client

1. Pick one recent, vivid attack.
2. Ask for the first thing noticed, then "What went through your mind?" Write the thought in their words with a belief rating at the time.
3. Ask "When you thought that, what happened to your heart and breathing?" Draw the arrow back to sensations.
4. Ask "What did you do to stop it happening?" Add safety behaviors, then: "If that had not been there, what would have happened?" This exposes the belief the behavior protects.
5. Offer the alternative explanation as a hypothesis to test: "Could this be anxiety feeding on itself, rather than your heart?" Rate belief in each explanation.
6. Run a brief socializing experiment, such as overbreathing or focusing on the heartbeat for a short time, to show that thoughts and attention change sensations.

Example wording: "Your racing heart led to the thought 'heart attack', which made you more frightened, which made your heart race more. Shall we see whether that circle fits other attacks?"

## Treatment targets it implies

- **Belief in catastrophic interpretations:** rate it every session. In brief cognitive therapy, end-of-treatment cognitive scores predicted panic at 12-month follow-up [Clark-etal-1999].
- **Safety behaviors:** identify and drop them in behavioral experiments [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- **Fear of sensations:** interoceptive exposure; see `panic-and-phobias.interoceptive-exposure`.
- **Avoidance:** graded or intensive in vivo exposure designed to violate expectancies [Craske-etal-2014].
- **Attention:** shift attention outward and test what body focus does.
- **Fainting physiology in blood-injection-injury phobia:** applied tension; see `panic-and-phobias.specific-phobia`.
