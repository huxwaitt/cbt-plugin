**Bottom line:** One-session treatment (OST) is a functional assessment followed by a single exposure session of up to 3 hours that combines graduated in vivo exposure, therapist modeling, testing of catastrophic beliefs and reinforcement [Zlomke-Davis-2008]. In children it was as effective as multi-session cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and cheaper [Wright-etal-2023]. Use it when the feared stimulus can be presented safely and the client agrees to an intensive session.

## When to use

- Specific phobias of animals, injections, heights, enclosed spaces, flying-related cues, vomiting and similar, in adults and in children aged about 7 to 17 [Ost-1989; Ollendick-etal-2009; Wright-etal-2023].
- Not when exposure is not the right first treatment, for example when a more severe problem takes priority, or when the stimulus cannot be presented or simulated safely, such as stinging insects [Wright-etal-2023].
- Comorbid anxiety does not rule it out; in one study of youth it worked equally well with comorbid generalized, social or other phobic anxiety, and the comorbid disorders improved too [Ryan-etal-2017].

## How it works

Massed exposure gives many disconfirming trials in a short time. The client sees the therapist model approach, then tests a specific prediction ("the dog will bite", "I will faint") step by step. The session continues until fear has fallen and the prediction has been tested, so the client leaves on a success rather than an escape [Zlomke-Davis-2008]. Testing the specific feared outcome and removing safety signals strengthen learning [Craske-etal-2014].

## Steps

1. **Functional assessment, about 1 hour** [Wright-etal-2023]. Build rapport, identify the feared outcome and its belief rating, list avoidance and safety behaviors, build a hierarchy, and explain the rationale. Agree that nothing happens without the client's consent and that the session aims to finish each step started (expert consensus).
2. **Prepare.** Arrange stimuli for every hierarchy step (pictures, video, a contained animal, needles and a nurse, a tall building). For blood-injection-injury phobia, teach applied tension first.
3. **Exposure session, up to 3 hours.** Start low. At each step: name the prediction, model it, invite the client to approach, praise each move, and stay until anxiety has dropped by at least 50% before moving up [Wright-etal-2023].
4. **Challenge the belief in action.** "You thought the spider would run up your arm. What did it do?" Re-rate belief.
5. **Go beyond everyday demands.** End at or above the top of the hierarchy, such as holding the spider, so ordinary encounters feel easy.
6. **Maintenance.** Agree self-directed exposure in daily life. Standard youth OST is followed by four weekly phone calls [Ollendick-etal-2021].

## Guidance

- If the client freezes at a step → drop to a smaller step (more distance, a glove, the therapist holding the stimulus), then return. Because graduated exposure within the session is the core of OST [Davis-etal-2019].
- If a parent usually protects the child from the feared object → invite the parent to watch and coach afterward. Because one trial found exposure with a parent present roughly equal to exposure alone [Ost-etal-2001], and parent support helps practice continue.
- If the client is autistic → add visual aids, more parent involvement, and four 1-hour booster sessions in place of the phone calls. Because an adapted OST with these changes cut fear ratings and phobia severity in a small preliminary study, with only modest change in behavioral avoidance [Ollendick-etal-2021].
- If the phobia is of needles or blood in a child → add pain education, smaller steps, disgust-focused tasks and fainting management. Because these were the adaptations proposed for pediatric blood-injection-injury phobia [Oar-etal-2015].
- If the session ends early with high fear → book a second session soon rather than stopping. Because one optional extra session was allowed in the children's trial [Wright-etal-2023], and leaving at high fear may strengthen avoidance.

Example phrasing: "We'll go one step at a time, and you decide when to take each one. What do you think will happen if you touch the jar?"

## Common problems

- Running out of stimuli midway. Prepare every step in advance.
- Ending the session at the first sign of relief, before the prediction is tested.
- No plan for practice afterward, so gains fade.

## Evidence

- Original series: mean 2.1 hours; 90% much improved or recovered at about 4 years [Ost-1989].
- Youth: in 196 youth aged 7 to 16, OST beat education support on clinician-rated severity and diagnosis-free rates, though not on self-report measures [Ollendick-etal-2009]. In 268 children aged 7 to 16 across UK services, OST was non-inferior to multi-session CBT (about 6 hours over 6 sessions) on a behavioral avoidance task at 6 months and cost about £303 less per child; only 149 children contributed primary outcome data, partly because of COVID-19 [Wright-etal-2023].
- Brief, intensive treatments for youth anxiety matched standard CBT (effect size 0.01 versus CBT) [Ost-Ollendick-2017].
- Disagreement: an adult meta-analysis found multi-session treatment marginally better on phobia questionnaires and more sessions predicting better outcomes [Wolitzky-Taylor-etal-2008]. Offer more sessions if one is not enough.
- Evidence strength: supported by randomized trials; independent replication beyond the developers' groups is still called for [Zlomke-Davis-2008].
