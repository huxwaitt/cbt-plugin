**Bottom line:** Specific phobia is marked, out-of-proportion fear of one kind of object or situation, avoided or endured, typically for 6 months or more. It is common, usually starts in childhood, and is rarely treated [Eaton-etal-2018]. Exposure, especially in vivo, is the treatment of choice [Wolitzky-Taylor-etal-2008], often in a single session of up to 3 hours [Ost-1989; Wright-etal-2023], with applied tension added when blood or needles cause fainting [Ost-etal-1991].

## Diagnostic essentials

Paraphrased from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) [APA-DSM5TR-2022; Barnhill-2026c]: the object or situation almost always provokes immediate fear; it is actively avoided or endured with intense fear; the fear is out of proportion to the actual danger and sociocultural context; it typically lasts 6 months or more; and it causes distress or impairment not better explained by another disorder. In children, fear may show as crying, tantrums, freezing or clinging [APA-DSM5TR-2022].

| Specifier | Examples |
|---|---|
| Animal | Spiders, dogs, insects |
| Natural environment | Heights, storms, water |
| Blood-injection-injury | Needles, blood draws, injuries, medical procedures |
| Situational | Flying, elevators, tunnels, bridges, enclosed spaces, driving |
| Other | Choking, vomiting, loud sounds, costumed characters |

Code each phobia that is present. The International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) frames specific phobia by its narrow focus of apprehension, without fixed duration counts [Reed-etal-2019].

## Course and prevalence

- Lifetime prevalence has a median of 7.2% across 25 adult surveys (range about 3% to 15%); it is higher in women; median onset is 8 years in the World Mental Health surveys [Eaton-etal-2018].
- 12-month prevalence is about 8% in women and 3% in men; about 75% of people with one phobia fear another object or situation [Barnhill-2026c].
- Persistence over years or decades occurs in 10% to 30% of cases; phobias predict later anxiety, mood and substance use disorders; only one tenth to one quarter of sufferers are treated [Eaton-etal-2018].
- In one US undergraduate self-report study, African American students endorsed more animal fears and White students more blood-injection-injury fears [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2010]; clinical samples may differ.

## Differential and comorbidity

- **Panic disorder:** attacks are unexpected and occur across situations, not only at the feared object.
- **Agoraphobia:** fear spans 2 or more situation groups and centers on escape or help.
- **Social anxiety disorder:** fear of being judged, for example vomiting in public because others would see.
- **Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD):** fear tied to obsessions, such as contamination, with neutralizing rituals.
- **Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD):** fear of reminders after a trauma, such as driving after a crash, with other PTSD symptoms.
- **Eating disorders:** food avoidance driven by weight or shape concerns rather than fear of choking or vomiting.
- Comorbidity is common, especially other anxiety, depressive and bipolar, substance-related, and somatic symptom disorders [APA-DSM5TR-2022; Barnhill-2026c].

## CBT approach in brief

- **Exposure first.** In 33 randomized trials, exposure produced large effects versus no treatment and beat placebo and other psychotherapies; in vivo exposure beat imaginal or virtual reality exposure at post-treatment, though not at follow-up [Wolitzky-Taylor-etal-2008].
- **Dose.** More sessions predicted better outcomes in that meta-analysis, and multi-session treatment slightly beat single sessions on phobia questionnaires [Wolitzky-Taylor-etal-2008]. Yet one massed session of exposure and modeling averaging 2.1 hours left 90% much improved or recovered at about 4 years [Ost-1989], and in children it matched multi-session cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [Wright-etal-2023]. See `panic-and-phobias.one-session-treatment`.
- **Design for learning.** Test the specific feared outcome, vary stimuli and settings, and drop safety signals [Craske-etal-2014].
- **Blood-injection-injury phobia.** An excessive vasovagal reflex can cause a drop in heart rate and fainting [Barnhill-2026c]. Teach applied tension: tense the large muscles of the arms, legs and torso until warmth rises in the face, release without fully relaxing, repeat in cycles, practice daily, then use it during graded exposure to pictures, blood and needles. In 30 patients given five sessions, 90% were clinically improved after applied tension, 80% after tension only and 40% after exposure alone [Ost-etal-1991]. A later review found all five controlled trials came from that one group with small samples, and exposure alone did as well or better on phobia questionnaires, so the added value of tension is uncertain [Ayala-etal-2009]. Evidence strength: randomized trials from one group; still widely recommended for clients who faint.
- **Medication** has a limited role: a prescriber may give a short-term benzodiazepine or beta-blocker when an unavoidable exposure, such as a flight, cannot wait for therapy [Barnhill-2026c]. Prefer exposure, because medication taken during exposure can act as a safety signal [Craske-etal-2014].

Example phrasing: "What exactly do you think the spider will do? How sure are you, 0 to 100? Let's find out together, one step at a time."

## Risk notes

- Blood-injection-injury phobia can lead to refusal of needed vaccinations, blood tests, dental care or surgery [Barnhill-2026c]. Ask about overdue medical care and coordinate with the medical team.
- Plan fainting safety in exposure: client seated or lying, applied tension learned first, and stop rules agreed.
- Do not use real stimuli that are genuinely dangerous; use simulation or imagery instead.
- Screen for depression and suicidal thoughts when a phobia sits within wider anxiety or mood problems, using `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`.
