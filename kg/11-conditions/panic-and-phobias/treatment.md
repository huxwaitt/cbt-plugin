**Bottom line:** Panic-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the best-supported psychological treatment for panic disorder: about 7 to 14 hours over up to 4 months, built on psychoeducation, cognitive work on catastrophic misinterpretations, interoceptive exposure, and in vivo exposure without safety behaviors [NICE-CG113; Clark-1986]. Interoceptive exposure and face-to-face delivery are the components most linked to good outcomes, while muscle relaxation is linked to worse ones [Pompoli-etal-2018].

## When to use

First-line psychological treatment for moderate to severe panic disorder with or without agoraphobia in adults, according to the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [NICE-CG113]. NICE reserves an antidepressant for long-standing disorder or when therapy has not helped or is declined, and offers facilitated or unfacilitated self-help first for mild to moderate panic disorder [NICE-CG113]. Medication choices belong to the prescriber; CBT can continue alongside them.

## How it works

Treatment tests the catastrophic interpretations in Clark's model by producing the feared sensations and situations while the client drops safety behaviors, so the predicted disaster fails to happen [Clark-1986; Salkovskis-etal-1999]. In learning terms, exposure works best when it violates expectancies, removes safety signals, and varies contexts [Craske-etal-2014].

## Steps

An illustrative outline of 10 to 12 weekly sessions (expert consensus, adapted from the protocols cited). Clark's full cognitive therapy runs 12 to 15 one-hour sessions, and a self-study version needed 6.5 therapist hours [Clark-etal-1999]. NICE specifies weekly sessions of 1 to 2 hours [NICE-CG113]; longer sessions leave room for exposure done in session.

| Session | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | Assessment review, baseline measures, map a recent attack into the vicious circle; start a panic diary |
| 2 | Psychoeducation on the fight-or-flight response; socializing experiment (overbreathing or body focus) to show thoughts and attention change sensations |
| 3 to 4 | Verbal reattribution: evidence for and against "heart attack", "faint", "go mad"; rate belief in each explanation |
| 4 to 6 | Interoceptive exposure: provoke sensations in session, then daily at home, without safety behaviors |
| 5 to 10 | Behavioral experiments and in vivo exposure to avoided situations, dropping safety behaviors one by one; therapist-guided where possible |
| 8 to 10 | Combine: provoke sensations inside feared situations (coffee before the supermarket) |
| 11 to 12 | Residual beliefs, relapse blueprint, plan for setbacks and booster contact |

Each experiment: state the prediction and belief rating, decide what would count as the catastrophe, do the task without the safety behavior, then compare outcome with prediction and re-rate.

## Guidance

- If the client asks to learn breathing to stop attacks → teach it only as information about overbreathing, not as a rescue technique, and phase it out. Because breathing retraining improves acceptability but adds little efficacy, while muscle relaxation is linked to worse outcomes [Pompoli-etal-2018], and any rescue strategy can become a safety behavior [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- If the client finishes an exposure task saying "I was fine because my wife was nearby" → plan the next trial without the companion. Because safety behaviors block disconfirmation [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- If panic attacks stop but avoidance continues → shift time to graded in vivo exposure, with the therapist present for early steps. Because therapist-guided exposure produced greater gains in agoraphobic avoidance than prescribed exposure alone [Gloster-etal-2011].
- If the client is already taking an antidepressant → continue CBT and coordinate with the prescriber. Because combined treatment beat either alone in the short term, and after treatment ended it matched psychotherapy alone [Furukawa-etal-2007].
- If the client cannot attend weekly → offer guided internet CBT. Because across 64 trials in depression, panic, social anxiety and generalized anxiety, internet CBT had a mean effect size g = 0.80, and in 9 head-to-head trials it appeared as beneficial as face-to-face CBT [Andrews-etal-2018].

## Common problems

- Too much talk, too little exposure: cognitive discussion without experiments.
- Exposure stopped when anxiety peaks, teaching that escape was needed. See `panic-and-phobias.pitfall-exposure-stopped-before-the-test`.
- Hidden safety behaviors. See `panic-and-phobias.pitfall-hidden-safety-behaviors`.
- Ending without a relapse plan. See `09-ending.relapse-prevention-blueprint`.

## Evidence

- **Guidelines.** NICE (CG113, last updated 2020 and still current in 2026) recommends CBT of 7 to 14 hours in total, as weekly 1 to 2 hour sessions completed within 4 months; when medication is used, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) licensed for panic is offered first, and benzodiazepines should not be prescribed [NICE-CG113]. In the US, the American Psychiatric Association lists its 2009 panic disorder guideline as a legacy guideline [APA-Psychiatry-Guidelines-Site], so there is no current APA guideline.
- **Network meta-analysis.** Across 54 trials (3,021 patients), CBT had the most consistent advantage for short-term remission and response, though overall evidence quality was low and small-study effects inflated waitlist comparisons [Pompoli-etal-2016].
- **Components.** In 72 trials (4,064 participants), interoceptive exposure and face-to-face delivery improved efficacy and acceptability; muscle relaxation and virtual reality exposure lowered efficacy. The best and worst CBT packages differed in remission odds by a ratio of 7.69 [Pompoli-etal-2018].
- **Against medication.** In a 312-patient trial, CBT and imipramine each beat placebo acutely (response 48.7% and 45.8% versus 21.7%), and CBT gains were more durable after treatment stopped [Barlow-etal-2000].
- Evidence strength: guideline-recommended and supported by randomized trials.
