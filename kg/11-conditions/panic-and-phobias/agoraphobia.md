**Bottom line:** Agoraphobia is fear and avoidance of several kinds of everyday situations because escape might be hard or help unavailable if panic-like or embarrassing symptoms strike. Since 2013 it has been diagnosed on its own, with or without panic disorder [APA-DSM5TR-2022]. It carries more comorbidity and impairment than panic alone [Kessler-etal-2006], and the treatment is in vivo exposure, ideally therapist-guided at first, with companions and other safety behaviors faded [Gloster-etal-2011].

## Diagnostic essentials

Paraphrased from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) [APA-DSM5TR-2022; Barnhill-2026b]:
- Marked fear about 2 or more of 5 groups: public transport, open spaces (parking lots, markets), enclosed places (shops, theaters), standing in line or in a crowd, and being outside the home alone.
- The fear is about escape being difficult or help unavailable if panic-like symptoms or other incapacitating or embarrassing symptoms occur, such as falling or incontinence in older adults.
- The situations almost always provoke fear and are avoided, need a companion, or are endured with intense fear; the fear is out of proportion to real danger.
- Typically lasting 6 months or more, with distress or impairment, and not better explained by another disorder.
- If panic disorder criteria are also met, both are diagnosed.

The separation from panic disorder followed evidence that agoraphobia occurs without panic and that avoidance shapes course and outcome [Wittchen-etal-2010]. The International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) defines it as fear in or anticipating multiple situations, focused on specific incapacitating or embarrassing outcomes, without a fixed situation count [Reed-etal-2019].

## Course and prevalence

- About 1% to 2% of people have agoraphobia in a given year; it is more common in women and usually begins in adolescence or young adulthood, though it can start late in life around safety and physical limits [Barnhill-2026b].
- 30% to 50% also have panic disorder. Severity waxes and wanes, from mild to housebound, and some people improve through their own informal exposure [Barnhill-2026b].
- In the National Comorbidity Survey Replication (NCS-R), 84.7% of people with panic disorder plus agoraphobia had severe role impairment, versus 56.2% with panic disorder alone. Lifetime agoraphobia with panic attacks but never panic disorder (0.8%) was nearly as common as panic disorder with agoraphobia (1.1%) [Kessler-etal-2006].

## Differential and comorbidity

| Ask | Points toward |
|---|---|
| "What would happen if you couldn't get out?" Collapse, panic, losing control of the bowels | Agoraphobia |
| Being judged or embarrassed by people watching | Social anxiety disorder |
| Only one kind of situation (only flying, only elevators) | Specific phobia, situational type |
| Staying in because of low energy or no interest | Depression |
| Avoiding reminders of a trauma | Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) |
| Realistic danger, or travel limits set by family or cultural norms | Not a disorder [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2010] |

Every agoraphobia subgroup in the NCS-R had at least one other lifetime disorder [Kessler-etal-2006]. Measure avoidance with the Mobility Inventory: in people with panic disorder, an avoidance-alone score of 1.61 identified interview-diagnosed agoraphobia with sensitivity .87 and specificity .73 [Chambless-etal-2011].

## CBT approach in brief

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) built on exposure is the most effective approach on the most robust evidence [Barnhill-2026b] and first-line for panic disorder with agoraphobia [Gloster-etal-2011].
- **Hierarchy.** Build it from the Mobility Inventory and the client's goals: distance from home, time, crowding, alone versus accompanied.
- **Therapist-guided exposure.** In a 369-patient trial, therapist-guided exposure in situ beat exposure that was only prescribed, on clinical global impression and the Mobility Inventory, and more exposure time produced less avoidance [Gloster-etal-2011].
- **Fade companions and safety items** (phone in hand, water, pills, sitting near exits). Dropping safety behaviors during exposure reduced catastrophic beliefs more than exposure with them [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- **Vary contexts** and practice between sessions so learning generalizes [Craske-etal-2014].
- **Add interoceptive exposure** if bodily sensations are feared [Pompoli-etal-2018].
- **Housebound clients:** start by telehealth or a home visit, then do the first steps together from the doorway outward.

Example phrasing: "Next time, could you walk to the corner with your phone in your pocket instead of in your hand? What do you predict will happen?"

## Risk notes

- Panic disorder is linked to suicide attempts, and comorbid personality disorder raises that link sharply [Nepon-etal-2010]. Housebound clients may also be isolated, so ask about mood, suicidal thoughts and who they can contact, and follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment` if risk is present.
- Check whether family members are drawn into accommodation (shopping, driving, staying home), and plan its reduction with consent.
- In older adults, assess real fall risk before setting exposure tasks.
