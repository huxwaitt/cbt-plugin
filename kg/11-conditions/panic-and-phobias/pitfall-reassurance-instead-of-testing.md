**Bottom line:** Reassurance ("your heart is fine") brings quick relief, then the doubt returns and the client needs more, so it works like a safety behavior. Education and reassurance alone rarely settle panic-related health fears [Tunnell-etal-2024]. Do one appropriate medical check, then let behavioral experiments, not the therapist's word, answer the question.

## What it looks like

- Each session starts with "Are you sure it's just anxiety?" and the therapist explains again.
- The client has had several emergency department visits, heart tracings and blood tests, each normal, each followed by renewed doubt.
- The therapist says "Nothing bad will happen" before an exposure task.
- Or the opposite error: new or different symptoms are waved away as "just panic" without assessment.

## Why it happens

- Reassuring feels kind and ends the client's distress in the moment.
- The client's catastrophic interpretation, for example "this time it really is a heart attack", is not tested, so it survives every reassurance [Clark-1986].
- Panic sends many people to emergency care: up to 12% of emergency department patients, and 20% to 35% of those with chest pain, meet panic disorder criteria [Tunnell-etal-2024], so medical checking can become a cycle.

## The fix

1. **Check once, properly.** Make sure medical causes have been considered, including onset after 45 or atypical symptoms such as vertigo or loss of consciousness [APA-DSM5TR-2022]. Record what was done.
2. **Name the pattern.** "Each time you're told it's fine, how long does the relief last? What happens to the doubt after that?"
3. **Put reassurance into the formulation** as a safety behavior that blocks disconfirmation [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
4. **Replace it with a test.** "If your heart were weak, what would happen if you ran up the stairs? Shall we find out together?" Use interoceptive exposure to produce the sensations on purpose [Antony-etal-2006].
5. **Agree a rule for new symptoms:** a genuinely new or different symptom goes to the doctor; the familiar pattern is tested, not checked.
6. **Reduce checking gradually**, including pulse checks, internet searches and asking family, with the client's agreement.

Example phrasing: "I can't give you certainty, and I've noticed my reassurance doesn't last for you. What we can do is test what your body actually does when your heart races." Avoid: "Trust me, you're not dying."

## Sources note

The cost of reassurance-seeking is best studied in health anxiety; in panic, the logic follows the safety behavior research [Salkovskis-etal-1999] and the cognitive model [Clark-1986]. The need for a careful medical work-up alongside psychological treatment is emphasized in reviews of panic and medical illness [Tunnell-etal-2024].
