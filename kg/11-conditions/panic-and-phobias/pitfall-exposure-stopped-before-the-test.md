**Bottom line:** Exposure works when the client's specific feared outcome is tested and fails to happen, not merely when anxiety goes up and down [Craske-etal-2014]. Stopping a task at peak fear teaches that escape was necessary, and tasks that are too easy or too short never reach the prediction. Write the prediction, design the task to test it, and finish only when the test is done.

## What it looks like

- The client leaves the bus at the first strong sensation, and the session moves on.
- Weeks are spent on the lowest steps; the hierarchy top is never planned.
- Tasks end as soon as anxiety drops a little, before the feared outcome ("I'll faint if I stay 20 minutes") could have occurred.
- Exposure happens only in the clinic or only with the therapist, and the client cannot do it alone.
- The therapist stops a task because the client looks distressed, without asking what they predict.

## Why it happens

- Therapist discomfort with client distress, and worry about harming the alliance.
- No written prediction, so neither person knows what the task is meant to test.
- An older habituation rule ("wait until anxiety halves") used as the only goal, rather than testing beliefs. Anxiety reduction is not a reliable marker of learning [Craske-etal-2014].
- Under-dosing: exposure time and frequency relate to how much agoraphobic avoidance falls [Gloster-etal-2011], and more sessions predicted better phobia outcomes [Wolitzky-Taylor-etal-2008].

## The fix

1. **Before each task**, write the specific feared outcome and belief rating: "If I stay in the queue until I reach the till, I'll pass out. 80%."
2. **Design the task to test it:** long enough, close enough, and without escape, so the outcome could happen if the belief were true.
3. **Agree a no-escape plan in advance:** "If the feeling gets strong, what will you do instead of leaving?" (stay, notice, describe the sensations).
4. **Stay with distress, warmly.** "This is the feeling we expected. Stay with it. What is your body doing right now?"
5. **Debrief** against the prediction: "What did you think would happen? What happened? What does that tell you?" Re-rate belief [Clark-1986].
6. **Climb and vary:** move up promptly, mix steps, change places and times, and include practice alone [Craske-etal-2014].
7. **If a task is abandoned**, treat it as data: identify the prediction that won, then repeat a smaller version soon rather than retreating down the hierarchy.

Example phrasing: "You wanted to get off at the second stop. What did you think would happen if you stayed on to the fourth? Let's ride to the fourth next time and find out." Avoid: "You looked too anxious, so we'll leave buses for now."

## Sources note

The inhibitory learning view [Craske-etal-2014] and the cognitive model [Clark-1986] differ in mechanism but agree on the fix: test the feared outcome without escape. Habituation-based protocols such as one-session treatment still use "stay until fear drops by at least 50%" [Wright-etal-2023]; that rule works as a floor, not as the goal.
