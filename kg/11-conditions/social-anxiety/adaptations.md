**Bottom line:** Keep the core of cognitive therapy for social anxiety disorder (CT-SAD) and adapt around it: sequence treatment when depression is present, do not let drinking delay treatment, involve parents for young people, read fears against cultural norms, and broaden the formulation for autistic clients [NICE-CG159] [Hofmann-etal-2010] [Lei-etal-2024]. Medication is the client's informed choice.

## The principle

Adaptations change delivery, sequence or focus, not the mechanism: the client still tests feared predictions while externally focused and without safety behaviors. Only licensed prescribers prescribe; therapists inform, monitor and coordinate.

## Guidance

**Comorbidity and severity**
- If social anxiety began only after a depressive episode → treat the depression first. If it came first → ask whether the client would still be depressed without it: if not, treat the social anxiety unless depression is too severe; if so, consider treating both, by preference. Because the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) sets this rule [NICE-CG159].
- If the client drinks or uses drugs to cope socially → keep social anxiety treatment going; offer a brief intervention for hazardous use and consider specialist services for harmful or dependent use. Because use often serves to manage the anxiety [NICE-CG159], and the two disorders commonly co-occur [Morris-etal-2005].
- If the client also has avoidant personality disorder or beliefs such as "I'm unlikable" → still offer CT-SAD, target unconditional beliefs from early experiments onward, and add memory work, a positive data log and compassionate responses to the self-critic if beliefs persist. Because CT-SAD addresses these beliefs this way [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020], and in one trial 82% of reassessed patients with avoidant personality disorder no longer met its criteria at 1 year [Clark-etal-2023].
- If the client is too anxious to attend in person → begin by video call, not telephone. Because remote delivery removes travel and clinic barriers [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020], and NICE suggests remote first contact when attending is hard [NICE-CG159].
- If suicidal thoughts are disclosed → assess risk that day, build a safety plan (`02-risk.safety-planning`), and use emergency services if risk is imminent (`social-anxiety.overview`). Because social anxiety is associated with suicidal thoughts and behavior in young people, not wholly explained by depression [Leigh-etal-2023].

**Age**
- If the client is a child → offer individual (8 to 12 sessions of 45 minutes) or group (8 to 12 sessions of 90 minutes) cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for social anxiety, with parent psychoeducation and skills training, especially for young children. Because NICE recommends this [NICE-CG159].
- If the client is 15 or older and mature enough → consider the adult protocol. Because NICE allows this [NICE-CG159]; internet CT-SAD for ages 14 to 18 removed the diagnosis in 77% versus 14% on a waitlist [Leigh-Clark-2023].
- If an adolescent is bullied, overprotected or anxious online → include peer victimization, parenting and social media in the formulation. Because these may maintain adolescent social anxiety [Leigh-Clark-2018].

**Culture and neurodivergence**
- If the fear is of offending or embarrassing others (by gaze, blushing or body odor) → formulate it as social anxiety in the client's own terms. Because fear of offending others is within the criteria and fits the culture-linked taijin kyofusho pattern [Hofmann-etal-2010] [Katzman-etal-2014].
- If norms of eye contact, assertiveness or deference differ from yours → ask what the client's community expects before judging fear as excessive. Because social anxiety must be judged against cultural context [Hofmann-etal-2010].
- If the client is autistic → separate fear of evaluation from social communication differences and sensory load, set goals the client values, and if social images are seen through their own eyes, explore their bodily and sensory content before video feedback. Because Clark and Wells processes appear alongside autism-specific factors [Lei-etal-2024], and many autistic adults report such field-perspective images [Lei-etal-2026].

**Medication**
- If the adult prefers medication → refer to a prescriber. Because NICE's first-line drugs are the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) escitalopram or sertraline [NICE-CG159].
- If response to CBT is partial → discuss adding an SSRI. If response to an SSRI is partial after 10 to 12 weeks → add individual CBT. Because NICE recommends these combinations [NICE-CG159].
- If a client under 30 starts an SSRI → expect a warning about suicidal thinking, review within 1 week and weekly risk checks for the first month. Because NICE requires this for a small increased risk at this age [NICE-CG159].
- If a client takes an as-needed benzodiazepine or beta-blocker before events → treat it as a possible safety behavior and plan, with the prescriber, how to test fears without it. Because NICE advises against routine benzodiazepines [NICE-CG159], and beta-blocker trials in social anxiety disorder were negative, though they are used for performance situations [Katzman-etal-2014].
- If a child or adolescent is referred for medication → raise the evidence and offer CBT. Because NICE advises against routine medication under 18 [NICE-CG159].

**Step up or refer when** there is active suicide risk, depression too severe to engage, substance dependence, psychosis, or no reliable change on the weekly measure by mid-treatment despite competent, supervised delivery.

## Example phrasings

- "In your family, how much eye contact with an older person is respectful? Let's set goals that fit that."
- To a parent: "Your support helps most when it encourages her to try, rather than speaking for her."
- Avoid: "Everyone gets nervous, you just need to push through."

## Evidence

Most rules here are NICE recommendations [NICE-CG159]. Adolescent internet CT-SAD has preliminary randomized trial support [Leigh-Clark-2023]. Cultural and autism adaptations rest on reviews, cross-sectional studies and expert consensus [Hofmann-etal-2010] [Lei-etal-2024] [Lei-etal-2026]. NICE also advises against routine anticonvulsants, tricyclics or antipsychotics, whereas the Canadian guideline lists pregabalin as first-line [NICE-CG159] [Katzman-etal-2014].
