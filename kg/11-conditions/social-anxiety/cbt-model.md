**Bottom line:** In the Clark and Wells model, people with social anxiety disorder (SAD) enter social situations predicting they will come across badly, then shift attention inward and judge how they look from their own anxious feelings and a distorted self-image [Clark-Wells-1995] [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]. Safety behaviors and worry before and rumination after events stop them from discovering that others see them more favorably than they think. Draw the model from one recent situation in the first session; every later technique targets one of its loops.

## Components

- **Assumptions** that a social situation activates: excessively high standards ("I must always be interesting"), conditional beliefs ("If I show anxiety, people will think I'm incapable"), and unconditional beliefs about the self ("I'm boring", "I'm unlikable") [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
- **Negative automatic thoughts:** predictions of coming across badly ("I'll have nothing to say", "I'll blush") and judgments of current performance ("I'm being boring") [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
- **Processing of the self as a social object:** attention turns inward to monitor feelings and imagined appearance. The person builds an impression of how they look, often an image seen from an observer's viewpoint, from internal information ("my face feels hot, so I look beetroot red") [Spurr-Stopa-2002] [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]. These images are often stable over time and linked to memories of humiliating events near the onset of the disorder [Hackmann-etal-2000].
- **Safety behaviors:** actions meant to prevent the feared outcome, such as rehearsing what to say, avoiding eye contact, gripping a cup, holding back opinions or wearing clothes that hide sweat [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
- **Somatic and cognitive symptoms:** blushing, trembling, sweating or the mind going blank, which become further evidence of danger.
- **Anticipatory processing and post-event processing:** detailed negative rehearsal before an event, and a "post-mortem" afterward that dwells on perceived failures [Leigh-Clark-2018].

Related models share most components. The Rapee and Heimberg model stresses a mental representation of the self as the audience sees it, compared against the standard the person believes the audience expects [Rapee-Heimberg-1997]. Hofmann adds unrealistic social standards, low perceived control over emotion and a belief that one's social skills are poor [Hofmann-2007].

## How it keeps the problem going

1. **Self-focus loop:** inward attention increases awareness of anxious feelings and images, which seem to confirm the fear, and blocks out the real responses of others [Spurr-Stopa-2002].
2. **Safety behavior loop:** if nothing bad happens, the person credits the safety behavior, so the belief survives. Safety behaviors also increase self-focus and symptoms (gripping a glass makes the hand shake) [McManus-etal-2008]. In a small within-patient experiment, exposure plus dropping safety behaviors reduced anxiety and belief in the feared catastrophe more than exposure alone [Wells-etal-1995].
3. **Interpersonal loop:** behaviors such as saying little or monitoring every sentence can make the person seem distant or uninterested, so others warm to them less, which seems to confirm the fear [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
4. **Before and after loop:** anticipatory worry primes failure memories and avoidance; post-event processing turns ambiguous moments into "evidence" of rejection [Leigh-Clark-2018].

This explains why repeated social contact does not cure SAD: the person is never fully present to collect disconfirming evidence. In a trial of face-to-face and internet cognitive therapy, change in these processes statistically accounted for 60% of the improvement [Clark-etal-2023].

## Drawing it with the client

Build it in session 1 on a whiteboard or shared screen, starting from one recent, specific situation [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]. Socialize only; belief change comes later [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
1. Situation: "Take me to a recent time you felt very anxious with people."
2. Thoughts: "What was going through your mind? What were you worried people would think?"
3. Self-image: "Did you have an image of how you looked? If I'd been watching, what would I have seen, as you imagined it?" If no picture comes, ask for the felt sense of how they came across, which plays the same role [Spurr-Stopa-2002].
4. Symptoms: "What did you notice in your body?"
5. Attention: "Where was your attention, on the other person or on yourself?"
6. Safety behaviors: "Did you do anything to stop that happening or hide it?"
7. Link the arrows: "When you're focused on how red you feel, what happens to your picture of how you look?" and "If you always rehearse, how would you ever find out what happens if you don't?"
8. Add anticipatory worry and the post-mortem as the loop extends over time.

Ask the client to rate belief in the main fear (0 to 100) and keep the diagram for every session.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Maintaining process | Target and method |
|---|---|
| Self-focused attention | Externally focused attention training |
| Distorted self-image | Video and still photograph feedback |
| Safety behaviors | Drop them in behavioral experiments |
| Feared predictions and beliefs | Behavioral experiments, decatastrophizing, surveys |
| Anticipatory and post-event processing | Weigh their costs, switch to testing |
| Images tied to early memories | Discrimination training, imagery rescripting |

Methods are detailed in `social-anxiety.treatment`, `social-anxiety.video-feedback-and-attention-training` and `social-anxiety.behavioral-experiments-and-surveys` [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
