**Bottom line:** In cognitive therapy for social anxiety disorder (CT-SAD), behavioral experiments are the main engine of change: the client states a specific, observable prediction, enters the situation without safety behaviors and with attention on the interaction, and checks what others actually do [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]. Early experiments drop safety behaviors; later ones deliberately show the feared sign (decatastrophizing). Surveys, rumination work and memory work support the experiments but do not replace them.

## When to use

From about session 4 onward, in most sessions and as homework, once the client has seen in session 2 and 3 that self-focus and safety behaviors make things worse [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]. Choose the belief with the highest current rating on the weekly cognitions measure.

## How it works

Safety behaviors prevent disconfirmation: in a small experiment with patients, exposure plus dropping them reduced anxiety and belief in the feared catastrophe more than exposure alone [Wells-etal-1995]. Experiments therefore change behavior and attention together so the client can see that feared outcomes rarely happen and matter less than predicted when they do [McManus-etal-2008].

## Steps

1. **Pick the target.** A prediction ("I'll go bright red"), assumption ("I have to speak fluently to be accepted"), unconditional belief ("I'm unlikable") or self-image ("I look like a rabbit in headlights") [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
2. **Loosen the belief.** Review what earlier experiments and video showed, and consider a survey.
3. **Write an observable prediction with a rating.** "If I speak without rehearsing to two people on a video call, they'll think I'm boring (60%), and I'll know because they'll look away or leave early."
4. **Run it.** Drop the named safety behaviors, focus externally, and watch others' reactions. Record it on video if possible.
5. **Review.** Re-rate the belief, compare with the prediction, and ask what it means for the wider belief: "What does this tell you about whether you're unlikable?"
6. **Set the next test** as homework, harder or in a new setting.

**Examples** [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]:

| Belief | Drop the safety behavior | Decatastrophizing version |
|---|---|---|
| "I'll have nothing to say" | Call a shop without preparing | Pause on purpose mid-conversation |
| "I'm boring" | Speak spontaneously in a group | Say things you think are boring, on purpose |
| "I'll blush" | Talk without hiding the face | Wear blusher to buy something in a shop |
| "I'm sweaty" | Leave the jacket off in a meeting | Dampen the underarms and give a short talk |
| "I'll shake" | Hold the cup loosely | Shake the hand on purpose and watch reactions |
| "People are staring" | Look up and count who is looking | Drop keys in public and count who stares |

**Surveys.** To loosen a belief such as "people will think I'm weird if I blush", the therapist asks others: Do you blush? What do you think when you see someone blush? How weird does it make them seem, 0 to 100? Read responses before sharing them [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

**Virtual audiences.** Recorded audiences of varying size and difficulty let clients practice speeches when live audiences are impractical, including deliberate pauses [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

**Anticipatory worry and post-event rumination.** If these do not fall on weekly measures, weigh their advantages and disadvantages, then redirect: "How could we test this instead of going over it?" A flashcard can prompt switching attention away from the post-mortem, especially after posting online [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

**Socially traumatic memories.** If the self-image persists, look for an early memory with the same meaning, such as being mocked for blushing in class [Hackmann-etal-2000]. Start with discrimination training ("Then I was 10 and bullied; now I'm 34 in a work meeting"). If the memory keeps intruding, use imagery rescripting: identify and rate the meaning, update it with what therapy has shown, then relive the memory from the younger self, the adult self stepping in, and the younger self receiving that help [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

## Guidance

- If the client wants to "just get through it" → agree a precise prediction first. Because without one there is nothing to disconfirm [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
- If the experiment "went fine because I was careful" → identify the subtle safety behavior and repeat without it. Because credit given to the safety behavior keeps the belief intact [Wells-etal-1995].
- If the client fears a decatastrophizing task → do it together, with the therapist showing the feared sign first. Because in CT-SAD therapist and client deliberately demonstrate the feared behavior and observe reactions [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
- If an experiment goes badly → review what others actually did, how long it mattered and how the client coped. Because ambiguous or mildly negative reactions are common and survivable (expert consensus).
- If the client is remote → use colleagues on the call, phone calls during the session, or walks with the therapist on the phone. Because these keep in-session experiments possible without an office [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

Avoid: "See, I told you nobody would notice." Ask the client what they noticed.

## Common problems

- Testing vague fears ("it'll go badly") that cannot be checked.
- Leaving out the generalization question, so learning stays tied to one event.
- Reassurance instead of experiments. Surveys and discussion loosen beliefs; experiments change them [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

## Evidence

Behavioral experiments are a core component of CT-SAD, which is guideline-recommended; one trial ran a mean of 13.5 in-session experiments per patient [Clark-etal-2023]. Support for dropping safety behaviors comes from a small within-patient experiment and an experimental study in socially anxious volunteers [Wells-etal-1995] [McManus-etal-2008]. Memory rescripting has pilot evidence: in 11 patients, one rescripting session improved negative beliefs, image distress and fear of evaluation, while a prior control session did not [Wild-etal-2008].
