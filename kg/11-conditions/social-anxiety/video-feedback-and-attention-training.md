**Bottom line:** Video feedback works when the client first makes specific, rated predictions of how they will look, is coached to watch themselves "as a stranger", and then compares predictions with what the recording shows; prepared this way, 98% of patients saw they came across better than predicted [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017]. Externally focused attention training follows, so the client can attend to the interaction and collect real evidence during behavioral experiments [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]. Computerized attention bias modification is not ready for routine use [Heeren-etal-2015].

## When to use

In cognitive therapy for social anxiety disorder (CT-SAD), video feedback is first used in session 3 to review the two recorded conversations from session 2, and then throughout therapy. Attention training usually comes in the next session [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017] [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

## How it works

Self-focused attention increases access to anxious feelings and images and stops the person noticing how others respond [Spurr-Stopa-2002]. Experimentally, self-focus and safety behaviors were unhelpful in several ways [McManus-etal-2008]. Five biases can block learning from video: re-experiencing feelings while watching, searching for any flaw, discounting the camera, mistaking safety behaviors for personal deficits, and reactivating the self-critic [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].

## Steps

**Video feedback** [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017]:
1. **Set up.** Get written consent and record routinely so the camera becomes ordinary. Frame both people, not a close-up of the client. If blushing is feared, place a color chart in shot, usually without drawing attention to it.
2. **Elicit predictions.** Ask for 0 to 100 ratings: how anxious they will look, how much each feared outcome will show (boring, shaky, sweaty), and overall performance. Make them concrete: ask the client to demonstrate the shake, point to the shade of red, or show the size of a sweat patch.
3. **Run the internal video.** The client closes their eyes and pictures how they will appear.
4. **Set the viewing mode.** "Watch it like a television show, as if you're watching two strangers. Use only what you can see and hear, not what you felt." With harsh self-critics, show the first 30 seconds with the sound off, or cover their image at first and watch the other person.
5. **Watch, pause early, rewind.** Check: "Are you watching that person as you would anyone else, or as your worst critic?" Rewind to the moments the client felt worst.
6. **Re-rate and compare.** Put before and after ratings in a two-column table. Ask: "What does this tell you about how visible your anxiety is?"
7. **Spot safety behaviors.** Ask whether apparent awkwardness was deliberate (holding back, rehearsing); the client can choose to drop it.
8. **Consolidate.** Add the conversation partner's written feedback (partners are not told the client's fears), capture the moment of disconfirmation as a still image, and write the learning on it: "I felt 80% anxious but I don't look it."

**Externally focused attention training** [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]:
1. Explain the aim: attention on the interaction, not on yourself, so you can see how people actually respond.
2. Practice shifting attention outward in steps: sounds, colors, textures, music, then the therapist reading a passage aloud.
3. Reduce self-consciousness: the therapist angles their chair away and closes their own eyes when the client does.
4. Apply it in conversation: "Get lost in what the other person is saying," and rate self-focus 0 to 100 after each experiment.
5. Assign daily practice, using recorded exercises.

## Guidance

- If the client says "the camera doesn't show how red I really was" → set up the color chart beforehand and ask them to choose their predicted shade before viewing. Because discounting the video is a known bias that calibration prevents [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- If the client cringes or starts criticizing as soon as they hear their voice → pause, name the critic, and restart with sound off or from a friend's viewpoint. Because self-critical commentary distorts what they see [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- If the client still rates themselves poorly after viewing → turn attention to how others in the video react and share partner feedback. Because others' responses show the feared features carry little weight [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- If therapy is remote → record with the platform only where secure and policy allows, and ask clients who record themselves not to watch until the joint session. Because unprepared viewing invites the biases above [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
- If the client asks for a computerized attention training program → explain the small, short-lived effects and keep it optional. Because attention bias modification effects were small and absent at follow-up [Heeren-etal-2015].

## Common problems

- Showing video without predictions or preparation. See `social-anxiety.pitfall-unprepared-video-feedback`.
- Zooming in on the client, which raises self-consciousness and hides the interaction.
- Treating attention training as distraction. The target is engagement with the other person.
- Storing recordings carelessly. Agree storage and deletion in the consent.

## Evidence

Supported by within-treatment data rather than dismantling trials. In 47 patients, ratings improved for 98% after prepared video feedback, and Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (LSAS) scores fell from 73.4 to 65.5 in the following week, more than in control weeks [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017]. Two studies with little preparation found no benefit [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017], and videotaped feedback did not enhance exposure-based treatment [Katzman-etal-2014]. Wells's Attention Training Technique, a separate exercise, has early single-case support in panic and social phobia [Wells-etal-1997], and in a small preliminary comparison it was as effective as cognitive therapy over 6 weeks [Donald-etal-2014] (emerging evidence). Computerized attention bias modification gave a small reduction in symptoms (g = 0.27) with no effect at 4-month follow-up [Heeren-etal-2015], and broader meta-analysis found effects may disappear after correcting for publication bias [Cristea-etal-2015].
