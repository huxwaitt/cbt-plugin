**Bottom line:** Video feedback changes a distorted self-image only when the client predicts in detail how they will look, is set up to watch "as a stranger", and compares predictions with the footage afterwards [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017]. Without this, social anxiety biases take over the viewing, and studies that skipped preparation found no benefit [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017]. Never let the client watch a recording alone before the joint session [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

## What it looks like

- The therapist says "Let's have a look at how you came across" and presses play.
- The client winces at their voice, comments "I look so awkward", and points to details no one else would notice.
- Afterwards the client says, "The camera doesn't pick up how red I went."
- Quietness that came from holding back is read as "proof I'm dull."
- In remote therapy, the client records the call and watches it alone that evening.

## Why it happens

Therapists assume the evidence on screen will speak for itself. People with social anxiety disorder (SAD) bring five biases to the viewing: re-experiencing their anxious feelings and reading them into the image, searching for any possible flaw, discounting the camera's accuracy, mistaking their safety behaviors for personal deficits, and switching on their habitual self-critical commentary [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017]. Short sessions also tempt therapists to cut preparation and discussion, which is where the change happens.

## The fix

- If you are about to show a recording → first get 0 to 100 ratings of the specific feared features and overall performance, and have the client demonstrate them (shake the hand, choose the shade of red). Because clear predictions stop the client hunting for new flaws [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- If blushing, sweating or shaking is the fear → calibrate before recording, for example with a color chart in shot. Because otherwise the client can discount the footage [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- If the client re-experiences feelings while watching → instruct them to watch "that person" as if a stranger on television, using only what can be seen and heard. Because feelings are not visible to others [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- If the self-critic switches on → pause early to check the viewing mode, try the first 30 seconds with sound off, or watch from a liked friend's perspective. Because self-critical commentary distorts perception [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- If the client sees themselves as withdrawn → ask whether they were deliberately holding back. Because apparent deficits are often visible safety behaviors that the client can choose to drop [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- If therapy is remote → ask clients who record calls not to watch until the joint review. Because unguided viewing invites these biases [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

Example phrasing: "Before we watch, let's write down exactly what you expect to see. How anxious will you look, from 0 to 100? Show me how much your hand will shake."

Avoid: "You looked fine, didn't you?"

## Sources note

Procedures and the five biases come from the Oxford group's clinical guidance and audit data, in which 98% of prepared patients rated themselves more favorably after viewing [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017]. The two negative studies they cite gave little preparation or discussion, which is an explanation rather than a tested cause.
