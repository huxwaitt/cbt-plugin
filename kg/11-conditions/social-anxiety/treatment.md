**Bottom line:** Offer individual cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) built on the Clark and Wells model or the Heimberg model as the first treatment for adults; the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) does not recommend group CBT in preference or a low-intensity first step [NICE-CG159] [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]. Cognitive therapy for social anxiety disorder (CT-SAD) runs up to 14 weekly sessions of up to 90 minutes, with in-session behavioral experiments, some outside the office [NICE-CG159] [Clark-etal-2023]. In one trial, 84% of patients no longer had the diagnosis after treatment [Clark-etal-2006].

## When to use

For adults with social anxiety disorder (SAD) as the main problem, including when depression or substance use is secondary (see `social-anxiety.adaptations`). From about age 15, adult protocols can be considered for young people who can use them [NICE-CG159].

## How it works

Each component reverses a maintaining process in the model: self-focus, the distorted self-image, safety behaviors, anticipatory and post-event processing, and early memories (see `social-anxiety.cbt-model`). The aim is for the client to collect new evidence of how they actually come across while fully engaged in the situation, not to endure anxiety until it fades [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

## Steps

Typical CT-SAD sequence [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020] [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017] [Clark-etal-2023]:

| Session | Content |
|---|---|
| Assessment | Diagnosis, goals, weekly measures (`social-anxiety.assessment`) |
| 1 | Goals and the individualized model; socialize, no belief change yet |
| 2 | Self-focused attention and safety behaviors experiment: two short conversations, first self-focused with safety behaviors, then externally focused without them, both recorded |
| 3 | Video feedback of the two conversations, with predictions made first |
| 4 | Externally focused attention training; behavioral experiments start, in session and as homework |
| 5 to 12 | Behavioral experiments in and out of the office; decatastrophizing; surveys; work on anticipatory worry and post-event processing as needed |
| Later sessions | Memory work (discrimination training, imagery rescripting) and work on unconditional beliefs and self-criticism if images or beliefs persist |
| Final sessions | Therapy blueprint and relapse prevention |
| Follow-up | Up to 3 booster sessions in the next 3 months |

Behavioral experiments run in most sessions from session 4 [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]; in one trial therapists ran a mean of 13.5 in-session experiments per patient [Clark-etal-2023]. Patients attended a mean of 12.8 sessions [Clark-etal-2023].

**Heimberg model alternative:** 15 sessions of 60 minutes plus one 90-minute exposure session over about 4 months, covering education, cognitive restructuring, graduated exposure in session and as homework, core beliefs and relapse prevention [NICE-CG159].

**Delivery standards:** use a manual, regular supervision, competence monitoring and a session-by-session outcome measure reviewed with the client [NICE-CG159]. All core components can be delivered by video call; start by video rather than telephone and ask the client to hide their self-view [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

## Guidance

- If the service only offers 50 to 60 minute sessions → book double sessions for experiments and video feedback. Because experiments, including some out of the office, need time to set up, run and review [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
- If the client asks for group treatment first → explain that individual therapy has stronger evidence, but respect an informed choice. Because NICE advises against routinely preferring group CBT [NICE-CG159] and individual cognitive therapy outperformed group treatment in a trial [Mortberg-etal-2007].
- If the client declines face-to-face CBT → offer CBT-based supported self-help or internet cognitive therapy. Because NICE supports self-help for people who decline CBT [NICE-CG159] and internet CT-SAD matched face-to-face outcomes on the primary measure, though face-to-face did slightly better on a behavior test [Clark-etal-2023].
- If belief ratings are not moving by mid-treatment → check that experiments drop safety behaviors and test a specific prediction. Because experiments without these features leave beliefs intact (see `social-anxiety.pitfall-safety-behaviors-during-exposure`).
- If the client wants mindfulness or supportive counseling instead → explain the evidence and agree a plan together. Because NICE advises against routinely offering mindfulness-based interventions or supportive therapy for SAD [NICE-CG159].
- If symptoms only partly respond after an adequate course → discuss adding a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) with a prescriber while continuing individual CBT. Because NICE recommends this combination for partial response [NICE-CG159].

Example phrasing: "We've seen on the model that rehearsing keeps the fear going. Shall we find out together what happens if you speak without rehearsing, with someone you've never met?"

## Common problems

- Treating CT-SAD as graded exposure with habituation. The aim is testing predictions while externally focused.
- Skipping video feedback or the session 2 experiment because of equipment or embarrassment. Routine recording with consent makes it ordinary [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2017].
- Ending without a written blueprint covering what was learned and how to handle setbacks [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

## Evidence

Guideline-recommended and supported by randomized trials. In a network meta-analysis of 101 trials (13,164 participants), individual CBT had a standardized mean difference of -1.19 against waitlist and was one of only two classes better than placebo [Mayo-Wilson-etal-2014]. In trials, CT-SAD outperformed exposure plus applied relaxation (84% vs 42% losing the diagnosis) [Clark-etal-2006], fluoxetine with self-exposure [Clark-etal-2003], and interpersonal psychotherapy (response 65.8% vs 42.1%) [Stangier-etal-2011]. Internet CT-SAD needed 6.45 therapist hours against 15.8 for the same change [Clark-etal-2023]. NICE recommends both models as first-line [NICE-CG159]. The Canadian guideline calls CBT the gold-standard psychological treatment [Katzman-etal-2014]. The American Psychiatric Association has no social anxiety guideline; its anxiety coverage is a legacy panic disorder guideline [APA-Psychiatry-Guidelines-Site].
