**Bottom line:** Social anxiety disorder (SAD) is a marked, persistent fear of social or performance situations where the person might be scrutinized, driven by the fear of acting in a way, or showing anxiety, that will be judged negatively [APA-DSM5TR-2022] [Katzman-etal-2014]. It is common (about 7% of US adults in a year), starts early, and often stays chronic without treatment [NIMH-SAD-Statistics] [Leigh-Clark-2018]. Individual cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the best-supported first treatment [Mayo-Wilson-etal-2014].

## Diagnostic essentials

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) requires all of the following, paraphrased here [APA-DSM5TR-2022] [Katzman-etal-2014]:
- Marked fear or anxiety about one or more social situations involving possible scrutiny (conversations, strangers, being watched eating or drinking, performing).
- Fear of acting in a way or showing anxiety symptoms that will be negatively evaluated: humiliating, embarrassing, leading to rejection, or offending others.
- The situations almost always provoke fear and are avoided or endured with intense fear.
- The fear is out of proportion to the actual threat and to the sociocultural context.
- Duration is persistent, typically 6 months or more.
- It causes clinically significant distress or impairment.
- It is not due to a substance or another medical condition and is not better explained by another mental disorder. If a medical condition is present (for example stuttering, obesity, disfigurement), the fear is unrelated to it or excessive.

**Children:** the anxiety must occur with peers, not only with adults, and may show as crying, tantrums, freezing, clinging, shrinking or failing to speak [Katzman-etal-2014].

**Specifier:** "performance only" when the fear is restricted to speaking or performing in public. The 2013 DSM-5 dropped the older "generalized" subtype [Katzman-etal-2014]. DSM-5-TR removed the parenthetical "(social phobia)" from the name [APA-SAD-FactSheet-2022].

**International classification differences.** The International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) codes SAD as 6B04. It requires symptoms for at least several months rather than a fixed 6 months, abolishes the requirement in the 10th Revision (ICD-10) for a minimum count of physical symptoms, folds childhood social anxiety into the same category, and explicitly accommodates culture-specific forms such as taijin kyofusho [Domschke-Zwanzger-2025]. An ICD-11 and DSM-5 comparison rated the differences as minor [First-etal-2021].

## Course and prevalence

- **Prevalence:** in the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication (2001 to 2003, DSM-IV), 7.1% of adults had SAD in the past year (women 8.0%, men 6.1%; 3.1% at age 60 and over) and 12.1% over their lifetime; among adolescents aged 13 to 18, lifetime prevalence was 9.1% [NIMH-SAD-Statistics]. Rates are far lower in East Asian surveys [Hofmann-etal-2010].
- **Onset:** median age 13, and about 90% of cases begin by age 23; new onset after about 25 is uncommon [Leigh-Clark-2018]. About half begin by age 11 [Stein-Stein-2008].
- **Course:** chronic without treatment. In one 12-year follow-up only 37% recovered, compared with 82% for panic disorder without agoraphobia [Leigh-Clark-2018].
- **Impact:** poorer education and work outcomes, fewer and lower quality relationships, and a raised risk of later depression and substance misuse [Leigh-Clark-2018] [Stein-Stein-2008].

## Differential and comorbidity

Ask what the person fears will happen; in SAD it is negative evaluation by others.
- **Panic disorder and agoraphobia:** fear of the panic sensations themselves or of being unable to escape, not of judgment (expert consensus).
- **Generalized anxiety disorder:** worry spans many domains, not mainly social ones (expert consensus).
- **Avoidant personality disorder:** overlapping, more pervasive pattern; often co-occurs, so assess both [NICE-CG159].
- **Body dysmorphic disorder:** concern centers on a perceived appearance flaw (expert consensus); the two often co-occur [Katzman-etal-2014].
- **Autism:** social difficulty from differences in social communication rather than, or as well as, fear of evaluation (expert consensus); the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) lists autism among conditions to check [NICE-CG159].
- **Psychosis:** fear of others driven by persecutory beliefs (expert consensus) [NICE-CG159].
- **Taijin kyofusho:** fear of offending others (for example by blushing, gaze or body odor), which the DSM criteria cover through their "offending others" clause [Hofmann-etal-2010] [Katzman-etal-2014].

Comorbidity is common: up to 72% of people with SAD meet criteria for another disorder, most often depression and other anxiety disorders; avoidant personality disorder, body dysmorphic disorder, substance use disorders and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) also often co-occur [Katzman-etal-2014]. If depression co-occurs, ask which came first; drinking to manage social anxiety should not block SAD treatment [NICE-CG159] (see `social-anxiety.adaptations`).

## CBT approach in brief

Treatment targets the maintaining processes: self-focused attention, negative self-images, safety behaviors, and anticipatory and post-event processing (see `social-anxiety.cbt-model`). In a network meta-analysis of 101 trials, individual CBT had the largest effect of all treatments against waitlist; it and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs and SNRIs) were the only classes to beat an appropriate placebo, and SSRIs are the evidence-based choice for people who decline therapy [Mayo-Wilson-etal-2014]. The protocol is on `social-anxiety.treatment`.

## Risk notes

In people aged 10 to 25, social anxiety shows small cross-sectional associations with suicidal ideation and attempts, and several studies suggest depression does not fully explain the link [Leigh-etal-2023]. Screen for suicide risk at intake, especially with comorbid depression, isolation or alcohol use, and follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`. If risk is present, assess it that day, make a safety plan (`02-risk.safety-planning`) and escalate. US: call, text or chat 988, available 24 hours [Lifeline-988], or 911 or the nearest emergency department for immediate danger. UK: Samaritans 116 123, free and open 24 hours [Samaritans-Site], National Health Service (NHS) 111, or 999. Many clients with SAD avoid phone calls, so agree a route in advance, such as 988 text or chat (expert consensus).
