**Bottom line:** People with social anxiety disorder (SAD) face social situations every day without getting better, because safety behaviors and self-focus stop them from learning that the feared outcome does not happen [Wells-etal-1995] [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020]. Exposure that keeps these behaviors in place repeats the client's everyday experience. Name the safety behaviors, drop them deliberately, and test a specific prediction each time.

## What it looks like

- A hierarchy of situations is climbed, ratings of anxiety fall slightly, but the client still believes "they'll see I'm boring" at 80%.
- The client reports, "It was fine, but only because I'd prepared topics and kept the conversation on them."
- Homework records list situations entered but no predictions and no notes on what others did.
- The client's anxiety returns at full strength in any new setting.
- The therapist praises attendance at the event rather than asking what was learned.

## Why it happens

Safety behaviors are often subtle and mental: rehearsing sentences, checking how one sounds, avoiding eye contact, gripping a glass, staying near the exit, letting a friend do the talking. Clients see them as helpful, and those high in social anxiety use more of them, more often, in more situations [McManus-etal-2008]. Therapists trained in habituation-based exposure may focus on staying in the situation until anxiety drops and not ask what the client did while there. When nothing bad happens, the client credits the safety behavior, so the belief survives. Long-standing SAD persists despite daily exposure for this reason [Leigh-Clark-2018].

## The fix

- If an exposure task is planned → ask "What will you do to stop the feared thing happening or being noticed?" and list each safety behavior to drop. Because exposure plus dropping safety behaviors lowered anxiety and belief in the catastrophe more than exposure alone [Wells-etal-1995].
- If the client cannot identify safety behaviors → run the session 2 experiment: one conversation with safety behaviors and self-focus, one without. Because experiencing the contrast shows that self-focus and safety behaviors make anxiety and self-judgment worse [McManus-etal-2008].
- If the task is framed as "stay until the anxiety goes down" → reframe it as a test: write the prediction, how likely it is (0 to 100), and how the client will know if it happened. Because experiments in cognitive therapy for SAD (CT-SAD) test observable predictions while externally focused [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].
- If the client says it went well "because I was careful" → repeat the same situation without that behavior. Because credit given to the safety behavior blocks learning [Wells-etal-1995].
- If progress is still slow → check attention: ask the client to rate how self-focused they were (0 to 100) and add attention training. Because self-focus keeps the person from seeing how others respond [Warnock-Parkes-etal-2020].

Example phrasing: "You've been to three meetings, which took courage. In each one you planned your words in advance. What do you think would happen if you spoke without planning?"

Avoid: "Well done for going. Keep going and the anxiety will fade."

## Sources note

The core evidence is a small within-patient experiment comparing exposure with and without safety behaviors [Wells-etal-1995] and an experiment manipulating safety behaviors and self-focus in socially anxious volunteers [McManus-etal-2008]. In a randomized trial, cognitive therapy built on these principles outperformed exposure plus applied relaxation (84% vs 42% losing the diagnosis) [Clark-etal-2006]. Phrasing advice is expert consensus.
