**Bottom line:** In the Ehlers and Clark model, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) persists when the trauma is processed in a way that creates a sense of serious current threat. That threat comes from two sources: negative appraisals of the trauma or its aftermath, and a poorly integrated trauma memory that is easily triggered. Avoidance, suppression, rumination and safety behaviors stop both from changing [Ehlers-Clark-2000]. Treatment targets all three.

## Components

**1. Negative appraisals** of the trauma and its consequences [Ehlers-Clark-2000]:
- About the event: "Nowhere is safe." "It was my fault." "I should have fought back."
- About the aftermath: symptoms ("I'm going mad"), other people's reactions ("They think I'm weak"), physical changes ("My body is ruined"), and lost futures.
- Appraisals drive specific emotions: danger leads to fear, blame to guilt, others' judgment to shame, violated rules to anger, loss to sadness.

**2. The nature of the trauma memory** [Ehlers-Clark-2000]:
- Poorly elaborated and poorly placed in its context of time, place and later information, so it is re-experienced as happening now rather than remembered as past.
- Strong associative learning: sights, sounds, smells or body sensations that were present during the trauma trigger it.
- Strong perceptual priming: triggers are detected easily and often outside awareness, so intrusions seem to come "out of the blue".
- Hot spots: the worst moments, where the strongest emotion and the most threatening meanings sit [Wild-etal-2020].

**3. Maintaining strategies** [Ehlers-Clark-2000] [Wild-etal-2020]:
- Thought suppression and avoidance of reminders.
- Rumination ("Why me?" "If only...").
- Safety behaviors (checking, carrying weapons, hypervigilance, sitting near exits).
- Dissociation, numbing, alcohol or drugs.
- Withdrawal from activities and people.

**Two related frames** used by other protocols:
- **Cognitive processing therapy (CPT):** survivors try to fit the trauma into prior beliefs. Assimilation distorts the event to fit old beliefs, often through self-blame or just-world thinking ("I must have done something wrong"). Over-accommodation changes beliefs too far ("No one can be trusted"). The goal is balanced accommodation [VA-CSP591-2018] [Dondanville-etal-2016]. Therapists who prioritized assimilated beliefs before over-accommodated ones achieved greater improvement [Farmer-etal-2017].
- **Emotional processing theory (prolonged exposure):** PTSD reflects a pathological fear structure linked to beliefs that the world is utterly dangerous and the self incompetent. Avoidance prevents corrective learning [VA-CSP591-2018]. The inhibitory learning account adds that exposure works by violating expectancies rather than by fear dropping [Craske-etal-2014].

## How it keeps the problem going

- **Suppression cycle:** pushing memories away → more intrusions → stronger belief that "I'm losing control" → more suppression.
- **Rumination cycle:** going over "why" and "if only" → keeps attention on the trauma and blame → more guilt, shame and intrusions, and no updating.
- **Safety behavior cycle:** checking, vigilance or avoidance → nothing bad happens, and the client credits the safety behavior → "Nowhere is safe" survives → more threat.
- **Memory cycle:** avoiding the memory → the worst moments never connect with what happened next ("I survived", "It wasn't my fault") → the memory stays fragmented and "now", so triggers keep firing.
- **Withdrawal cycle:** giving up work, friends and hobbies → fewer disconfirming experiences and more low mood → "My life is ruined" is confirmed.

## Drawing it with the client

1. Start from one recent intrusion. Use an intrusion diary if needed [Wild-etal-2020].
2. Ask about the memory: "When it comes, how much does it feel like it is happening now, from 0 to 100?" "What triggered it?"
3. Ask about meaning: "What does it say about you that this happened? That you reacted the way you did?" "What's the worst part of it for you now?"
4. Ask about coping: "What do you do when the memory comes? What do you do to stay safe?" Then: "What effect does that have in the long run?"
5. Draw the cycles collaboratively and share them [Wild-etal-2020]. Put "sense of current threat" in the center, memory and appraisals feeding in, and strategies looping back.
6. Test a link live. A suppression demonstration, trying not to think of a specific image, shows that suppressed images return [Wild-etal-2020].
7. Summarize in the client's words: "So it makes sense that you feel in danger, because the memory feels like now, and you believe you're to blame. Pushing it away and checking the locks keep both going."

## Treatment targets it implies

- **Elaborate and update the trauma memory:** reliving or narrative writing, then link new information to each hot spot, and teach the client to tell triggers now from the trauma then [Wild-etal-2020].
- **Change the appraisals:** Socratic questioning, surveys, responsibility pie charts and behavioral experiments on blame, shame and danger [Wild-etal-2020].
- **Drop the maintaining strategies:** reduce suppression, rumination and safety behaviors, and reclaim activities from session 1 [Wild-etal-2020].
- Track change in trauma-related beliefs alongside symptoms. Good outcome in cognitive therapy for PTSD was related to greater change in dysfunctional posttraumatic cognitions [Ehlers-etal-2005].
- These targets map onto the components that the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) lists for any trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): memory processing, restructuring meanings, overcoming avoidance and restoring functioning [NICE-NG116].
