**Bottom line:** Comorbidity, substance use, dissociation and even psychosis are reasons to adapt trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), not to withhold it. Treat posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) first unless depression or risk blocks the work, integrate substance use care rather than requiring abstinence first, and keep medication as an option for those who prefer it. Step up through more sessions or intensive formats, and refer for acute danger, acute psychosis or mania, or medically unsafe substance use.

## The principle

Guidelines converge: co-occurring disorders should not preclude first-line trauma-focused therapy [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023], and the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises adjusting time, safety and support rather than excluding people [NICE-NG116]. Adapt pace, format, length and language while keeping the active ingredients: memory processing, meaning change and approach.

## Guidance

**Comorbidity and severity**
- If depression coexists → usually treat PTSD first; treat depression first if it is severe enough to block trauma work or creates risk to self or others. Because depression often improves with successful PTSD treatment [NICE-NG116].
- If the client has a substance use disorder → offer trauma-focused CBT alongside usual substance use care. Because the American Psychological Association (APA) strongly recommends this combination over substance use care alone [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025], and an integrated prolonged exposure protocol reduced PTSD more than relapse prevention in veterans [Back-etal-2019]. Arrange medically managed withdrawal first if withdrawal could be dangerous.
- If the client has suicidal ideation but is not at imminent risk → make a safety plan and proceed with trauma-focused work while monitoring. Because suicidal ideation fell during cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure, in step with PTSD improvement [Gradus-etal-2013], and NICE puts the safety plan into initial planning [NICE-NG116].
- If intent, a plan or imminent danger emerges → pause memory work and follow `02-risk.crisis-response`: in the United States (US) call or text 988 or call 911; in the UK call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because acute risk is managed before trauma processing resumes (expert consensus).
- If the client reports dissociation → plan grounding, use eyes-open or written memory work, and continue. Because dissociation did not moderate psychotherapy outcome across 21 trials [Hoeboer-etal-2020], and narrative writing is easier to pace [Wild-etal-2020].
- If the client has a psychotic disorder and stable care → consider standard prolonged exposure or eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) without a stabilization phase. Because 8 weekly 90-minute sessions were effective and safe in a trial of 155 patients with psychotic disorders [VanDenBerg-etal-2015].
- If the client is still living with the perpetrator or in real danger → prioritize safety planning and safeguarding, and keep exposure targets objectively safe. Because NICE asks clinicians to account for the safety and stability of the person's circumstances [NICE-NG116].

**Stepping up and down**
- If scores have not fallen meaningfully by session 8 to 10 → review adherence and avoidance, then extend. Because about a third of completers in variable-length cognitive processing therapy needed 13 to 18 sessions [Galovski-etal-2012].
- If weekly attendance is impossible or dropout risk is high → offer a massed or intensive format. Because 10 prolonged exposure sessions over 2 weeks [Foa-etal-2018] and cognitive therapy over about a week [Ehlers-etal-2014] matched weekly delivery.
- If symptoms are mild, the trauma was more than 3 months ago and the client prefers digital help → consider supported computerized trauma-focused CBT, unless symptoms are severe, dissociation is prominent, or there is risk of harm. Because NICE limits it to those cases [NICE-NG116].
- If the client cannot attend in person → use secure video. Because the US Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) strongly recommend video delivery when validated or when no other option exists [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].

**Culture, language and access**
- If culture, faith or language shape how distress is expressed → explore the client's explanatory model and adapt examples, metaphors and family involvement. Because cultural adaptations can improve acceptability and outcomes [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025].
- If the client asks for a therapist of a particular gender → accommodate where possible. Because NICE lists therapist choice that reflects the trauma experience [NICE-NG116].

**Medication**
- If the client prefers medication → refer for a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) such as sertraline or paroxetine, or venlafaxine. Because VA/DoD strongly recommends these drugs [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023] and NICE suggests venlafaxine or an SSRI when preferred [NICE-NG116].
- If a benzodiazepine is prescribed for PTSD → raise it with the prescriber. Because VA/DoD recommends against benzodiazepines [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
- If nightmares persist after trauma-focused work → discuss prazosin with the prescriber and present it as uncertain. Because VA/DoD weakly suggests it for nightmares [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023], while APA found insufficient evidence to recommend it [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025].
- If combining or augmenting therapy with medication comes up → defer to a licensed prescriber. Because evidence is insufficient either way [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].

## Example phrasings

- "Many people with PTSD also drink to cope. We don't need you to stop completely before we start. We'll work on both together."
- "Feeling spaced out during memory work is common. If it happens, we'll slow down and bring you back to the room. It doesn't mean the treatment won't work for you."
- Avoid: "You need to be stable before we can touch the trauma." It implies the client is too fragile and can delay effective care.

## Evidence

APA rates trauma-focused CBT plus substance use treatment as first-line for co-occurring disorders (strong) [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025]. The psychosis and dissociation findings come from one randomized trial and one meta-analysis respectively. Evidence for older adults, people with intellectual disability and many cultural groups remains thin, so these adaptations rest on expert consensus.
