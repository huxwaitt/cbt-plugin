**Bottom line:** Cognitive processing therapy (CPT) treats posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) by finding and testing "stuck points", the beliefs that keep the trauma unresolved, first about blame for the event and then about safety, trust, power and control, esteem and intimacy. It runs 12 weekly sessions, about 60 minutes individually or 90 minutes in groups [VA-NCPTSD-CPT]. Writing a trauma account is optional [VA-NCPTSD-CPT]. It is a first-line, strongly recommended treatment [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025] [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].

## When to use

- Adults with PTSD after any trauma type, individually or in groups.
- Especially useful where self-blame, guilt, anger or broken beliefs dominate, or where the client prefers not to recount the memory in detail.
- Clients need enough literacy for worksheets or a simplified version; the manual includes simplified worksheets [Resick-etal-2024].

## How it works

Survivors try to fit the trauma into earlier beliefs. **Assimilation** bends the event to fit old beliefs ("If only I had done X, it wouldn't have happened"). **Over-accommodation** bends beliefs too far ("I can never trust anyone"). CPT works toward **accommodation**: balanced beliefs that fit both the trauma and the rest of life [VA-CSP591-2018] [Dondanville-etal-2016]. Feelings that flow from the event itself, such as fear and grief, are allowed to run their course, while feelings produced by distorted beliefs, such as misplaced guilt, are reduced by changing the beliefs [VA-CSP591-2018].

## Steps

Session titles follow the second edition manual [Resick-etal-2024]; content follows the trial protocol [VA-CSP591-2018].

| Session | Focus | Practice assignment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Overview of PTSD and CPT | Symptoms, the cognitive model, identify the index (worst) event, explain stuck points | Write an impact statement: why the event happened and how it changed beliefs |
| 2 Impact statement | Read it; move stuck points to the stuck point log; gentle Socratic questions on self-blame | ABC worksheets daily (A: activating event, B: belief or thought, C: consequence or feeling) |
| 3 ABC worksheets | Review; shape stuck points into testable statements | More ABCs; optional written account of the worst event |
| 4 Processing the index event | Socratic dialogue on blame: intent versus responsibility, hindsight bias, outcome-based reasoning | Continue worksheets |
| 5 Exploring questions | Introduce questions for testing one stuck point | Exploring questions worksheets |
| 6 Thinking patterns | Recurring patterns such as jumping to conclusions or all-or-nothing thinking | Patterns worksheet |
| 7 Alternative thoughts | Worksheet combining earlier steps, ending in a balanced statement with belief ratings; theme 1, safety | Worksheets on safety |
| 8 to 11 Themes | Safety, trust, power and control, esteem | Giving and receiving compliments; one pleasant activity daily |
| 12 Intimacy and facing the future | Read the new impact statement beside the original; review | Plan continued practice |

In earlier editions, sessions 5 and 7 were called Challenging Questions and Challenging Beliefs Worksheets.

## Guidance

- If the client blames themself for the event → work on assimilation before broader beliefs. Ask "Did you intend for this to happen? Who intended harm?" Because therapist competence in prioritizing assimilation before over-accommodation predicted greater improvement [Farmer-etal-2017].
- If the client says "I should have known" → separate what they knew then from what they know now. "What did you know at the time? When did you learn the rest?" Because hindsight bias is a core target in session 4 [VA-CSP591-2018].
- If the client is reading the written account and cries → stay quiet and let the emotion run, then ask what they felt. Because the protocol asks therapists not to interrupt and to let natural emotion emerge [VA-CSP591-2018].
- If worksheets stop → complete one together in session and ask: "What came up when you sat down to do it?" Because skipping is often avoidance (expert consensus).
- If scores remit before session 12 → consider ending early with the final session content. Because in variable-length CPT, 58% of completers met end-state criteria before session 12 [Galovski-etal-2012].
- If the client is still above threshold at session 12 → add sessions on remaining stuck points. Because 34% of completers reached end-state criteria between sessions 12 and 18 [Galovski-etal-2012].
- If a crisis hits mid-treatment → use a stressor session to problem-solve, then resume. Because stressor sessions did not reduce outcomes [Galovski-etal-2012]. Do not let crises become a way of stopping the protocol [VA-CSP591-2018].

**Avoid:** "That's not true, it wasn't your fault." Telling rather than asking leaves the stuck point untested. Instead: "Let's look at what you actually knew and could do in that moment."

## Common problems

- **Challenging feelings instead of thoughts.** Help the client tell the thought from the emotion on the ABC worksheet.
- **Arguing.** Socratic questioning works by curiosity; debating hardens stuck points.
- **Starting with trust or esteem themes while self-blame is untouched.**
- **Skipping the impact statement** because it is distressing. It is the map for the whole treatment.

## Evidence

- Women with rape-related PTSD: CPT and PE were both highly effective and better than minimal attention; CPT scored better on 2 of 4 guilt subscales [Resick-etal-2002].
- Dismantling trial in 150 women: full CPT, cognitive therapy only and written accounts only all improved PTSD and depression substantially; the cognitive-only version improved PTSD faster than written accounts alone [Resick-etal-2008]. The written account is now optional [VA-NCPTSD-CPT].
- In 916 veterans, CPT improved PTSD by a standardized mean difference of 0.71 before to after treatment, against 0.99 for PE; the difference was not clinically significant [Schnurr-etal-2022].
- Suicidal ideation fell sharply during CPT and PE, tracking PTSD improvement [Gradus-etal-2013].
- CPT is among the trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapies with the strongest evidence [Lewis-etal-2020]; strong recommendation from the American Psychological Association (APA) and the United States (US) Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025] [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
