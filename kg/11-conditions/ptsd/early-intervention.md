**Bottom line:** Most people recover from early stress reactions, so the first weeks call for practical help, accurate information and active monitoring, not routine therapy. Do not offer single-session psychological debriefing. Offer individual trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) within the first month only to people with acute stress disorder or clinically important posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms, and do not use medication, including benzodiazepines, to prevent PTSD [NICE-NG116].

## The principle

Early reactions such as intrusions, poor sleep, jumpiness and numbness are common and often fade. The United States (US) Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guideline describes acute stress reactions as transient and often resolving with reassurance, rest and safety [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023]. Without treatment, remission was higher in studies starting within 5 months of the trauma (51.7%) than in later-starting studies (36.9%) [Morina-etal-2014]. Intervention for everyone exposed has not helped. Targeted treatment for people with marked symptoms has [Roberts-etal-2019]. The task is to separate the two and follow people up.

## Guidance

- If the person was exposed in the last month and has few or mild symptoms → offer information about common reactions, practical help and a follow-up contact within 1 month (active monitoring). Because the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends considering active monitoring with follow-up within 1 month for subthreshold symptoms [NICE-NG116].
- If an organization asks you to run a group or individual debriefing for everyone involved → decline single-session emotional processing of the event, and offer information, practical support and screening instead. Because NICE says not to offer psychologically focused debriefing [NICE-NG116], and the Cochrane review found it did not prevent PTSD; one trial found higher PTSD risk at 1 year (odds ratio 2.88) [Rose-etal-2002].
- If the person meets criteria for acute stress disorder or has clinically important PTSD symptoms within the first month → offer individual trauma-focused CBT, such as cognitive processing therapy, cognitive therapy for PTSD, narrative exposure therapy or prolonged exposure. Because NICE recommends offering it [NICE-NG116], and VA/DoD suggests trauma-focused CBT for acute stress disorder (weak for) [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
- If the person has no acute stress disorder but symptoms are rising → do not reassure them that they are at low risk; keep monitoring. Because acute stress disorder fails to identify most people who later develop PTSD [Bryant-etal-2011].
- If someone requests medication to prevent PTSD → explain that no drug, including benzodiazepines, is recommended for prevention and discuss support and monitoring instead. Because NICE advises against drug prevention [NICE-NG116] and VA/DoD found insufficient evidence for any drug [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
- If a major disaster affected many people → plan a validated brief screen at about 1 month. Because NICE advises routine screening at 1 month for people at high risk after a disaster [NICE-NG116].
- If a child was involved → tell parents about normal reactions and to contact their doctor if symptoms last beyond 1 month. Consider active monitoring or individual trauma-focused CBT within 1 month for acute stress disorder or marked symptoms, and group trauma-focused CBT (ages 7 to 17, typically 5 to 15 sessions) after large-scale shared trauma. Because these are NICE recommendations [NICE-NG116].
- If the person reports suicidal thoughts or intent → assess risk now (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`); for immediate danger, in the US call or text 988 or call 911, and in the UK call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because acute risk overrides watchful waiting (expert consensus).
- If the person is still in danger, injured or without basic needs → address safety, medical care, shelter and contact with loved ones first. Because survival, safety and security come first in acute care [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].

## Example phrasings

- "What you're feeling, the flashbacks, the jumpiness, not sleeping, is a common reaction to something terrible. For many people it eases over the next few weeks. I'd like to check in with you in a couple of weeks to see how it's going."
- "You don't have to talk about what happened unless you want to. What would help most right now?"
- "If the memories are still taking over in a month, or getting worse, there is an effective treatment, and I'll help you get it."
- Avoid: "You need to talk it all through now or it will get buried." There is no evidence for this, and pressing early disclosure can harm.

## Evidence

- Early multi-session interventions showed no clinically important benefit over usual care for people not screened for symptoms. For people with symptoms, trauma-focused CBT, cognitive therapy without exposure and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) helped, most clearly for acute stress disorder and PTSD [Roberts-etal-2019].
- Early trauma-focused CBT had a moderate effect on later PTSD diagnosis (effect size 0.54), shrinking to 0.34 at long-term follow-up [Kliem-Kroger-2013].
- Evidence for psychological first aid style support rests mainly on expert consensus; the recommendations above against debriefing and for targeted CBT rest on trials and guidelines.
