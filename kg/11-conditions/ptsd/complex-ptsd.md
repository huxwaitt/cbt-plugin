**Bottom line:** In the International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11), posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) becomes complex PTSD when severe, persistent disturbances in emotion regulation, self-concept and relationships are also present. Most evidence now shows that standard trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and related treatments work for these clients and that a mandatory stabilization phase is not required. Add skills, time and practical support where they help engagement, but start trauma-focused work early and set a limit on any preparatory phase.

## Diagnostic essentials

- **Event:** extremely threatening or horrific, most often prolonged or repeated and hard to escape, such as torture, slavery, prolonged domestic violence or repeated childhood abuse [WHO-ICD11-2022].
- **Requirements:** all ICD-11 PTSD requirements (re-experiencing in the present, avoidance, sense of current threat) plus three "disturbances in self-organization": severe problems in affect regulation; persistent beliefs of being diminished, defeated or worthless with shame, guilt or failure; and persistent difficulty sustaining relationships and feeling close to others. Symptoms cause significant impairment [WHO-ICD11-2022].
- **One or the other:** complex PTSD excludes a PTSD diagnosis [WHO-ICD11-2022].
- **United States (US) diagnosis:** the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) has no complex PTSD diagnosis. The United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) notes that DSM-5 covers complexity through its broader negative mood and cognition symptoms and the dissociative subtype [NICE-NG116]. Code PTSD and any comorbid conditions.
- **Measure:** the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ), free, scored by its diagnostic algorithm rather than cutoffs [ITQ-Site].

## Course and prevalence

- In a US population sample, 3.4% met ICD-11 PTSD and 3.8% complex PTSD. Cumulative childhood trauma, especially abuse by caregivers, was more strongly associated with complex PTSD, which carried a higher psychiatric burden [Cloitre-etal-2019].
- In a trial of childhood abuse-related PTSD, 54% met complex PTSD criteria at baseline [Hoeboer-etal-2021].

## Differential and comorbidity

- **Borderline personality disorder (BPD):** in a latent class analysis of women with childhood abuse, frantic efforts to avoid abandonment, an unstable sense of self, unstable intense relationships and impulsiveness separated BPD from complex PTSD [Cloitre-etal-2014]. World Health Organization (WHO) guidance adds that complex PTSD shows a stable but negative self-view and avoidance of closeness rather than fear of abandonment, and that a personality disorder may also be diagnosed when both sets of requirements are met [WHO-CDDR-2024].
- **Depression, dissociative disorders, substance use, self-harm:** common; screen for each.

## CBT approach in brief

**The phased position.** In a 2011 survey by the International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ISTSS), 84% of experts endorsed phase-based or sequenced therapy for complex PTSD [Cloitre-etal-2011]. In a trial of women with childhood abuse PTSD, skills training in affective and interpersonal regulation (STAIR) followed by exposure gave more sustained remission (27%) than supportive counseling followed by exposure (13%), with fewer cases of worsening [Cloitre-etal-2010].

**The direct position.** A critical review found no rigorous evidence that a phase-based approach is necessary, that trauma-focused treatment carries unacceptable risk for these clients, or that a stabilization phase improves results; it warned that guidelines risk delaying effective care [DeJongh-etal-2016].

**What newer evidence shows:**
- CBT, exposure and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) improved PTSD symptoms, negative self-concept and relationship problems in samples likely to have complex PTSD features. Childhood-onset trauma predicted poorer outcome [Karatzias-etal-2019].
- After complex traumatic events, trauma-focused interventions beat non-trauma-focused ones. Phase-based interventions combining skills and trauma focus were most promising for emotion dysregulation and interpersonal problems [Coventry-etal-2020].
- Prolonged exposure, intensified prolonged exposure and STAIR plus prolonged exposure all produced large gains (Cohen's d above 1.6) with similar dropout; the skills phase added no benefit [Oprel-etal-2021]. Complex PTSD status did not predict or moderate outcome [Hoeboer-etal-2021]. A second trial (96 adults) found that 8 STAIR sessions before prolonged exposure did not improve emotion regulation more than prolonged exposure alone [Oprel-etal-2026].
- A 2026 meta-analysis of few trials found no difference between phase-based and non-phase-based treatment on most outcomes; multi-phase designs did better on PTSD, and phase-based, multi-phase and exposure designs on affect regulation [Lee-etal-2026].
- The American Psychological Association (APA) guideline states that complex presentations may be addressed effectively with first-line trauma-focused treatments [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025].

**Practical synthesis:**
- If a client has complex PTSD features → offer a first-line trauma-focused treatment with extra sessions to build trust, attention to housing and personal safety, help with barriers such as substance misuse, dissociation, emotion dysregulation and negative self-perception, and planned ongoing support. Because these are the adjustments NICE recommends for complex PTSD [NICE-NG116].
- If dysregulation repeatedly blocks sessions, or the client prefers it → add a brief, time-limited skills module, such as 8 sessions of STAIR, with a set date to begin memory work. Because a skills phase of that length was well tolerated, though it did not improve outcomes over exposure alone [Oprel-etal-2021].
- If the client is in current danger or acute crisis → address safety first, then return to the trauma plan. Because that is risk management, not stabilization for its own sake (expert consensus).

## Risk notes

- Screen for self-harm, suicidal ideation, revictimization and current abusive relationships at intake and regularly (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`, `02-risk.safeguarding`).
- WHO notes suicidal behavior in complex PTSD tends to be less frequent and less impulsive than in personality disorder but more serious when it occurs [WHO-CDDR-2024]. Infrequent ideation is not a reason to stop asking; restrict access to lethal means when risk rises.
- Immediate danger: in the US call or text 988 or call 911; in the UK call 999, the National Health Service (NHS) 111 line, or Samaritans on 116 123.
