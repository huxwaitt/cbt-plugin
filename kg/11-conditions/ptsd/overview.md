**Bottom line:** Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a persistent reaction to an extremely threatening event, built around intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, negative changes in thinking and mood, and heightened arousal lasting more than a month. About 6% of United States (US) adults meet criteria at some point [Goldstein-etal-2016]. Many recover in the first months, but a large minority stay unwell for years [Morina-etal-2014], so offer trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) rather than waiting.

## Diagnostic essentials

Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) requirements, paraphrased as counts [APA-DSM5TR-2022] [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023]:

| Criterion | Requirement |
|---|---|
| A. Exposure | Actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence: experienced, witnessed in person, learned about for a close family member or friend (violent or accidental if a death), or repeated or extreme exposure to aversive details at work. Media exposure counts only when it is work-related. |
| B. Intrusion | At least 1 of 5 (memories, dreams, flashbacks, distress at cues, physical reactions to cues) |
| C. Avoidance | At least 1 of 2 (internal reminders, external reminders) |
| D. Negative cognitions and mood | At least 2 of 7 (amnesia for parts of the event, exaggerated negative beliefs, distorted blame, persistent negative emotion, loss of interest, detachment, inability to feel positive emotions) |
| E. Arousal and reactivity | At least 2 of 6 (irritability or anger, reckless or self-destructive behavior, hypervigilance, startle, poor concentration, sleep disturbance) |
| F. Duration | More than 1 month |
| G. Impairment | Clinically significant distress or impairment |
| H. Exclusion | Not due to a substance or another medical condition |

- **Specifiers:** with dissociative symptoms (persistent depersonalization or derealization), and with delayed expression (full criteria not met until at least 6 months after the event) [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
- **Children 6 years and younger:** a separate set needing 1 intrusion symptom, 1 avoidance or negative cognition symptom and 2 arousal symptoms [Mikolajewski-etal-2017].
- **Acute stress disorder:** 9 or more of 14 symptoms across intrusion, negative mood, dissociation, avoidance and arousal, lasting 3 days to 1 month after the event [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
- **Subthreshold PTSD:** coded as other specified trauma- and stressor-related disorder; DSM-5-TR gives no standard criteria for it [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].

**International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11).** PTSD has three core elements: re-experiencing the event in the present (vivid intrusive memories, flashbacks or nightmares with strong emotion), avoidance, and a persistent sense of current threat such as hypervigilance or enhanced startle. Symptoms last at least several weeks and cause significant impairment [WHO-ICD11-2022]. Blame, mood and numbing symptoms are not required, so ICD-11 PTSD is usually less prevalent than DSM-5-TR PTSD, and the two systems identify partly different people [Brewin-etal-2017]. ICD-11 adds complex PTSD: all PTSD requirements plus severe, persistent problems with affect regulation, self-concept and relationships. A person receives one of the two diagnoses, not both [WHO-ICD11-2022]. DSM-5-TR has no complex PTSD diagnosis.

## Course and prevalence

- 70.4% of adults across 24 countries reported at least one lifetime trauma [Kessler-etal-2017].
- US adults, using the 2013 DSM-5 criteria: lifetime PTSD 6.1% and past-year 4.7%; lifetime rates were 8.0% in women and 4.1% in men [Goldstein-etal-2016] [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
- Cross-national lifetime prevalence is 3.9%, or 5.6% among people exposed to trauma, and about half of cases are persistent [Koenen-etal-2017].
- Interpersonal violence carries the highest risk; rape, other sexual assault, stalking and unexpected bereavement carry the most PTSD burden [Kessler-etal-2017].
- Without specific treatment, an average of 44% no longer meet criteria after at least 10 months (range 8% to 89%). Remission is higher when the baseline falls within 5 months of the trauma (51.7%) than later (36.9%) [Morina-etal-2014].

## Differential and comorbidity

- **Adjustment disorder:** the stressor does not meet Criterion A, or the symptoms do not fit the PTSD pattern.
- **Prolonged grief disorder:** longing for or preoccupation with a person who died at least 12 months earlier in adults (6 months in children and adolescents), with at least 3 of 8 symptoms [APA-PGD-Site]. A violent death can cause both, but only PTSD involves reliving the death as happening now [WHO-CDDR-2024].
- **Depression, generalized anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder:** PTSD intrusions are memories of a real past event; worry and obsessions concern feared possibilities.
- **Psychosis:** trauma-related voices or flashbacks can look like hallucinations. Ask whether the experience replays the event.
- **Borderline personality disorder and complex PTSD:** see `ptsd.complex-ptsd`.
- **Head injury, intoxication, medical causes:** gaps in memory may be organic rather than dissociative, and Criterion H excludes substance and medical causes.
- **Comorbidity is common.** The United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises treating PTSD first when depression coexists, unless depression is severe enough to block trauma work or raises risk, and not excluding people because of drug or alcohol misuse [NICE-NG116].

## CBT approach in brief

Formulate with a trauma-focused model (`ptsd.cbt-model`), then offer individual trauma-focused CBT, such as cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure or cognitive therapy for PTSD, more than 1 month after the trauma [NICE-NG116].

## Risk notes

- PTSD is associated with past suicide attempts and with current suicidal ideation; the link weakens but remains after controlling for depression [Krysinska-Lester-2010]. Ask about suicidal thoughts at assessment and during treatment (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`).
- Ask about ongoing danger, such as living with an abusive partner, and about reckless behavior and anger toward others. Where risk to self or others is significant, a risk management and safety plan belongs in initial treatment planning [NICE-NG116].
- Immediate danger: in the US call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911; in the UK call 999, the National Health Service (NHS) 111 line, or Samaritans on 116 123.
