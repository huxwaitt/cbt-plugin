**Bottom line:** Prolonged exposure (PE) helps people with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) stop avoiding by repeatedly revisiting the trauma memory aloud (imaginal exposure) and approaching safe but avoided situations (in vivo exposure), then talking through what they learned. It usually takes 8 to 15 weekly sessions of 60 to 90 minutes [VA-NCPTSD-PE] and is a first-line, strongly recommended treatment [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025] [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].

## When to use

- Adults with PTSD from any trauma type.
- Particularly when avoidance is broad, the memory is fragmented, or the client says "I can't think about it."
- The manual is Foa and colleagues' therapist guide, second edition [Foa-etal-2019].

## How it works

Emotional processing theory holds that PTSD keeps a fear structure linked to two beliefs: the world is utterly dangerous, and I am incompetent. Approaching the memory and reminders without escape gives corrective information, organizes the memory, and shows the client they can cope [VA-CSP591-2018]. The inhibitory learning view adds that learning is strongest when expectations are violated, not merely when fear falls [Craske-etal-2014]. In practice, ask "What did you expect would happen? What happened?" after each exposure.

## Steps

Session content from the United States (US) Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) comparative trial protocol, which used the 12-session form with 90-minute sessions [VA-CSP591-2018]:

1. **Session 1:** overview and rationale, a structured interview about the trauma and its effects, and breathing retraining (slow, even breathing). Homework: breathing daily, listen to the session recording, read the rationale handout.
2. **Session 2:** common reactions to trauma, the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS, 0 to 100), and the in vivo hierarchy of avoided situations. Homework: first in vivo exercises.
3. **Session 3:** imaginal exposure begins. The client recounts the trauma aloud, in the present tense, as vividly as possible, repeating it for the allotted time (60 minutes in that protocol; a 2023 trial used about 40 minutes within 90-minute sessions [Sloan-etal-2023]). The therapist rates SUDS about every 5 minutes. Then **processing**: talk through thoughts, feelings and remembered details. Homework: listen to the imaginal recording daily, plus in vivo practice.
4. **Sessions 4 to the session before last:** imaginal exposure, then processing. The therapist identifies the most distressing parts and focuses repeated exposure on these **hot spots**. In vivo items move up the hierarchy.
5. **Final session:** a shorter imaginal exposure (30 minutes), review of SUDS and symptom change, discussion of how to manage temporary symptom rises, and relapse prevention.

## Guidance

- If the client is under-engaged (flat, detached, skipping details) → ask for present tense, eyes closed if comfortable, and sensory detail. "What do you see right now? What are you feeling in your body?" Because the protocol asks for vivid, present-tense recounting to engage the memory [VA-CSP591-2018].
- If the client is over-engaged (dissociating, losing contact with the room) → keep eyes open, switch to past tense, write it instead of speaking, and add grounding. "You're here with me in the office. Tell me what happened next." Because the aim is engagement with the memory while knowing it is the past (expert consensus).
- If SUDS stay high at the end of an imaginal exposure → process anyway and repeat next session. Because learning can occur without within-session fear reduction [Craske-etal-2014].
- If the client skips the recording homework → explore the prediction behind it ("If I listen, I'll fall apart"), and test it with a short, scheduled listen. Because daily listening is a core component [VA-CSP591-2018].
- If symptoms rise after the first imaginal sessions → normalize and continue. Because exacerbations affected 20% of PE clients in one sample, were not linked to dropout, and those clients still improved [Larsen-etal-2016].
- If the client needs a fast course → consider massed PE. Because 10 sessions over 2 weeks were noninferior to 10 sessions over 8 weeks in active duty personnel [Foa-etal-2018].

**Avoid:** "We can skip the worst part for now." Instead: "That's the part that keeps coming back. Let's slow down and go through it together."

## Common problems

- **Therapist avoidance:** cutting imaginal time, steering away from hot spots, or letting processing become general support.
- **Safety behaviors during in vivo exposure:** carrying a phone in hand, a companion, staying near exits. Plan to drop them (`behavioral.safety-behavior-reduction`).
- **Hierarchy stalls at middle items.** Vary contexts and add items rather than repeating easy ones.
- **Exposure to realistic danger.** In vivo items must be objectively safe, never contact with an abuser.

## Evidence

- A meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials (675 participants) found a large effect for PE against control (Hedges' g 1.08) and no difference from other active treatments [Powers-etal-2010].
- A wider meta-analysis of 65 trials (4,929 patients) found large effects for exposure therapy against waitlist and usual care, a small effect against non-trauma-focused comparators, and negligible differences against other trauma-focused treatments [McLean-etal-2022].
- In 916 veterans, PE was statistically, but not clinically, more effective than cognitive processing therapy, and dropout was higher with PE (55.8% versus 46.6%) [Schnurr-etal-2022].
- The American Psychological Association (APA) guideline lists PE at 9 to 21 sessions across reviewed studies [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025].
