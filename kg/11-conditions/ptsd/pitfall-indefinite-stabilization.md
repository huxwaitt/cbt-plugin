**Bottom line:** In posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) treatment, a preparatory skills phase can help some clients, but when it has no end date it becomes a way of avoiding effective treatment. Trials show that people with complex presentations, dissociation and even psychosis benefit from trauma-focused therapy without a long stabilization phase. Set a time limit, name the trauma-focused plan from the start, and move to memory work.

## What it looks like

- Session 15 and the client has learned grounding, breathing and distress tolerance, but has never described the index event in therapy.
- Each week a new crisis or a "bad week" is the reason to wait.
- The therapist's notes say "not yet stable enough for trauma work" with no criteria for what stable means.
- PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) scores are unchanged or rising, and the client says "I don't think anything can fix this."

## Why it happens

- Older consensus favored phased treatment for complex PTSD, and many clinicians were trained that way.
- Therapists fear making the client worse or causing dropout, and clients' fear of the memory meets the therapist's own avoidance.
- "Stability" is rarely defined, so it can always be one more session away.
- A critical review found no rigorous evidence that a stabilization phase is necessary, that trauma-focused treatment carries unacceptable risk for these clients, or that a preparatory phase improves outcome. Its authors warned that phase-first guidelines risk denying or delaying effective care [DeJongh-etal-2016].

## The fix

- If you plan any preparatory work → state its length in advance (for example 2 to 4 sessions, or a brief manualized module such as 8 sessions of skills training) and a date for memory work. Because adding 8 skills sessions before prolonged exposure did not improve outcomes over prolonged exposure alone in childhood abuse PTSD [Oprel-etal-2021].
- If the client meets complex PTSD criteria → do not treat that alone as a reason to delay. Because complex PTSD did not predict or moderate outcome of three exposure-based variants [Hoeboer-etal-2021], and the American Psychological Association (APA) guideline notes complex presentations may be treated effectively with first-line trauma-focused interventions [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025].
- If dissociation is the concern → adapt memory work (eyes open, written narrative, grounding) instead of postponing. Because dissociation did not moderate psychotherapy outcome [Hoeboer-etal-2020].
- If comorbid psychosis or substance use is the concern → proceed with monitoring. Because standard prolonged exposure and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) worked without stabilization in psychotic disorders [VanDenBerg-etal-2015], and the United States (US) Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guideline suggests comorbidity should not preclude trauma-focused therapy [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
- If something genuinely blocks trauma work (imminent suicide risk, living with an abuser, medically dangerous withdrawal) → name it as a safety problem with a specific plan and review date. Because the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks clinicians to address barriers to engaging in trauma-focused therapy, not to replace it [NICE-NG116].
- Say it plainly: "The skills you've learned will help you do the memory work. The memory work is what makes the flashbacks lose their power. Let's set a date."

## Sources note

The case against open-ended stabilization rests on a critical review [DeJongh-etal-2016] and randomized trials in childhood abuse PTSD and psychosis [Oprel-etal-2021] [VanDenBerg-etal-2015]. Evidence is not one-sided: a 2026 meta-analysis found no difference between phase-based and non-phase-based treatment on most outcomes, but some advantage for multi-phase or exposure designs on PTSD or affect regulation [Lee-etal-2026]. The fix therefore keeps brief, planned skills work and rejects only the open-ended version.
