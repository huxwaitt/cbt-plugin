**Bottom line:** Cognitive therapy for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), abbreviated CT-PTSD, applies the Ehlers and Clark model directly. It aims to reduce the sense of current threat by updating the trauma memory at its worst moments, changing the appraisals that make the trauma and its aftermath feel threatening, and dropping the strategies that maintain both, while the client reclaims their life [Ehlers-Clark-2000] [Wild-etal-2020]. Standard delivery is about 12 weekly sessions, with 90 minutes for memory sessions, plus up to 3 optional monthly boosters [Ehlers-etal-2014] [Wild-etal-2020].

## When to use

- Adults with PTSD after single or multiple traumas; the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) names it among the trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapies to offer [NICE-NG116].
- Especially suited to strong "nowness" of intrusions, shame, guilt, anger, a sense of permanent change, or heavy safety behaviors.
- Can be delivered intensively over about a week [Ehlers-etal-2014]; its developers describe how to deliver it by video or telephone [Wild-etal-2020].

## How it works

The memory is updated with information the client did not have at the time ("I didn't die", "He was arrested", "It wasn't my fault"), which places it in the past. Triggers are discriminated from the trauma so they stop firing the memory. Appraisals are tested with Socratic questioning and experiments. Dropping suppression, rumination and safety behaviors lets the client learn the threat is over [Wild-etal-2020].

## Steps

1. **Formulate together** around current threat, using an intrusion diary and process questionnaires, and draw the maintenance cycles [Wild-etal-2020].
2. **Reclaim your life** from session 1: plan activities the trauma took away, framed as experiments on beliefs about permanent change, and review them every session [Wild-etal-2020].
3. **Memory work:** imaginal reliving or a written narrative. Choose narrative writing for prolonged trauma, dissociation or telephone work, because it is easier to pace [Wild-etal-2020].
4. **Identify hot spots,** the moments with the most distress and "nowness", and the meaning of each ("I'm going to die").
5. **Update hot spots** in three steps: find the threatening meaning, find the updating information (what happened next, or what the client now knows), and bring that information in while the client holds the hot spot in mind. Imagery, movement or incompatible sensations can strengthen it. Update as early as possible, one hot spot at a time [Wild-etal-2020].
6. **Then versus now discrimination:** list triggers, write how the trigger now differs from the trauma then, and practice with real or recorded cues while attending to the differences [Wild-etal-2020].
7. **Site visit,** planned in advance, to the trauma location, ideally with support, looking for then-versus-now differences. Virtual visits through street-view images are an alternative [Wild-etal-2020].
8. **Change appraisals** of guilt, shame, anger, loss and permanent change with Socratic questioning, surveys, pie charts, behavioral experiments, imagery and positive data logs [Wild-etal-2020]. Match the tool to the appraisal.
9. **Drop maintaining strategies:** experiments reducing checking and other safety behaviors, a thought suppression demonstration, and labeling rumination and switching away from it [Wild-etal-2020].
10. **Blueprint:** a written plan answering how the problem developed, what kept it going, what helped, the key old and updated thoughts, and how to handle setbacks [Wild-etal-2020].

## Guidance

- If a hot spot's meaning is "I'm going to die" → update with "I survived; I'm here now", and have the client feel their body in the chair or look at a current photo of themselves while holding the moment in mind. Because linking corrective information to the hot spot is the core of memory updating [Wild-etal-2020].
- If intrusions come "out of the blue" → keep an intrusion diary and search for sensory triggers. Because strong perceptual priming makes triggers easy to miss [Ehlers-Clark-2000].
- If the client says "I'm not the same person anymore" → set a reclaiming-life experiment. "What is one thing the old you did that we could test this week?" Because these assignments test beliefs about permanent change [Wild-etal-2020].
- If shame blocks disclosure → run a survey of what others would think, with the client's consent. Because a survey tests what others actually think, and surveys are among the protocol's restructuring tools [Wild-etal-2020].
- If the client is at risk of suicide → screen at the start, monitor each session (for example Patient Health Questionnaire-9 item 9), and follow `02-risk.crisis-response` if risk rises. Because the protocol builds in risk screening and session-by-session monitoring [Wild-etal-2020].

## Common problems

- **Talking about the trauma without reliving it.** Updating needs the hot spot activated in memory.
- **Updating with reassurance** ("You did your best") rather than information the client discovers.
- **Forgetting reclaiming life** once memory work starts.

## Evidence

- First randomized trial (28 patients): large effects against a 3-month waitlist (controlled effect sizes 2.25 self-report and 2.18 assessor-rated), dropout 3% across the trial and a case series, and better outcome linked to greater change in posttraumatic cognitions [Ehlers-etal-2005].
- In 121 adults with chronic PTSD, 73% recovered with 7-day intensive therapy, 77% with standard weekly therapy, 43% with emotion-focused supportive therapy and 7% on a waitlist [Ehlers-etal-2014].
- A meta-analysis placed CT-PTSD with cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure as the trauma-focused treatments with the strongest evidence [Lewis-etal-2020].
- Guideline status differs: NICE lists it for first-line offer [NICE-NG116]; the American Psychological Association (APA) gives a conditional recommendation [APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025]; the United States (US) Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guideline downgraded it to weak for, because it rated specific named therapies and found only 2 trials with clinician-rated outcomes [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].
