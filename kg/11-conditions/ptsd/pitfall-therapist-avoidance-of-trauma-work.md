**Bottom line:** Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is maintained by avoidance, and therapists avoid too. When the therapist steers away from the memory, accepts every crisis as a reason to skip the protocol, or softens the hardest moments, the client learns that the memory is too dangerous to touch. Trauma-focused treatment is safe; temporary symptom rises are expected. Plan the memory work, protect its time, and use supervision to spot your own avoidance.

## What it looks like

- The session agenda fills with the week's stressors, and memory work is "next week".
- Imaginal exposure or the written account is cut short when the client becomes upset.
- The worst moment is summarized ("and then it happened") and never slowed down.
- Processing turns into reassurance ("You did nothing wrong") instead of questions.
- Homework recordings or worksheets go unreviewed.

## Why it happens

- Clinicians fear harm. In a survey sent to 852 licensed psychologists, only a small minority reported using imaginal exposure for PTSD, and even trained clinicians reported many perceived barriers [Becker-etal-2004].
- Distress in the room feels like evidence of damage.
- Crises are real, and attending to them feels kinder than returning to the protocol.
- Yet exacerbations during prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy affected only a minority, did not predict dropout, and those clients still improved clinically [Larsen-etal-2016]. The United States (US) Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guideline judged harms of trauma-focused therapy low and any symptom rise typically transitory [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023].

## The fix

- If a crisis arrives → give it a set part of the session or one planned "stressor session", then return to the protocol. Because trial therapists were told not to collude with avoidance by stopping the protocol, while still attending to emergencies [VA-CSP591-2018].
- If the client becomes very distressed during memory work → stay with it, slow down and ground if needed, then continue. "This is the hard part. You're doing it. Stay with me, and tell me what happens next." Because the protocol asks for repeated, full engagement with the most distressing parts [VA-CSP591-2018].
- If you notice you are skipping hot spots → put "hot spot work" on the written agenda and time it. Because elaborating the memory and processing its worst moments is a core component of trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [NICE-NG116] [Wild-etal-2020].
- If you find yourself reassuring → switch to a question. Because competence in Socratic questioning predicted better outcome in cognitive processing therapy [Farmer-etal-2017].
- If you dread a session → take a recording to supervision and rate your adherence. Because therapist avoidance is easier to see from outside (expert consensus).
- Avoid: "Maybe we shouldn't go there today." Instead: "I know part of you wants to stop, and that makes sense. Stopping is what keeps the memory powerful. Let's do a shorter round and see what you notice."

## Sources note

Underuse of exposure comes from survey data [Becker-etal-2004]. Safety of trauma-focused treatment rests on trial data on symptom exacerbation [Larsen-etal-2016] and guideline review [VA-DoD-PTSD-CPG-2023]. The supervision advice is expert consensus.
