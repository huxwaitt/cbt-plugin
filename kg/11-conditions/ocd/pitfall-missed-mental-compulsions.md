**Bottom line:** Exposure and response prevention (ERP) fails quietly when rituals move out of sight: mental reviewing, praying, counting, replacing an image, telling oneself "it's just OCD," or drifting attention away. Covert compulsions are harder to resist or monitor than overt ones [Veale-2007], and mishandling them is a recognized therapist error [Gillihan-etal-2012]. Before deciding obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is "treatment resistant," hunt for hidden neutralizing.

## What it looks like

- Exposures are "fine," with low distress from the start or a sudden drop within seconds.
- The client goes quiet, closes their eyes or moves their lips during exposure.
- Overt washing stops, but wipes, sleeves, elbows or "careful" touching appear.
- Rituals are delayed ("I'll check later") rather than dropped.
- Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) scores stall short of the 35% reduction that defines response [Mataix-Cols-etal-2016].
- Hierarchy items never reach the core fear.

## Why it happens

- Mental rituals are invisible and the client may not see them as rituals.
- Clients learn to use calming self-talk, distraction or relaxation as "coping," which then neutralizes [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- Therapists, uncomfortable with distress, allow distraction or stop below the top of the hierarchy [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- A delayed or substituted ritual keeps the "danger, then neutralize" link alive and blocks expectancy violation [Craske-etal-2014].

## The fix

- If distress falls fast or stays low during exposure → ask, "What went on in your mind just then? Did you say anything to yourself or picture anything?" Because obsessions raise distress and rituals lower it, which flags covert neutralizing [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If a mental ritual is found → add it to the written response prevention rules and block it with an exposure statement or by reading the imaginal script aloud. Because these methods leave no room for covert rituals [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If a ritual slips through → "spoil" it by re-exposing to the trigger straight away. Because this undoes the neutralizing [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If the client chats or looks away during exposure → bring attention back to the trigger and the feared consequence. Because distraction weakens exposure and can be credited for safety [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If items stop below the core fear → design steps that test it directly. Because not going far enough is a common error [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If homework adherence is low → shrink and specify tasks, and review them first. Because low adherence to between-session exposures predicts higher symptom severity [Hezel-Simpson-2019].

Example phrasings:
- "Some rituals happen silently in your head. Let's make a list of yours so we can include them."
- "Instead of telling yourself it's fine, try saying 'Maybe I did, maybe I didn't' and let that sit."
- Avoid: "Just relax and think of something else while you touch it."

## Sources note

The list of therapist errors, the heuristic for spotting mental rituals and the blocking methods come from a published review by the Foa group [Gillihan-etal-2012]. Veale notes that covert compulsions are harder to resist or monitor [Veale-2007]. The adherence finding is from a review of ERP outcomes [Hezel-Simpson-2019]. The specific questions used to detect rituals are expert consensus.
