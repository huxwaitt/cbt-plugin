**Bottom line:** Exposure and response prevention (ERP) for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) means planned, repeated contact with the triggers and feared consequences of the client's core fear while all rituals, including mental ones and reassurance, are dropped. Stay with the exposure, block distraction and reassurance, and aim the hierarchy at the core fear [Gillihan-etal-2012]. Add cognitive work on inflated responsibility when the client's belief in harm or blame stays high [Salkovskis-1999].

## When to use

After assessment and a shared formulation, for any OCD theme. Combine in vivo and imaginal methods. Use cognitive techniques alongside ERP, or as the main method if the client will not do ERP, since outcomes were similar (effect 0.07) [Ost-etal-2015].

## How it works

Each exposure is a test: the client predicts what will happen, stays in contact without neutralizing, and finds out. The inhibitory learning model names eight ways to strengthen this: expectancy violation, deepened extinction, occasional reinforced extinction, removal of safety signals, variability, retrieval cues, multiple contexts and affect labeling [Craske-etal-2014]. The goal is tolerance of distress and uncertainty, not zero anxiety [Hezel-Simpson-2019].

## Steps

1. **Give the rationale with the client's own cycle.** "Every time you wash, you get relief, and you never find out what would have happened. We'll do the opposite on purpose, a step at a time, so your brain gets new information."
2. **Find the core fear.** Ask what links the obsessions: being responsible for harm, being bad, going to hell, losing control. Many surface fears share one core fear [Gillihan-etal-2012].
3. **Build the hierarchy** with distress ratings (0 to 100) and the feared outcome for each item. Include items beyond what people without OCD do, but nothing truly dangerous [Gillihan-etal-2012].
4. **Write the response prevention rules.** Which rituals stop, from when, what counts as a ritual (including "just once more," wipes instead of washing, mental reviewing), and what to do with the urge. In the Simpson protocol clients were asked to try refraining from rituals after the first exposure session [Simpson-etal-2013].
5. **Run the in-session exposure** (often 90 minutes in trials [Simpson-etal-2013]):
   - Name the prediction and rate belief: "What do you expect will happen, and how sure are you?"
   - Prefer in vivo exposure whenever possible. Use imaginal exposure when the feared outcome is distant, internal or cannot be staged, such as going to hell or becoming a different person [Gillihan-etal-2012].
   - Keep attention on the trigger; light chat is acceptable, but distraction or mental distancing is not [Gillihan-etal-2012].
   - Vary stimuli, places and order, and drop safety signals such as the therapist's presence over time [Craske-etal-2014].
6. **Imaginal script.** Write a detailed first-person story in which the catastrophe follows from not doing the ritual. Read or record it, check distress at points, and use the recording for daily practice [Gillihan-etal-2012].
7. **Block mental rituals.** Have the client read the script aloud, use exposure statements in place of a neutralizing phrase ("I might have caused the accident"), and "spoil" a slipped ritual by re-exposing at once [Gillihan-etal-2012].
8. **Process afterward.** "What did you predict? What happened? What did you learn about the fear and about coping?"
9. **Set homework.** Daily self-directed exposure; the Simpson protocol asked for at least 1 hour a day, with phone check-ins and at least 2 sessions outside the clinic [Simpson-etal-2013].

**Cognitive techniques for responsibility.** Target overestimated danger and inflated personal responsibility [VanOppen-Arntz-1994] [Salkovskis-1999]:
- **Theory A versus Theory B:** "I am dangerous" against "I worry a lot about being dangerous because I care." Live as if B is true for a set period, then review [Veale-2007].
- **Responsibility pie chart:** list everyone and everything that would contribute to the feared event, give each a slice, then shade the client's share last (expert consensus technique).
- **Double standard:** "If your friend had this thought, would you hold them responsible?"
- **Thought-action fusion experiments:** test whether thinking about an event makes it happen [Shafran-etal-1996].
- **Suppression experiment:** record how often a neutral thought comes with and without trying to suppress it [Veale-2007].

## Guidance

- If the client refuses an item → ask what makes it hard, revisit the rationale and costs, then shrink the step (one fingertip, not a whole hand) rather than dropping it. Because therapists who back away leave the core fear untested [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If the client asks "What are the chances?" → do not debate probabilities; return to the experiment. Because Veale advises against arguing about the likelihood of a bad event, which turns into reassurance [Veale-2007].
- If the client says "I'll just tell myself it's OCD" → use an exposure statement instead. Because the label can become a reassurance ritual [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If belief in responsibility stays high after several exposures → add a pie chart or Theory A versus Theory B session. Because cognitive change can unlock harder exposures (expert consensus; no validated threshold).
- If progress stalls at the top of the hierarchy → move from therapist-guided to solo practice in varied settings. Because removing safety signals and varying contexts strengthens learning [Craske-etal-2014].

## Common problems

Substituted rituals, delayed rather than dropped rituals, and covert reassurance through searching online or confessing. Treat each as a ritual and add it to the rules.

## Evidence

ERP is supported by many randomized controlled trials and is guideline-recommended (see `ocd.treatment`). The inhibitory learning tactics rest on laboratory and translational evidence rather than OCD-specific trials of each tactic [Craske-etal-2014]. The pie chart and double standard are expert consensus techniques within cognitive therapy for OCD.
