**Bottom line:** Ask directly about washing, checking, unwanted thoughts, slowness and order, because people with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) rarely volunteer them. Then map each obsession to its feared consequence and to every way the client neutralizes it, including mental acts, avoidance and reassurance. Rate severity with the clinician-rated Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) at baseline and track a self-report weekly [Storch-etal-2015] [Mataix-Cols-etal-2016].

## When to use

- A client describes rituals, intrusive thoughts or long delays doing ordinary tasks.
- Screening groups at higher risk: people with symptoms of depression, anxiety, alcohol or substance misuse, body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) or an eating disorder, and people attending dermatology clinics. The United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises asking direct questions in these groups [NICE-CG31].
- Before exposure and response prevention (ERP) starts, and at the end of treatment.

## Steps

1. **Screen.** Ask about the main symptom areas in plain words: "Do you wash or clean a lot? Check things over and over? Have thoughts that keep bothering you that you can't get rid of? Take a long time to finish everyday things? Get very upset by mess or things out of order? Do these problems trouble you?" [NICE-CG31]. A self-report such as the Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R; 18 items, 6 subscales) [Foa-etal-2002] or the Dimensional Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (DOCS; 20 items, 4 symptom dimensions) [Abramowitz-etal-2010] can screen and track.
2. **Normalize before asking about content.** In a study of 777 students in 13 countries on six continents, 93.6% reported at least one unwanted intrusion in the past 3 months [Radomsky-etal-2014]. "Most people get odd or upsetting thoughts. Some people get thoughts about harming someone, sex, or religion that horrify them. Does anything like that happen for you?"
3. **Map each episode.** Trigger, intrusion, what it means to the client ("If I don't check, the house will burn and it will be my fault"), emotion (anxiety, disgust, "not just right"), and every response: rituals, mental acts, avoidance, reassurance seeking, and what family members do.
4. **Find mental compulsions.** Ask what goes on in the mind after the thought: reviewing, praying, counting, replacing images, self-reassurance. Obsessions raise distress; rituals lower it or are meant to [Gillihan-etal-2012].
5. **Rate insight.** "Right now, how sure are you, 0 to 100, that this would happen if you didn't do the ritual?"
6. **Rate severity.** Administer the Y-BOCS symptom checklist and severity scale (10 items, 0 to 40: time, interference, distress, resistance and control for obsessions and for compulsions). Do not copy items into forms; the scale is proprietary. Empirical adult bands: 0 to 13 mild, 14 to 25 moderate, 26 to 34 moderate to severe, 35 to 40 severe [Storch-etal-2015]. Use the Children's Y-BOCS (CY-BOCS) for youth.
7. **Confirm the diagnosis** with a structured interview where possible, such as the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5), the Anxiety and Related Disorders Interview Schedule for DSM-5 (ADIS-5) or the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) [Stein-etal-2019].
8. **Screen comorbidity and risk.** Depression, tics, substance use, eating problems, and suicidal thinking (see `02-risk.c-ssrs`).
9. **Gather collateral** with consent, especially for children and when family members join rituals (see `01-intake.collateral-information`).

## What to record

- Obsession themes, triggers, feared consequences and core fear.
- Overt and mental compulsions, avoidance, reassurance seeking, with time per day.
- Family accommodation: who answers questions, joins rituals or changes routines. Accommodation correlates with severity (r = .42 across 41 studies) [Wu-etal-2016].
- Insight rating, Y-BOCS total and subtotals, self-report score, onset, past treatment (how much ERP, which medicines, at what dose and for how long).
- Tics, comorbidity and risk screen results.

## Guidance

- If a client says "I could never say it out loud" → normalize and offer a choice: "You can write it down or give me the first word. I've heard many thoughts like this." Because shame about taboo content drives non-disclosure, and intrusions are near universal [Radomsky-etal-2014].
- If the client reports few rituals but hours of distress → ask specifically about mental reviewing and self-reassurance. Because mental compulsions are easy to miss [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If the client is fully convinced the feared outcome is real, with no hallucinations or thought disorder → record OCD with poor or absent insight, not psychosis. Because the International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11) advises against diagnosing delusional disorder when the belief fits the OCD picture [Reed-etal-2019].
- If repetitive thoughts are about real-life problems and come without rituals → consider generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) or depressive rumination. Because those worries are less unwanted and less senseless than obsessions [Stein-etal-2019].
- If the client asks you during assessment "Do you think I'd really do it?" → note it as reassurance seeking and gently defer: "That question is part of what we'll work on. For now I want to understand it." Because answering adds a ritual to the pattern you are trying to map (expert consensus).

## Common problems

- **Content-based checklists miss idiosyncratic symptoms.** The Y-BOCS rates time, interference, distress, resistance and control, so it works for any content [Storch-etal-2015].
- **Treating a high self-report score as a diagnosis.** Screening scores need an interview.
- **Response judged by feel.** Use the consensus rules: response is a 35% or greater Y-BOCS drop with clinician-rated much or very much improved; remission is a Y-BOCS of 12 or less with minimal global severity, each for at least a week [Mataix-Cols-etal-2016].
