**Bottom line:** Unwanted thoughts of harming someone, sexual images involving children, or blasphemous urges are common obsessional themes in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). They distress the person because they clash with the person's values [Rachman-1997] [Veale-2007]. Assess them with the same structured questions you use for any risk, but do not treat the content itself as evidence of intent. Alarm, unnecessary reports or misdiagnosis confirm the client's worst fear and drive disclosure underground.

## What it looks like

- The clinician's face or voice changes, the topic is closed fast, or the session ends with a referral to forensic services.
- A report is made on content alone, with no intent, plan, behavior or victim access established.
- Notes label the client a "risk to children" or record a paraphilia without assessing whether the thoughts are wanted.
- The client stops disclosing, increases avoidance (will not bathe their baby, hides knives) and asks for reassurance.
- At the other extreme, suicide risk is overlooked because attention went to the content.

## Why it happens

- Clinicians are trained to act on disclosures about harm, and the content sounds like a threat.
- Many clinicians are not taught that intrusions are near universal (93.6% of students across six continents reported at least one in 3 months) [Radomsky-etal-2014] and that OCD is defined by unwanted, resisted thoughts [Stein-etal-2019].
- Fear of liability pushes toward action without a full assessment.

## The fix

- If a client discloses a harm or sexual intrusion → stay calm, thank them, and assess the function: "Is the thought wanted or unwanted? How do you feel when it comes? What do you do afterward?" Because in OCD the thoughts are unwanted and followed by neutralizing or avoidance [Stein-etal-2019].
- If the thought is unwanted, horrifying to the client, and followed by avoidance, checking or reassurance seeking, with no intent, plan, arousal-seeking or acts → formulate it as an obsession and record why. Because catastrophic misreading of normal intrusions is the core of the cognitive model [Rachman-1997].
- If there is intent, planning, pleasure or arousal sought from the thought, past violence, or acts toward a victim → assess as harm to others and follow `02-risk.harm-to-others`, consulting a supervisor the same day; if someone is in immediate danger, call 911 in the United States (US) or 999 in the United Kingdom (UK). Because these features fall outside the obsessional pattern (expert consensus).
- If you are unsure about a legal duty → consult a supervisor or legal adviser before acting, and document the reasoning. Because duties vary by jurisdiction (see `14-ethics-law.duty-to-warn-and-protect`).
- If the client is distressed and hopeless about "being a monster" → ask about suicidal thoughts. Because OCD carries a raised risk of suicide death (unadjusted odds ratio 9.83) [FernandezDeLaCruz-etal-2017]. In crisis, US: call or text 988 or call 911; UK: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans 116 123.
- If the core fear is "I am a dangerous person" → offer Theory A versus Theory B and build exposure to it. Because testing the core fear directly is central to exposure work [Gillihan-etal-2012] [Veale-2007].

Example phrasings:
- "Thank you for telling me. Many people with OCD have thoughts exactly like this, and they are usually the people most horrified by them."
- "I'm going to ask some careful questions so we both understand this properly."
- Avoid: "Have you ever acted on it? Should I be worried about your children?" as an opening response.

## Sources note

That unwanted intrusions are normal and that OCD arises from their misinterpretation rests on the cognitive theory of obsessions [Rachman-1997] and cross-cultural data [Radomsky-etal-2014]. The suicide figure is from a Swedish population cohort [FernandezDeLaCruz-etal-2017]. Features that separate obsessions from risk are expert consensus; no validated tool makes this distinction.
