**Bottom line:** Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is diagnosed when obsessions, compulsions or both take a lot of time (for example, more than an hour a day) or cause marked distress or impairment [Stein-etal-2019]. About 1.2% of United States (US) adults have it in a given year and mean onset is about 19.5 years [Ruscio-etal-2010]; the course is often long, though many people remit [Stein-etal-2019]. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) with exposure and response prevention (ERP) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the first-line treatments [APA-Psychiatry-OCRD-Site]. Suicide risk is raised, so screen for it at intake [FernandezDeLaCruz-etal-2017].

## Diagnostic essentials

Paraphrased from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition, text revision (DSM-5-TR); read the manual for the criteria text [APA-DSM5TR-2022].

- **Obsessions:** recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, urges or images that cause anxiety or distress, which the person tries to ignore, suppress or neutralize.
- **Compulsions:** repetitive behaviors (washing, checking, ordering) or mental acts (praying, counting, reviewing) the person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or by rigid rules. They aim to reduce distress or prevent a feared event but are not realistically connected to it, or are clearly excessive.
- **Threshold:** obsessions or compulsions or both, time-consuming or causing clinically significant distress or impairment [Stein-etal-2019]. DSM-5-TR sets no minimum duration [APA-DSM5TR-2022].
- **Exclusions:** not caused by a substance or medical condition, and not better explained by another disorder, such as worry in generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), guilty rumination in depression, or thought insertion in psychosis [Stein-etal-2019].
- **Specifiers:** insight (good or fair, poor, or absent with delusional beliefs) and tic-related (current or past tic disorder) [Stein-etal-2019]. International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) code F42.2 [APA-DSM5TR-2022].

**Key differences in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th revision (ICD-11)**

| Feature | DSM-5-TR | ICD-11 |
|---|---|---|
| Grouping | Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders | Same grouping, plus hypochondriasis (health anxiety disorder) and olfactory reference disorder [Reed-etal-2019] |
| Insight | Three levels | Two qualifiers, "fair to good" (6B20.0) or "poor to absent" (6B20.1) [WHO-ICD11-2022]; do not add delusional disorder when fixed beliefs fit OCD [Reed-etal-2019] |
| Tics | Tic-related specifier | Tourette syndrome and chronic tic disorders are cross-listed instead [Reed-etal-2019] |
| Emotions | Anxiety or distress | Notes disgust, shame, incompleteness or "not just right" feelings as common [Reed-etal-2019] |

ICD-11 also dropped the ICD-10 subtypes and the ICD-10 bar on diagnosing OCD alongside depression [Reed-etal-2019]. The grouping rests on shared repetitive thoughts and behaviors and on family and neurobiological evidence [Stein-etal-2016].

## Course and prevalence

- **Prevalence:** lifetime 2.3% and 12-month 1.2% in the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication [Ruscio-etal-2010]; lifetime 2% to 3% across national surveys [Stein-etal-2019].
- **Onset:** mean 19.5 years. Nearly a quarter of affected males had onset before age 10; very early onset is mostly male, and females accumulate cases faster after age 10 [Ruscio-etal-2010].
- **Burden:** 12-month cases reported a mean of 5.9 hours a day on obsessions and 4.6 hours on compulsions, and 65.3% reported severe role impairment [Ruscio-etal-2010].
- **Course:** usually early onset and long duration; symptoms can persist for decades, though many remit [Stein-etal-2019]. Only 20.0% of 12-month cases received OCD-specific treatment [Ruscio-etal-2010].

## Differential and comorbidity

About 90% of people with lifetime OCD meet criteria for another disorder: anxiety disorders 75.8%, mood disorders 63.3%, impulse-control disorders 55.9%, substance use disorders 38.6% [Ruscio-etal-2010].

- **GAD and depression:** worry and rumination usually concern real-life matters, feel less senseless and less unwanted, and rarely bring compulsions [Stein-etal-2019].
- **Psychosis:** poor or absent insight without hallucinations or thought disorder is still OCD [Stein-etal-2019] [Reed-etal-2019].
- **Autism:** repetitive behaviors overlap, but OCD lacks autism's social communication differences [Stein-etal-2019].
- **Related disorders and health anxiety:** body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), hoarding, hair pulling and illness worry have their own focus (see `ocd.related-disorders`).
- **Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder:** traits such as perfectionism and rigidity are usually experienced as reasonable rather than intrusive (expert consensus).

## CBT approach in brief

The cognitive model holds that intrusions become obsessions when read as signs of responsibility for harm, and that rituals, avoidance and reassurance keep that meaning alive (see `ocd.cbt-model`). Treatment combines ERP with cognitive work on responsibility and the meaning of thoughts (see `ocd.treatment` and `ocd.erp-protocol`). For mild to moderate OCD, ERP or an SSRI, or both, are first-line by client preference; severe OCD warrants both together [APA-Psychiatry-OCRD-Site]. In the United Kingdom (UK), National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline CG31 matches intensity to functional impairment, and an update is due in 2027 (see `ocd.treatment`) [NICE-CG31].

## Risk notes

- In a Swedish cohort of 36,788 patients, OCD carried an unadjusted odds ratio of 9.83 for suicide death and 5.45 for attempts. A previous attempt was the strongest predictor of death; comorbid substance use and personality disorders added risk [FernandezDeLaCruz-etal-2017].
- Ask directly about suicidal thoughts at intake and when depression rises (see `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`). If there is current intent or a plan, follow `02-risk.crisis-response` the same day. In crisis, US: call or text 988 (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or 911; UK: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans 116 123.
- Unwanted harm, sexual or blasphemous intrusions are typical OCD content, not intent. Assess them calmly (see `ocd.pitfall-misreading-intrusions-as-risk`).
