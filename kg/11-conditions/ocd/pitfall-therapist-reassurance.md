**Bottom line:** In obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), reassurance seeking is a compulsion, and a therapist who answers "Could I have...?" questions becomes part of the ritual [Gillihan-etal-2012]. Debating how unlikely the feared outcome is works the same way [Veale-2007]. Give warmth and validation of the distress, decline to answer the content, and turn the moment into practice with uncertainty.

## What it looks like

- The client asks "Do you think I'd ever actually do it?" or "Is this thought normal?" and the therapist answers.
- Sessions are spent proving a fear is unlikely, with facts, statistics or examples.
- The client asks for summaries or emails "to remember what you said," then rereads them when anxious.
- "Cognitive restructuring" becomes a long evidence list the client must recite to feel safe.
- Relief comes quickly and fades within minutes or hours; the question returns with a new detail ("But what if this time...").
- The therapist feels pressure to be certain and ends sessions over time.

## Why it happens

- Reassurance feels kind, and refusing feels cold, especially with taboo content.
- The cognitive model is misread as "correct the thought." In OCD the target is the meaning given to intrusions and the neutralizing that follows, not the intrusion's content [Salkovskis-1999].
- Some early corrective information is useful, which blurs the line [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- Clinicians are prone to delivery errors in exposure and response prevention (ERP) [Hezel-Simpson-2019], and giving reassurance is one of seven common ones [Gillihan-etal-2012].

## The fix

- If the client asks the same content question a second time → name the pattern kindly: "I notice we've come back to this. Answering it again would feed the doubt. What would it be like to leave it open?" Because repeated reassurance requests are rituals and should not be granted [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If this is early in treatment → agree a rule together: "I'll give you basic facts once, then we'll treat further checking with me as a ritual we're working on." Because a small amount of reassurance can fit early in treatment, but repeated requests are rituals that must stop [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If the client is distressed → validate the feeling, not the fear: "This is really painful, and you're staying with it." Because praising engagement is helpful while reassuring about the outcome is not [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If you catch yourself debating odds → stop and return to an experiment or exposure. Because probability debates feed reassurance [Veale-2007].
- If the client substitutes self-reassurance → add it to the response prevention rules. Because self-reassurance is another compulsion or way of neutralizing [Veale-2007].

Example phrasings:
- "I'm not going to answer that, and that's because I care about you getting better."
- "Can we let that question stay unanswered for the rest of the session and see what happens to the urge?"
- Avoid: "Trust me, people like you never act on these thoughts."
- Avoid: "Let's go through the evidence again so you can feel sure."

## Sources note

The rule against granting repeated reassurance and the line between praise and reassurance come from a published review of common errors in exposure and ritual prevention [Gillihan-etal-2012]. Treating reassurance seeking as a compulsion and avoiding probability debates follow Veale's clinical review [Veale-2007]. No trial has compared reassuring with non-reassuring therapist styles directly; this is expert consensus grounded in the model.
