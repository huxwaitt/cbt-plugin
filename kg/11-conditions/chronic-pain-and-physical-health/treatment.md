**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for chronic pain gives small but reliable gains in pain, disability and distress [Williams-etal-2020], and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) also helps, with its clearest gains in functioning [Ma-etal-2023; Hughes-etal-2017]. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) says to consider either for chronic primary pain, alongside supervised exercise [NICE-NG193]. For depression with a long-term condition, offer stepped CBT, usually adapted to the illness and coordinated with medical care [NICE-CG91].

## When to use

- Chronic primary pain, or chronic secondary pain with distress and disability.
- Depression, anxiety or distress alongside diabetes, coronary heart disease, cancer or another long-term condition.
- Fear of recurrence, fear of exertion or illness-related avoidance that blocks self-management.

## How it works

CBT for pain changes threat appraisals, catastrophizing and avoidance, and rebuilds activity, so that pain takes up less of life. ACT aims to increase valued action while pain is present, rather than reduce pain first [Hughes-etal-2017]. In long-term conditions, CBT treats the depression or anxiety and the behaviors (inactivity, poor adherence, avoidance of care) that worsen the illness.

## Steps

A typical course for pain runs about 8 to 12 sessions (expert consensus; follow your protocol):
1. **Engage and formulate.** Shared fear-avoidance or illness formulation; goals stated as valued activities.
2. **Pain education.** Hurt is not always harm; flare-ups are expected and survivable.
3. **Pacing and graded activity.** Find a sustainable baseline, then increase by plan, not by pain.
4. **Graded exposure.** For high fear of movement, build a hierarchy of feared activities and test predictions. In 88 people with chronic back pain and high fear, exposure reduced movement-related disability more than CBT on one of two disability measures, with no difference in pain intensity, and a short version worked faster [Glombiewski-etal-2018].
5. **Cognitive work.** Restructure catastrophic predictions; practice decentering from pain thoughts.
6. **Acceptance and values.** Willingness exercises, defusion and committed action toward named values.
7. **Mood, sleep and relationships.** Behavioral activation, sleep scheduling, communication with family.
8. **Flare-up and relapse plan.**

For depression with a long-term condition, NICE suggests [NICE-CG91]:
- Mild to moderate: guided self-help based on CBT, computerized CBT, group physical activity or peer support.
- Moderate: group CBT (6 to 8 people sharing a condition, over 6 to 8 weeks) or individual CBT (typically over 6 to 8 weeks, normally no more than 16 to 18 weeks), with 2 follow-up sessions in the next 6 months.
- Severe: individual CBT over about 16 to 18 weeks, starting with behavioral activation, with 2 or 3 follow-up sessions in the next 12 months; consider combining it with an antidepressant.
- No response: collaborative care with a case manager and close links between physical and mental health services.

## Guidance

- If the client has chronic primary pain → consider CBT or ACT for pain alongside a supervised group exercise program. Because NICE recommends offering supervised group exercise and considering ACT or CBT for pain [NICE-NG193].
- If the client is in the US → present psychological therapy as a first-line nonopioid option. Because the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) prefers nonopioid therapies for chronic pain and names CBT as improving pain and function without serious harms [Dowell-etal-2022], and the American College of Physicians lists CBT among initial treatments for chronic low back pain [Qaseem-etal-2017].
- If a client with cancer has moderate depression or anxiety → offer CBT, behavioral activation or structured physical activity, plus mindfulness-based stress reduction for depression or ACT for anxiety. Because the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) recommends these within stepped care [Andersen-etal-2023].
- If fear of cancer recurrence dominates → target worry, rumination and attention to threat rather than the content of fears. Because these contemporary approaches had larger effects [Tauber-etal-2019].
- If depression persists with poorly controlled diabetes or heart disease → advocate for nurse-led collaborative care. Because in 214 patients it improved glycated hemoglobin, blood pressure and depression together [Katon-etal-2010].

## Common problems

- **Pain does not fall.** Review function and values gains; pain reduction is usually small.
- **Exercise flare-up.** Lower the baseline, do not stop the plan.
- **Poor attendance from illness.** Offer telephone or video sessions and flexible timing.

## Evidence

- Chronic pain, CBT: 59 trials. Against usual care, small benefits on the standardized mean difference (SMD) for pain (-0.22), disability (-0.32) and distress (-0.34); against active controls, very small [Williams-etal-2020].
- Chronic pain, ACT: 21 trials (1,962 participants) showed SMD -0.88 for pain-related functioning, -0.45 for pain intensity and -0.74 for depression [Ma-etal-2023]. An earlier review of 11 trials found no effect on pain intensity [Hughes-etal-2017], and Cochrane rated ACT evidence very low quality [Williams-etal-2020].
- Emerging: pain reprocessing therapy (8 sessions over 4 weeks) left 66% of 151 adults with back pain pain-free or nearly so, against 20% with placebo and 10% with usual care, in one trial [Ashar-etal-2022].
- Diabetes: in 12 trials, CBT improved depression and short- to medium-term glycemic control [Uchendu-Blake-2017].
- Coronary heart disease: across 35 trials, psychological interventions modestly reduced depression (SMD -0.27), anxiety (-0.24) and cardiac mortality, but not total mortality, with low-quality evidence [Richards-etal-2017].
- Biofeedback is not recommended by NICE for chronic primary pain [NICE-NG193].
