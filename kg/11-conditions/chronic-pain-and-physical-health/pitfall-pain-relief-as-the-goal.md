**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) usually reduces pain only a little [Williams-etal-2020], so a plan built on pain relief sets both people up to fail. Set goals in function and valued activity, measure interference as well as intensity, and tell the client early that quality of life can improve even if pain does not [NICE-NG193].

## What it looks like

- The first question each session is "How is the pain, out of 10?" and the answer decides the mood of the session.
- Homework is cancelled on bad days; activity plans wait "until the pain settles".
- The client says, "Nothing is working," after weeks of real gains in walking, sleep or work.
- The therapist starts searching for a new technique to reduce pain, or ends treatment as a failure.

## Why it happens

Pain relief is what the client came for and what medicine usually promises. The therapist may share the hope, or fear seeming to dismiss the pain. Yet in the fear-avoidance account, putting life on hold until pain is solved is itself the trap: repeated, futile attempts to fix the pain crowd out other goals [Crombez-etal-2012]. Trials of CBT against usual care show small effects on pain intensity and somewhat larger effects on disability and distress [Williams-etal-2020], and reviews of acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) show gains in functioning without reliable change in pain intensity [Hughes-etal-2017].

## The fix

- If you are agreeing goals → name two or three valued activities and track them, not pain alone. Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) suggests discussing priorities, abilities and goals and what already helps when building the care plan [NICE-NG193].
- If the client expects cure → explain early that pain may persist or flare, a cause may not be found, and life can still improve. Because NICE advises discussing exactly these points [NICE-NG193].
- If you measure progress → add interference alongside intensity, for example with the PEG (pain, enjoyment, general activity) scale. Because it captures pain's effect on enjoyment and activity and is sensitive to change [Krebs-etal-2009].
- If the client wants to wait for pain to fall → test the plan as an experiment: "What would you do this week if the pain stayed the same?" Because ACT targets valued action in the presence of pain [Hughes-etal-2017].
- If pain rises sharply or changes character → refer for medical review rather than treat it as a setback in motivation. Because new symptoms may need investigation [NICE-NG193].

Example phrasing: "I wish I could promise the pain will go. What I can work on with you is how much room it takes up, and getting back to the things you care about."

Avoid: "If you do this program, your pain will get better."

## Sources note

Effect sizes come from the 2020 Cochrane review of psychological therapies for chronic pain [Williams-etal-2020]. Expectation-setting advice is from the NICE chronic pain guideline, which is UK guidance; US guidance makes similar function-focused points. Using valued activity as the primary goal is supported by ACT trials and the goal-based fear-avoidance account, and remains partly expert consensus.
