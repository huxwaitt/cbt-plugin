**Bottom line:** Assess the person, not just the pain or disease: how the condition affects their life, how life affects the condition, what they believe about it, and what they want [NICE-NG193]. Confirm medical status with the treating team, screen mood and anxiety, and add one or two condition-specific measures such as a pain interference scale or a diabetes distress scale. Always ask about suicidal thoughts.

## When to use

At intake for any client whose referral involves chronic pain or a long-term condition. Repeat it at a flare-up or change in symptoms, when the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises reassessment [NICE-NG193], and at a new complication or major change in medical status, when diabetes guidance advises rescreening for depression [ADA-Standards-2026].

## Steps

1. **Confirm medical status.** With consent, contact the treating clinician: diagnosis, current treatment, investigations pending, activity limits and medications. Check that the physical problem is being treated optimally, and consider whether it or its medication contributes to low mood [NICE-CG91].
2. **Hear the illness story.** Onset, course, flare-ups, contacts with services, and what tests and explanations the client has been given. Acknowledge that living with pain is distressing [NICE-NG193].
3. **Map impact both ways.** Ask how the condition affects work, sleep, mood, relationships, family and finances, and how stress, trauma, substance use and social problems affect the condition [NICE-NG193].
4. **Elicit beliefs and expectations.** What does the client think causes the pain or symptoms? What do they expect to happen? What do they expect from treatment [NICE-NG193]? Listen for catastrophic beliefs ("this pain means damage"), fear of movement, and fear of recurrence.
5. **Map behavior.** Activity patterns (avoidance, overactivity and crash), checking, help seeking, medication use, and self-management tasks such as glucose monitoring or cardiac rehabilitation.
6. **Ask about strengths.** What helps when pain is difficult, and what living well means to them [NICE-NG193].
7. **Screen and measure** (see table).
8. **Assess risk.** Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, and screen hopelessness, mental defeat, burdensomeness, substance misuse and access to stored medicines [Racine-2018]. If risk is present, follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment` and restrict access to lethal means. For imminent risk in the US call 911 or call or text 988; in the UK call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123.
9. **Share a provisional formulation** and agree goals framed as function and valued activity.

| Measure | What it gives | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| PEG (pain, enjoyment, general activity) | Average pain and interference, each item 0 to 10, score is the mean of three | Derived from the Brief Pain Inventory for primary care; sensitive to change [Krebs-etal-2009] |
| Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) | 13 items rated 0 to 4 (total 0 to 52) with rumination, magnification and helplessness subscales | A total above 30 is the manual's clinically relevant level [SRALab-PCS]. Licensed through Mapi Research Trust [PCS-Site] |
| Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire | Confidence in doing activities despite pain | Useful before and after treatment [Nicholas-2007] |
| Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) | Depression and anxiety severity | Cutoff 10 on each [Kroenke-etal-2001; Spitzer-etal-2006] |
| Distress Thermometer | Single 0 to 10 distress rating, common in cancer care | A score of 4 performed best in most validation studies [Donovan-etal-2014] |
| Diabetes Distress Scale | 17 items on emotional burden, physician, regimen and interpersonal distress | Distinct from depression [Polonsky-etal-2005] |

The American Diabetes Association advises screening people with diabetes at least annually for diabetes distress, anxiety and depression, and for fear of hypoglycemia in those at high risk of or with frequent or severe hypoglycemia [ADA-Standards-2026].

## What to record

- Medical diagnoses, treating clinicians, medications, activity limits and the agreed rule for new symptoms.
- Impact on function, the client's beliefs and goals, and baseline scores.
- Risk screen result and actions; consent for information sharing.

## Guidance

- If the client brings normal test results with anger or despair → acknowledge the pain is real before exploring meaning. Because NICE warns that communicating normal or negative results can invalidate the experience of pain [NICE-NG193].
- If the client has language, sensory or learning difficulties → use the Distress Thermometer or ask a family member or carer about symptoms. Because NICE suggests these routes when standard questions are hard to use [NICE-CG91].
- If PHQ-9 is high mainly from fatigue, sleep or appetite items → review those items with the medical team before calling it depression. Because illness and treatment can raise somatic items (expert consensus).
- If care is shared between services → agree in writing who monitors and treats what. Because NICE asks for clear agreement on responsibility, especially with the family doctor [NICE-CG91].
- If the client describes fear of a missed diagnosis rather than fear of harm from activity → add the health anxiety assessment. Because the two fears need different formulations (expert consensus).

## Common problems

- **Pain scores used as the only outcome.** Add interference and function measures.
- **Assessment feels like another medical interrogation.** Lead with the client's story and goals.
- **No contact with the medical team.** Get consent early; work blind only as a last resort.
