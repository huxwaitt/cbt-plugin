**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for people with physical illness works inside a medical plan. Without agreed roles, the therapist may push activity the body cannot take, contradict the doctor, or miss a change that needed review. Agree roles, a symptom rule and information sharing at the start, as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises for shared care [NICE-CG91].

## What it looks like

- A graded walking plan is set after a cardiac event without knowing the cardiologist's limits.
- The client asks whether to cut down an opioid and the therapist gives an opinion.
- The doctor tells the client to rest while the therapist encourages activity.
- A new or changed pain is formulated as catastrophizing without anyone examining it.
- Depression scores worsen, but the primary care clinician is never told.

## Why it happens

Therapists may lack contacts in physical health services, worry about confidentiality, or assume the doctor is too busy. Medical teams may not know what CBT involves. Yet NICE expects physical activity programs to be planned in liaison with the team treating the physical problem [NICE-CG91], and recommends reassessment when symptoms change, since an initial diagnosis of chronic primary pain may change with time [NICE-NG193].

## The fix

- If you begin treatment → get consent and agree in writing who monitors and treats what, including the family doctor. Because NICE asks for clear agreement on responsibility when care is shared [NICE-CG91].
- If activity or exercise is part of the plan → confirm safe limits with the treating team first. Because NICE ties physical activity for people with chronic illness to the physical health team [NICE-CG91].
- If symptoms change or a flare-up is different from usual → prompt medical reassessment before continuing exposure. Because NICE advises reassessment and investigating new symptoms at a flare-up [NICE-NG193].
- If the client raises stopping or reducing pain medicines → refer to the prescriber and offer CBT support for a planned taper. Because stopping is a shared decision with the prescriber [NICE-NG193], and abrupt opioid discontinuation has been linked to mental health crises and suicide [Dowell-etal-2022].
- If depression does not respond → recommend collaborative care. Because coordinated nurse-led care improved depression and disease control together [Katon-etal-2010], and NICE suggests collaborative care when initial treatments have not worked [NICE-CG91].
- If suicidal thoughts emerge during a taper or a medical setback → assess risk now and tell the prescriber. In the US call 911 or call or text 988; in the UK call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because rapid tapering or stopping has been linked to psychological distress, overdose and suicidal ideation [Dowell-etal-2022].

Example phrasing: "I'd like to check with Dr. Lee what level of activity is safe for you, so that we are all giving you the same message. Is that OK?"

## Sources note

Shared-care rules come from NICE CG91 and NG193 (UK guidance). The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2022 opioid guideline supports coordinated, gradual tapering with behavioral health input [Dowell-etal-2022]. Collaborative care evidence is from the TEAMcare randomized trial [Katon-etal-2010].
