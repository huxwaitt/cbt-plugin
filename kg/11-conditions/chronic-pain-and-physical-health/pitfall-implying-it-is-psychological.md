**Bottom line:** A psychological referral already sounds to many clients like "they think I'm making it up". If your explanation adds to that, the alliance breaks. Pain is defined as a sensory and emotional experience [Raja-etal-2020], and chronic primary pain is a diagnosis in its own right [Nicholas-etal-2019]. Say the pain is real first, then explain what makes it louder.

## What it looks like

- The therapist says the pain is "caused by stress" or "psychosomatic", or calls it "medically unexplained" as if that settles it.
- After normal scans the client is told there is "nothing wrong".
- The client spends sessions proving the pain is physical, brings new reports, or asks for more tests.
- The client drops out, or only attends because a doctor insisted.

## Why it happens

The therapist wants a reason for CBT and reaches for a psychological cause. Normal test results can sound like proof of that. But the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) definition treats pain as a sensory and emotional experience, distinct from nociception, that can be present when tissue damage is not discernible [Raja-etal-2020]. Chronic primary pain is classified as a condition regardless of whether biological or psychological contributors are found [Nicholas-etal-2019], and somatic symptom disorder no longer requires symptoms to be medically unexplained [Lowe-etal-2022].

## The fix

- If you introduce cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) → say what it is for (coping, function, distress), not what caused the pain. Because blame-free explanations that do not reattribute symptoms to a psychological cause support engagement [Henningsen-2018].
- If the client reports normal or negative test results → acknowledge the pain before anything else. Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) warns that such results risk invalidating the person's experience of pain [NICE-NG193].
- If the client says "you think it's all in my head" → agree the pain is physical and real, and describe stress, sleep, mood and activity as amplifiers. Because a biopsychosocial explanation framed as amplification, not cause, keeps the alliance [Henningsen-2018].
- If distress is visible → name it and validate it. Because NICE asks clinicians to recognize that living with pain is distressing and to acknowledge this [NICE-NG193].
- If the client remains unconvinced → do not debate; agree to test what helps function. Because a collaborative, supportive relationship is the base of care [NICE-NG193].

Example phrasing: "Your pain is real, and it is in your body. Pain is also processed by the nervous system, which is affected by sleep, stress and fear. We can work on those parts while your doctor works on the rest."

Avoid: "The tests are clear, so this must be psychological."

## Sources note

The pain definition is the 2020 revised IASP definition [Raja-etal-2020]. Guidance on communication and validation comes from NICE NG193 (UK) and a clinical review of somatic symptom disorder management [Henningsen-2018]. The phrasing examples are expert consensus.
