**Bottom line:** The fear-avoidance model explains why some people with pain recover and others become disabled: pain read as a sign of harm leads to fear, avoidance, hypervigilance and disuse, which keep pain and disability going [Vlaeyen-Linton-2000; Leeuw-etal-2007]. Pain catastrophizing drives the threatening reading [Quartana-etal-2009]. The update adds goals: avoidance is a costly attempt to protect life goals [Crombez-etal-2012]. The same cycles, plus self-management burden, fit many long-term conditions.

## Components

**Fear-avoidance model** [Vlaeyen-Linton-2000; Leeuw-etal-2007]
- **Pain experience:** an injury, flare or sensation.
- **Appraisal:** two routes. Read as unpleasant but not dangerous, the person keeps moving ("confrontation") and fear fades. Read catastrophically, the person enters the fear cycle.
- **Pain catastrophizing:** magnifying the threat of pain, ruminating on it and feeling helpless [Quartana-etal-2009].
- **Pain-related fear:** of pain, movement or re-injury.
- **Escape and avoidance:** of movement, activity and situations, plus guarded movement.
- **Hypervigilance:** attention locked on bodily signals and threat information.
- **Disuse, disability and depression:** deconditioning, lost roles and low mood, which lower pain tolerance and feed back into the next pain experience.
- **Vulnerabilities:** negative affectivity, anxiety sensitivity and threatening illness information.

**Goal-based update** [Crombez-etal-2012]
- Pain interrupts valued goals. Avoidance is a persistent but futile attempt to solve the pain problem in order to protect and restore those goals. Clinically, some people do the opposite and push on until a flare-up, the overactivity side of the cycle (expert consensus).

**Extensions to long-term conditions** (clinical adaptation, expert consensus)
- Appraisals of the illness ("my heart could stop if I exert myself", "the cancer is back"), self-management demands, and losses of role and identity sit in the appraisal box.
- Behaviors include avoiding exercise after a cardiac event, over-checking glucose or under-managing it, and avoiding scans or clinics.

## How it keeps the problem going

1. Avoidance prevents disconfirmation: the client never learns that activity is safe.
2. Hypervigilance makes sensations more noticeable and more threatening.
3. Disuse lowers fitness, so activity really does hurt more, which "confirms" the belief.
4. Lost activity and roles bring depression, which itself raises pain and catastrophizing.
5. Boom-and-bust overactivity on good days produces flare-ups, which strengthen fear and avoidance.
6. Hopelessness and mental defeat can follow, and these relate to suicidality [Racine-2018].

## Drawing it with the client

- Start from a recent flare-up or a day the client avoided something. Flare-ups are sudden, temporary worsenings and a cause may not be found [NICE-NG193].
- Ask: "When the pain went up, what went through your mind? What did you do next? What happened to the pain and your mood over the next week?"
- Draw the circle in the client's words, then add the "confrontation" path as a second route that is possible for them.
- Include what the client is trying to protect: "You stopped gardening to stop the pain getting worse, and because you want to stay well for your grandchildren."
- Avoid: "The pain is caused by your thoughts." Say instead: "Pain is real. These cycles change how loud it is and how much of your life it takes."
- Add a long-term condition layer when needed: the illness, its demands, and the client's beliefs about what symptoms mean.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Maintaining process | Target and method |
|---|---|
| Threat appraisal and catastrophizing | Psychoeducation about pain and hurt versus harm; cognitive restructuring of catastrophic predictions |
| Fear of movement | Graded exposure and behavioral experiments with activity |
| Avoidance and disuse | Graded activity linked to valued goals |
| Boom-and-bust | Pacing to a sustainable baseline |
| Hypervigilance | Attention training and mindfulness |
| Depression and lost roles | Behavioral activation and values work |
| Struggle with pain | Acceptance-based work: acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) aims to increase valued action while pain is present [Hughes-etal-2017] |

Some newer treatments also target the belief that pain signals injury directly [Ashar-etal-2022]. When the formulation does not fit, or symptoms change, recheck medical status before continuing.
