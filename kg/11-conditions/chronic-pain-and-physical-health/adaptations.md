**Bottom line:** Keep the cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) model, but fit it to the illness: agree roles with the medical team, adjust session length and format to the client's capacity, and separate realistic concerns from catastrophic ones. Where health anxiety overlaps, define with the doctor which checks are medically needed and treat the rest as safety behaviors [Tyrer-etal-2014]. Never change medicines or activity limits yourself.

## The principle

Physical illness changes the content of thoughts, the safety of behaviors and who else is involved. The therapist's job is to treat the psychological problem inside the medical plan, not beside it. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks for clear agreement on who monitors and treats what when care is shared [NICE-CG91].

## Guidance

**Working with medical teams**
- If you start with a client who has a long-term condition → get consent to contact the treating clinician and agree roles, information flow and a rule for new symptoms. Because NICE asks for clear agreement on responsibility and checks that physical treatment is optimal [NICE-CG91].
- If the plan involves exercise or graded activity → confirm activity limits with the medical team first. Because NICE ties physical activity programs to liaison with the team treating the physical problem [NICE-CG91].
- If symptoms change or a flare-up occurs → prompt reassessment and review of the care plan. Because NICE advises reassessment and possible investigation of new symptoms at a flare-up [NICE-NG193].
- If depression persists despite good treatment → suggest collaborative care with a case manager. Because a nurse working with primary care physicians improved both depression and disease control [Katon-etal-2010].

**Medication and opioids**
- If an opioid taper is planned → offer CBT support and coordinate timing with the prescriber. Because the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) warns that rapid tapering or discontinuation has been linked to mental health crises and suicidal ideation, names behavioral health professionals as taper support, and suggests consultation before a taper when suicide risk is high [Dowell-etal-2022].
- If the client asks whether to stop a pain medicine → refer to the prescriber for shared review. Because NICE frames stopping as a shared decision that discusses withdrawal problems [NICE-NG193].

**Health anxiety overlap**
- If a client with real disease checks, seeks tests or searches more than the doctor advises → treat the excess as health anxiety and use `health-anxiety.reassurance-seeking`. Because brief adapted CBT reduced health anxiety in cardiac, endocrine and other clinics [Tyrer-etal-2014].
- If distress is about symptoms more than disease → consider somatic symptom disorder. Because it can be diagnosed alongside a confirmed medical condition [Lowe-etal-2022].

**Condition-specific**
- If the client has diabetes → screen for diabetes distress, fear of hypoglycemia and disordered eating, and refer on when needed. Because the American Diabetes Association recommends at least annual distress screening, screening for fear of hypoglycemia in those at risk and for disordered eating, and referral to professionals ideally with diabetes experience [ADA-Standards-2026].
- If the client had a heart attack or cardiac event → screen for depression and treat it actively. Because the American Heart Association recommends screening and regards depression as a risk factor for poor prognosis after acute coronary syndrome [Lichtman-etal-2008; Lichtman-etal-2014].
- If the client has or had cancer → use stepped care matched to severity. Because the American Society of Clinical Oncology recommends this [Andersen-etal-2023]; for fear of recurrence, a five-session intervention with attention training, metacognitive work and values beat relaxation [Butow-etal-2017].
- If the threat is realistic (progressive disease, poor prognosis) → work on coping, meaning and problem solving, not on disputing the facts. Because accurate thoughts are not distortions (expert consensus).

**Capacity and format**
- If illness or mobility limits attendance → offer telephone delivery, flexible timing or shorter sessions. Because NICE suggests telephone delivery when mobility or other difficulties prevent face-to-face contact [NICE-CG91].
- If the client has cognitive impairment → keep the same interventions and adjust delivery or duration. Because NICE recommends this [NICE-CG91].

**Risk**
- If hopelessness, burdensomeness or talk of stopping treatment appears → assess suicide risk with `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`. In the US call 911 or call or text 988; in the UK call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because risk of death by suicide appears at least doubled in chronic pain [Tang-Crane-2006].

## Example phrasings

- "Your doctor and I will each look after a different part of this. Can I share our plan with her?"
- "The pain may stay with you. What would you want to get back to, even with it?"
- "Checking your glucose as your nurse advised is part of looking after yourself. The extra checks are the part we will work on."
- Avoid: "Your scans are clear, so there is nothing to worry about."

## Evidence

Adaptations for medical comorbidity rest on guideline recommendations [NICE-CG91; NICE-NG193; ADA-Standards-2026; Andersen-etal-2023] and randomized trials in medical clinics [Tyrer-etal-2014; Katon-etal-2010; Butow-etal-2017]. Rules on realistic threats and role agreement are expert consensus. The UK manages depression with chronic physical illness under NICE CG91; US practice follows specialty society guidance.
