**Bottom line:** Sleep hygiene is general lifestyle and bedroom advice, and on its own it is not an effective treatment for chronic insomnia. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and the US Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) both advise against using it as a stand-alone therapy [Edinger-etal-2021a] [VA-DoD-CPG-Insomnia-2025]. Offer cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), brief behavioral therapy or digital CBT-I instead, with hygiene points folded in where relevant.

## What it looks like

- The first session ends with a handout: avoid caffeine, keep the room dark, no screens, exercise, a warm drink.
- The client returns: "I already do all that."
- Therapy drifts into tweaking the bedroom while time in bed, naps, lie-ins and clock watching continue.
- The client concludes nothing works and declines further help, or asks for sleeping pills.
- A referral pathway sends every insomnia complaint to a hygiene class first.

## Why it happens

- Hygiene advice is quick, familiar and feels safe.
- Clinicians may not have been trained in sleep restriction and stimulus control.
- It seems logical that fixing habits fixes sleep. But the main perpetuating factors are extended time in bed, irregular timing, conditioned arousal in bed and sleep-related worry [Cox-Olatunji-2021], and hygiene advice does not target them.
- Sleep hygiene education is designed to help normal sleepers keep healthy sleep, not to treat insomnia disorder [VA-DoD-BehaviorChange-2025].

## The fix

- If a client with insomnia of 3 months or more is offered only hygiene advice → replace it with CBT-I or a brief behavioral course. Because hygiene alone is not sufficiently effective [Schutte-Rodin-etal-2008], and nurse-delivered sleep restriction outperformed a sleep hygiene booklet at 6 months (Cohen's d -0.74) [Kyle-etal-2023].
- If the client says "I've tried everything" → ask exactly what they tried, then explain how a sleep window and stimulus control differ from tips. Because hygiene advice does not target the perpetuating factors that the sleep window and stimulus control address [Cox-Olatunji-2021].
- If hygiene points are relevant (late caffeine, alcohol as a sleep aid) → address them inside CBT-I as one optional component. Because guidelines allow hygiene within multicomponent treatment [Edinger-etal-2021a] [VA-DoD-CPG-Insomnia-2025].
- If your UK service has no CBT-I therapist → digital CBT-I is the recommended alternative to hygiene advice or sleeping pills in primary care. Because the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends Sleepio for exactly that group [NICE-HTG624] [Atenstaedt-2023]. UK pathways still offer hygiene advice first, with CBT-I the standard next step [NICE-TA922]; do not stop at the leaflet.

Example phrasing: "Those tips help good sleepers stay good sleepers. Your sleep needs something more targeted: changing when you're in bed and what happens there."

Avoid: "Have you tried a warm bath and cutting out caffeine?" as the main plan.

## Sources note

The AASM recommendation against sleep hygiene as a single therapy is conditional, so clinical judgment applies [Edinger-etal-2021a]; the VA/DoD makes a weak recommendation against, on low-quality evidence. The VA/DoD also warns that hygiene alone may be harmful, because clients who received only hygiene advice may later refuse CBT-I, expecting it to fail too [VA-DoD-CPG-Insomnia-2025].
