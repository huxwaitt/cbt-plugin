**Bottom line:** Since the 2013 Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), insomnia that occurs with another condition is diagnosed and treated in its own right rather than labeled secondary [Khurshid-2015]. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) works in comorbid insomnia (remission 36.0% versus 16.9% for controls) [Wu-etal-2015] and is first-line for adults with comorbidities [Riemann-etal-2023]. Waiting for sleep to recover on its own leaves a problem that raises depression and suicide risk [Baglioni-etal-2011] [Pigeon-etal-2012].

## What it looks like

- Poor sleep is recorded as one item on a depression measure and never assessed further.
- The plan says "sleep should improve as mood lifts."
- Depression responds, but the client still lies awake for hours and fears relapse.
- Sleep complaints are met with "that's the anxiety" and no sleep diary is started.
- The client asks a family doctor for sleeping pills because therapy is "not about sleep."

## Why it happens

- The older primary versus secondary model taught that treating the "cause" would fix sleep [Khurshid-2015].
- Measures for depression and anxiety include sleep items, so it feels assessed.
- Therapists may not know CBT-I or may fear overloading the client.
- Once insomnia is established, its own perpetuating habits and beliefs keep it going, independent of the original trigger (see `insomnia.cbt-model`).

## The fix

- If a client with another disorder reports poor sleep on at least 3 nights a week for 3 months or more → assess insomnia properly and consider a separate diagnosis with the comorbidity specifier. Because the current text revision (DSM-5-TR) allows insomnia disorder with a coexisting mental disorder [APA-DSM5TR-2022], and the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) diagnoses it when insomnia is an independent focus of clinical attention [WHO-ICD11-2022].
- If the client has depression and insomnia → offer CBT-I alongside depression treatment. Because CBT-I works in comorbid insomnia [Wu-etal-2015], and adding it to an antidepressant gave 61.5% versus 33.3% depression remission in a 30-person pilot, a promising difference that was not statistically significant [Manber-etal-2008].
- If insomnia persists after the main disorder improves → treat it before discharge and name it in the relapse plan. Because insomnia predicts new depression (odds ratio 2.60) [Baglioni-etal-2011].
- If the client is in exposure-based trauma therapy or acutely unwell → note insomnia on the problem list and schedule CBT-I for when it is safe. Because the US Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense advise delaying in these cases [VA-DoD-CPG-Insomnia-2025].
- If sleep is very poor → ask about suicidal thoughts, and if present follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment` and give crisis contacts (US: call or text 988, or 911; UK: 999, NHS 111, Samaritans 116 123). Because sleep disturbance is a risk factor for suicidal thoughts and behavior [Pigeon-etal-2012].

Example phrasing: "Sometimes sleep gets better when mood does, but often it develops a life of its own. Shall we look at it directly as part of our plan?"

## Sources note

The comorbidity meta-analysis pooled 37 trials, with larger gains in psychiatric than medical groups [Wu-etal-2015]. The depression trial was a small pilot whose remission difference did not reach significance [Manber-etal-2008]. In 3,755 students, digital CBT-I also slightly reduced paranoia and hallucinations, mediated by better sleep [Freeman-etal-2017], supporting sleep as a treatment target, not only a symptom.
