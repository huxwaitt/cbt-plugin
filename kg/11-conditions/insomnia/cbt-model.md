**Bottom line:** Two complementary models guide cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). Spielman's 3P model explains how a short spell of poor sleep becomes chronic through perpetuating habits that weaken sleep drive and condition the bed to wakefulness [Spielman-etal-1987] [Cox-Olatunji-2021]. Harvey's cognitive model explains how sleep-related worry, threat monitoring, misperception and safety behaviors keep arousal high [Harvey-2002]. Together they say: treat what keeps insomnia going now, not what started it.

## Components

**Spielman's 3P model** [Spielman-etal-1987] [Cox-Olatunji-2021]

- **Predisposing factors:** pre-existing vulnerabilities such as prior insomnia, high sleep reactivity, neuroticism, evening chronotype, pain or other conditions. They do not cause insomnia alone.
- **Precipitating factors:** stressors such as bereavement, a new baby or a new job that trigger acute insomnia, which is common and usually resolves.
- **Perpetuating factors:** what the person does and thinks in response, which keeps insomnia going after the trigger has gone. Behavioral examples: spending extra time awake in bed, going to bed early, sleeping in, napping, non-sleep activities in bed, late caffeine or nicotine. Cognitive examples: worry, attention to sleep-disturbing cues, distorted beliefs about sleep.

A later 4P version adds Pavlovian conditioning, in which the bedroom itself comes to trigger wakefulness [Grima-etal-2019].

**Harvey's cognitive model** [Harvey-2002] [Harvey-etal-2007]

1. **Worry and rumination** about sleep and about the next day's consequences.
2. **Arousal and distress** that follow the worry.
3. **Selective attention and monitoring** of internal cues (body sensations, a racing mind) and external cues (the clock, noises) for signs of poor sleep.
4. **Misperception** of sleep and daytime deficits: overestimating how long it took to fall asleep and how impaired one is.
5. **Unhelpful beliefs** about sleep and about the usefulness of worry.
6. **Safety behaviors**, such as trying to control thoughts or images and suppressing feelings at night, and canceling activities by day, which keep the beliefs from being tested. The cognitive therapy targets these at night and during the day [Harvey-etal-2007].

**Related models.** Espie's attention-intention-effort pathway adds that trying to sleep (sleep effort) disrupts an automatic process [Tang-etal-2023]. Neurocognitive and hyperarousal models emphasize physiological and cortical arousal [Grima-etal-2019].

## How it keeps the problem going

- **Sleep drive cycle:** a bad night leads to an earlier bedtime, a lie-in or a nap. Each reduces sleep pressure at the next bedtime and makes timing irregular, so the next night is also poor [Cox-Olatunji-2021].
- **Conditioning cycle:** long periods awake in bed, often worrying or on a phone, pair the bed with wakefulness and frustration, so getting into bed triggers alertness [Cox-Olatunji-2021].
- **Worry cycle:** "If I don't sleep, tomorrow will be a disaster" raises arousal, which delays sleep, which seems to confirm the worry [Harvey-2002].
- **Monitoring and misperception cycle:** checking the clock and the body for signs of wakefulness increases the chance of noticing them, and lying awake feels longer than it is. People with insomnia often perceive light sleep as wakefulness [Harvey-Tang-2012].
- **Effort cycle:** the harder the person tries to sleep, the less sleep comes [Tang-etal-2023].
- **Safety-behavior cycle:** canceling plans and resting all day protects against feared consequences but prevents learning that one can cope after a poor night, and reduces daytime activity that supports sleep [Harvey-2002].

## Drawing it with the client

1. **Timeline first.** Draw a horizontal line with a "threshold" for insomnia. Ask what life was like before (predisposing), what happened when sleep first went wrong (precipitating), and what they started doing to cope (perpetuating). Most clients see that the trigger has gone but the coping has stayed.
2. **Last bad night.** Use one recent night. "What time did you go to bed? What went through your mind? What did you do when you couldn't sleep? What did you do the next day?"
3. **Draw two loops.** One behavioral loop (time in bed, naps, lie-ins, lower sleep drive), one cognitive loop (worry, arousal, clock watching, beliefs, safety behaviors). Use the client's words.
4. **Validate the logic.** "Going to bed early makes complete sense if you are trying to catch up. The trouble is that it seems to have made the nights longer, not the sleep."
5. **Ask what would break the loops.** Let the client suggest targets before you introduce sleep restriction and stimulus control.

Avoid: "Your sleep problem is all in your head." It invalidates real sleep loss and misstates the model.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Maintaining process | Target | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Too much time in bed, irregular timing, naps | Build sleep drive, anchor timing | Sleep restriction, fixed rise time [Edinger-etal-2021a] |
| Bed paired with wakefulness | Re-pair bed with sleep | Stimulus control [Edinger-etal-2021a] |
| Worry and arousal at night | Lower cognitive and physical arousal | Wind-down, relaxation, worry work [Harvey-etal-2007] |
| Clock watching and monitoring | Reduce threat monitoring | Turn the clock away; attention experiments [Harvey-etal-2007] |
| Unhelpful beliefs, misperception | Test beliefs | Cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments [Harvey-etal-2007] |
| Daytime safety behaviors | Test coping after poor nights | Behavioral experiments with planned activity [Harvey-2002] |
| Sleep effort | Let sleep happen | Drop effort; paradoxical intention (see `insomnia.cbt-i-components`) [Tang-etal-2023] |

See `insomnia.cbt-i-components` for how each technique is done.
