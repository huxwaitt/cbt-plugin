**Bottom line:** When persistent bodily symptoms rather than disease fear are the main problem, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) aims at coping and function, not cure. It works on attention to symptoms, symptom expectations, avoidance and boom-and-bust activity, and illness beliefs, in close contact with medical care [Lowe-etal-2024; Henningsen-2018]. Effects are real but small to moderate [VanDessel-etal-2014].

## When to use

- Somatic symptom disorder (SSD) with predominant pain, fatigue, dizziness or bowel symptoms, or bodily distress disorder in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11).
- Symptoms with or without a medical diagnosis; SSD does not require symptoms to be medically unexplained [Lowe-etal-2022].
- When health anxiety is prominent too, add the health anxiety protocol, which helps SSD as well as illness anxiety disorder [Hedman-etal-2016].

## How it works

Persistent physical symptoms can follow infection, injury, disease or stress, or arise without a clear trigger. Their persistence is kept going by biological and psychological factors together, including illness-related anxiety, negative symptom expectations, symptom focusing, symptom learning and avoidance [Lowe-etal-2024]. The cognitive behavioral model proposes that attention, attributions, mood, behavior and social responses interact to generate and maintain symptoms [Deary-etal-2007]. CBT changes the psychological parts of this loop while medical care manages the rest.

## Steps

1. **Engage on the client's terms.** Acknowledge that the symptoms are real and that the client may come only to please others. Listen to the symptom story and to experiences with doctors, and reflect the anger or disappointment [Henningsen-2018].
2. **Give a blame-free explanation.** Present stress, sleep, mood and activity as amplifiers of symptoms, not their cause. Do not try to reattribute symptoms to a mainly psychological cause [Henningsen-2018].
3. **Set modest goals.** Better coping and function, not cure [Henningsen-2018]. Pick two or three valued activities to rebuild.
4. **Map the cycle.** Symptom → interpretation and attention → emotion → behavior (resting, avoiding, checking, consulting) → deconditioning and more symptoms.
5. **Pace and grade activity.** Replace boom-and-bust patterns with a steady baseline, then increase gradually.
6. **Work with attention and expectations.** Practice shifting attention; test predictions such as "If I walk, the pain will be unbearable."
7. **Address illness behaviors.** Reduce checking, searching and unneeded consulting; agree scheduled appointments with the primary care clinician [Henningsen-2018].
8. **Include emotions and context.** Explore stress, relationships and emotional triggers. A short symptom-context diary can help, but not for clients with very high health anxiety [Henningsen-2018].
9. **Plan for setbacks.** Flare-up plan and a rule for when new symptoms need medical review.

## Guidance

- If the client says "You think it's all in my head" → agree that the symptoms are physical and explain that you will work on what makes them louder or harder to live with. Because blame-free explanations that link body and mind support engagement [Henningsen-2018].
- If the client pushes you to discuss psychological issues before symptoms → keep starting from the lead complaint. Because moving to psychosocial topics too early loses these clients [Henningsen-2018].
- If chronic primary pain is central → offer CBT or acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) for pain alongside supervised exercise. Because the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends offering supervised group exercise and considering ACT or CBT for pain for people aged 16 and over with chronic primary pain [NICE-NG193].
- If the client asks about medication for pain or symptoms → refer to the prescriber. Because NICE lists amitriptyline, citalopram, duloxetine, fluoxetine, paroxetine or sertraline as options to consider for chronic primary pain from age 18 [NICE-NG193], and evidence for newer antidepressants in somatoform disorders is very low quality [Kleinstauber-etal-2014].
- If outpatient CBT is not enough → recommend multidisciplinary care with physiotherapy, occupational therapy and psychotherapy. Because stepped care is the recommended model [Henningsen-2018].
- If hopelessness, anger or substance use rises → ask about suicidal thoughts and follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`; for imminent risk call or text 988 or call 911 (US), or call 999 or Samaritans on 116 123 (UK). Because suicidal ideation and attempts are elevated in somatic symptom and related disorders [Torres-etal-2021].

## Common problems

- **Client declines a "talking" treatment.** Frame CBT as symptom management and keep contact with the medical team.
- **Rest is the main coping strategy.** Use activity diaries to show the link between overactivity, crashes and rest.
- **New investigations restart the worry.** Agree with the doctor how results will be explained before tests.
- **Therapist aims for cure.** Re-anchor goals on function.

## Evidence

- A Cochrane review of 21 trials (2,658 participants) found psychological therapies reduced symptom severity more than usual care or waiting list (standardized mean difference -0.34), a small effect from low-quality evidence. Only CBT was studied enough for tentative conclusions, and it was not better than enhanced care [VanDessel-etal-2014].
- For drugs, newer antidepressants showed benefit in a few trials, but the evidence was very low quality [Kleinstauber-etal-2014].
- NICE recommends considering CBT or ACT for chronic primary pain [NICE-NG193]. There is no NICE or American Psychological Association guideline for SSD as such; status is supported by randomized trials with small effects.
