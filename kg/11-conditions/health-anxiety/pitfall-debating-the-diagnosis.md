**Bottom line:** A therapist cannot win an argument about whether a client is ill, and trying confirms the client's fear of being dismissed. Instead, take the symptoms seriously, put two explanations side by side, and agree to test the worry explanation for a set time while medical options stay open [Warwick-1998].

## What it looks like

- The therapist lists normal test results to prove the client is healthy.
- Phrases such as "it's all in your head", "there's nothing wrong with you" or "it's just stress" appear in sessions or letters.
- The client brings articles to counter the therapist, and each session restarts the debate.
- With somatic symptoms, the therapist pushes to relabel pain or fatigue as psychological.
- The client stops attending, or quietly books more appointments elsewhere.

## Why it happens

Clinicians often feel that correcting the belief is the treatment. Many clients have already been told their symptoms are "all in the mind" and watch for the same attitude [Warwick-1998]. Older diagnostic language described these patients as "difficult" and unable to accept that tests were normal, which encouraged a contest [Lowe-etal-2022]. When conviction is very high, therapists may also wrongly treat it as delusion and either argue or give up; the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) instead asks clinicians to record poor insight and keep treating the health anxiety [Reed-etal-2019].

## The fix

- If you find yourself presenting evidence that the client is healthy → stop, summarize the client's view fairly, and ask what has happened when they followed the disease explanation so far. Because the aim is a fair test of two hypotheses, not persuasion [Warwick-1998].
- If the client is not ready to accept a psychological explanation → offer a time-limited trial of the worry explanation, with medical review still available afterward. Because this framing has helped many reluctant clients engage [Warwick-1998].
- If symptoms are prominent → present stress, attention and activity as amplifiers, not causes, and do not try to reattribute symptoms to a mainly psychological cause. Because blame-free explanations that link body and mind are recommended [Henningsen-2018].
- If you are rehearsing the client's evidence → ask the client to list evidence both ways and re-examine each item together. Because the client then builds the alternative instead of hearing it [Warwick-1998].
- If the debate has damaged the alliance → name your part and return to the formulation. Because repair works better than pushing on (expert consensus).

Example phrasing: "I can't know for certain what is happening in your body, and I won't pretend to. What I can do is help us test whether worry is making this harder. Would you be willing to try that for eight weeks?"

Avoid: "The tests are normal, so there is nothing wrong with you."

## Sources note

The engagement and competing-explanation approach is described by Warwick from the Salkovskis and Warwick treatment program [Warwick-1998]. Recommendations against reattribution in persistent bodily distress come from a clinical review [Henningsen-2018]. The repair bullet is expert consensus.
