**Bottom line:** Keep the core of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for health anxiety, which is testing a worry explanation and dropping safety behaviors, and adjust its targets and partners. With real illness, target excessive attention and checking, not the disease. With fixed conviction, invest longer in engagement. Step up to combined treatment or multidisciplinary care when CBT alone is not enough, and check suicide risk throughout [MataixCols-etal-2024].

## The principle

Adaptation changes how the model is applied, not whether it is applied. The same maintaining processes appear in medical patients, in people with depression, and across cultures, but the content of fears, the role of doctors and family, and the client's explanation of symptoms differ. Decide adaptations from the formulation and say why in the record.

## Guidance

**Medical comorbidity**
- If the client has a confirmed medical condition → define with the treating doctor which monitoring is clinically needed, and treat checks beyond that as safety behaviors. Because brief adapted CBT helped health-anxious patients in cardiology, endocrine, gastroenterology, neurology and respiratory clinics [Tyrer-etal-2014].
- If the fear is of cancer returning after treatment → focus on attention to threat, worry about worry, values and screening behavior. Because a five-session intervention on these targets reduced fear of recurrence more than relaxation [Butow-etal-2017].
- If the client sees doctors often → ask the primary care clinician to book regular scheduled appointments rather than symptom-driven ones. Because this is a recommended principle of care for persistent bodily distress [Henningsen-2018], and in somatization disorder a psychiatric consultation letter with management advice cut medical charges by about half in a trial [Smith-etal-1986].

**Psychiatric comorbidity and severity**
- If depression is moderate or worse, or hopelessness about health appears → monitor mood each session, ask directly about suicidal thoughts, and treat depression alongside or first if it blocks engagement; follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment` if thoughts are present. Because suicide rates are about four times higher in hypochondriasis [MataixCols-etal-2024].
- If panic disorder or generalized anxiety is also present → build one formulation showing which fear drives most avoidance. Because these are the most common comorbidities and somatic symptom disorder carries more of them [Newby-etal-2017].
- If conviction is fixed but there are no other psychotic features → record poor insight, spend more sessions on engagement and the two-explanation framing, and avoid debating. Because the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) treats this as health anxiety with poor insight, not delusional disorder [Reed-etal-2019].

**Medication**
- If the client prefers medication, or has severe symptoms or comorbid depression → discuss a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) with the prescriber. Because paroxetine beat placebo among completers in one trial, and in another fluoxetine beat placebo on continuous scores while responder rates for single treatments did not differ significantly from placebo and combining fluoxetine with CBT added only a small benefit [Greeven-etal-2007; Fallon-etal-2017]. Prescribing depends on license; non-prescribers refer.

**Age**
- If the client is a child or adolescent → use child anxiety CBT methods and involve parents in reducing reassurance and doctor visits. Because health anxiety symptoms are present and persist from age 5 to 7 into age 11 to 12, but no health-anxiety-specific CBT trials exist for youth [Rask-etal-2016; Kikas-etal-2024].
- If the client is older → expect real illness, more medications and more appointments; agree with the doctor which checks are needed and adapt pace and materials. Because evidence specific to older adults is sparse (expert consensus).

**Culture**
- If the client expresses distress mainly through the body or uses a local idiom of distress → explore the meaning of symptoms in the client's family and community before introducing a cognitive explanation. Because somatic expression of distress is common in every culture studied and carries social meanings [Kirmayer-Young-1998]. Use the Cultural Formulation Interview (`10-diagnosis.cultural-formulation-interview`).

**Format and stepping**
- If access is limited or the client prefers it → offer therapist-guided internet CBT first. Because it was noninferior to face-to-face CBT [Axelsson-etal-2020].
- If there is little change after an adequate course → review the formulation and adherence, check hidden safety behaviors, then step up to face-to-face CBT, combined medication, or multidisciplinary care. Because about half of patients did not respond to CBT, fluoxetine or both in one trial [Fallon-etal-2017], and stepped care is recommended for bodily distress [Henningsen-2018].
- If the client lives in the UK and attends a hospital clinic → brief CBT for health anxiety by trained nurses is an evidence-based option. Because benefits held for 8 years [Tyrer-etal-2017; Tyrer-etal-2021].

## Example phrasings

- To a client with heart disease: "Your cardiologist wants a check every six months. Let's separate those checks from the ones the worry asks for."
- To a parent: "When she asks if she is sick, try 'That sounds like the worry talking. What did we agree to do?' rather than checking her again."
- To a client who sees the problem as purely physical: "In your family, what does it usually mean when the body hurts like this?"
- Avoid: "Your culture just expresses stress through the body."

## Evidence

Evidence for these adaptations is mostly indirect. Medical-clinic CBT, internet CBT and medication rest on randomized trials [Tyrer-etal-2014; Axelsson-etal-2020; Fallon-etal-2017]. Child, older adult and cultural adaptations rest on observational data and expert consensus [Rask-etal-2016; Kirmayer-Young-1998].
