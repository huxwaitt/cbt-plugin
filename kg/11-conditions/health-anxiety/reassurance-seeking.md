**Bottom line:** In health anxiety, repeated reassurance works like a compulsion: it brings quick relief, then doubt returns and the next request comes sooner [Salkovskis-Warwick-1986; Abramowitz-Moore-2007]. One clear medical explanation from the responsible doctor is good care [Lowe-etal-2024]; answering the same question again is not. Respond with empathy, name the pattern, point back to the agreed plan, and never let the rule stop a genuinely new or urgent symptom from being checked.

## The principle

Separate two things. **Information** is a single, clear explanation of what tests showed and what to watch for, given by the clinician responsible for medical care. Good communication about bodily symptoms includes appropriate reassurance and a positive explanation [Henningsen-2018; Lowe-etal-2024]. **Reassurance seeking** is asking again, asking others, rechecking or searching to get certainty. It is a safety behavior. It lowers anxiety fast, but refraining lets anxiety and urges fall on their own, only more slowly [Abramowitz-Moore-2007]. Even normal test results in low-risk presentations do little to reduce illness worry [Rolfe-Burton-2013].

A cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) therapist does not judge medical meaning. The task is to help the client notice the request, tolerate the uncertainty, and test what happens without the answer.

## Guidance

- If a client in treatment asks "Do you think this is serious?" → acknowledge the fear, decline to judge it, and ask what the worry predicts will happen if they do not get an answer. Because answering would reinforce the cycle the formulation describes [Warwick-1998].
- If the client is early in treatment and has not yet agreed to drop reassurance → explain the cycle using their own episodes before asking them to stop. Because refusing without a shared rationale feels dismissive and harms engagement [Warwick-1998].
- If the client reports a symptom that is new, clearly different, or severe → direct them to their doctor, or to emergency care for emergency signs, and do not treat it as reassurance seeking. Because health anxiety does not protect anyone from real illness (expert consensus).
- If the same question returns about an already investigated symptom → name it as a reassurance request, refer to the agreed rule (for example "new symptoms lasting two weeks go to the doctor"), and support the client to sit with the urge. Because tolerating the urge is the exposure that weakens it [Abramowitz-Moore-2007].
- If family members are giving reassurance → agree a short, kind, consistent reply with the client present. Because consistent replies support response prevention, and Warwick taught relatives a fixed sentence tied to the treatment plan [Warwick-1998].
- If doctors are ordering tests to calm anxiety → with consent, ask them to base investigations on clinical need and to offer scheduled appointments. Because anxiety-driven tests maintain the problem and regular appointments are recommended [Warwick-1998; Henningsen-2018].
- If the client searches symptoms online → treat searching as a safety behavior with a reduction plan. Because online searching and cyberchondria are linked to health anxiety [McMullan-etal-2019].

**For clinical tools and artificial intelligence (AI) assistants**
- If a user asks a tool whether a symptom is dangerous → do not diagnose or reassure; reflect the worry, give emergency guidance for emergency signs (US 911, UK 999), otherwise suggest their usual clinician for new symptoms. Because a tool that answers each question becomes an unlimited source of reassurance (expert consensus).
- If a user in health anxiety treatment asks the tool the same question repeatedly → note the pattern gently and offer the coping step from their plan. Because this mirrors the therapist response and supports response prevention (expert consensus).
- If a user expresses hopelessness or suicidal thoughts → stop the health topic and follow `02-risk.crisis-response`: in the US call or text 988, or call 911; in the UK call 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because risk comes before any treatment rule (expert consensus).

## Example phrasings

- "I can hear how frightening this feels. I'm not the right person to judge what the pain means, and I notice this is the question the worry keeps asking. What would it be like to leave it unanswered for now?"
- "Your doctor explained the scan last week. If I add my opinion, what happens to the worry tomorrow?"
- "This is a new kind of symptom, so it belongs with your doctor. Let's keep our plan for the old ones."
- For relatives: "I love you, and we agreed I won't answer health questions. Let's do something else for ten minutes."
- Avoid: "There's nothing wrong with you, stop worrying."
- Avoid: "I'm sure it's fine, those tests would have picked it up."

## Evidence

The role of reassurance in maintaining health anxiety comes from the original cognitive behavioral account [Salkovskis-Warwick-1986] and an experiment in which safety behaviors gave faster short-term relief than refraining [Abramowitz-Moore-2007]. A meta-analysis of randomized trials found diagnostic tests in low-risk patients did little to reduce illness worry or anxiety [Rolfe-Burton-2013]. Guidance for tools is expert consensus.
