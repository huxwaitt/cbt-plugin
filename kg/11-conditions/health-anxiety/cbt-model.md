**Bottom line:** In the leading cognitive behavioral model, health anxiety comes from misreading bodily sensations and health information as signs of serious illness, driven by learned assumptions about health [Warwick-1998]. Checking, reassurance seeking, searching, avoidance, body-focused attention and arousal then keep the belief alive [Kikas-etal-2024]. Share it as a competing explanation to "I have an undiagnosed disease" and test it, rather than arguing about medical facts.

## Components

The model was first set out by Salkovskis and Warwick in 1986 and later described for clinicians by Warwick [Salkovskis-Warwick-1986; Warwick-1998]. A closely related cognitive account treats severe health anxiety as an anxiety disorder driven by catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily changes [Rachman-2012].

- **Early experience.** Illness or death of a relative, own childhood illness, medical mistakes, family focus on health.
- **Assumptions about health.** "Physical symptoms always mean illness." "I am like my mother, so I will get cancer too." "The doctor cannot know nothing is wrong without tests" [Warwick-1998].
- **Critical incident.** A symptom, a friend's diagnosis, a news story or a test result activates the assumptions.
- **Negative automatic thoughts and images** of threat to health, for example "This headache must be a brain tumor."
- **Threat appraisal.** Anxiety rises with the perceived likelihood and awfulness of illness and falls with perceived ability to cope and with trust in medical help [Kikas-etal-2024]. Clients rarely fear only the probability; ask about all four parts.
- **Health anxiety**, which feeds four kinds of maintaining response (below).

## How it keeps the problem going

Warwick's figure shows four loops that each feed back into the threat belief [Warwick-1998]:

1. **Behavioral.** Body checking, reassurance seeking, online searching and avoidance. Checking can create symptoms (a prodded lump becomes sore). Reassurance brings quick relief, so it is repeated, but it stops the client learning that the feared outcome does not happen [Abramowitz-etal-2007b]. In an experiment with patients, safety behaviors cut anxiety and urges fast, while refraining led to a slower decline without them [Abramowitz-Moore-2007]. Normal tests in low-risk presentations do little to reduce illness worry [Rolfe-Burton-2013].
2. **Cognitive.** Preoccupation, body focusing that makes ordinary sensations louder, and selective attention to confirming information [Warwick-1998]. Attentional bias to health threat is strongly linked to health anxiety [Kikas-etal-2024]. Online searching correlates with health anxiety (r = 0.34) and can become distressing cyberchondria [McMullan-etal-2019].
3. **Physiological.** Anxiety produces palpitations, breathlessness, dizziness and tension, which are read as more evidence of disease [Warwick-1998].
4. **Affective.** Persistent illness belief lowers mood, and low mood makes threat seem more likely [Warwick-1998].

Related processes include intolerance of uncertainty about health, anxiety sensitivity and health rumination [Kikas-etal-2024].

## Drawing it with the client

1. Take one recent, specific episode in the client's words: trigger, thought, belief rating, anxiety, what they did, what happened next.
2. Draw the circle: sensation → thought → anxiety → checking or reassurance → short-term relief → doubt returns → more attention to the body. Add arousal symptoms as a second arrow back to "sensation".
3. Ask what the client thinks is wrong. Usually the answer is an undiagnosed illness needing more tests [Warwick-1998].
4. Lay out the two explanations side by side. Explanation A: "I have a serious illness that doctors have missed." Explanation B: "I have a problem with worry about illness that makes ordinary sensations feel dangerous." Ask how much effort has gone into A and how well it has worked.
5. Agree to test B for a fixed number of sessions, making clear that medical options stay open if the client is still convinced afterward [Warwick-1998].
6. Run a quick experiment to make the model concrete. Warwick describes therapist and client tensing face and scalp muscles for several minutes; the client developed a headache and her tumor belief fell from 90% to 50% [Warwick-1998].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's not try to settle today whether you are ill. Let's look at what happens when the worry starts."
- "Each time you check, the worry goes down for a while. What happens to it by the next day?"
- Avoid: "There is nothing wrong with you, it is just anxiety."

## Treatment targets it implies

- **Safety behaviors:** reduce checking, reassurance seeking and searching; graded exposure where there is avoidance (response prevention) [Warwick-1998].
- **Attention:** demonstrate how body focus amplifies sensations and practice shifting attention.
- **Misinterpretations:** list the client's evidence for illness and against it, and re-evaluate each item [Warwick-1998].
- **Threat appraisal:** work on awfulness and coping, including fears about death and dependency, not only probability.
- **Assumptions:** test rules such as "a symptom always means illness" with behavioral experiments [Warwick-1998].
- **System:** coach family in an agreed response to reassurance requests and agree with doctors that investigations follow clinical need, not anxiety [Warwick-1998].
