**Bottom line:** Answering a health-anxious client's "Is it serious?" feels kind and calms the session, but it works like a compulsion and keeps the fear going [Salkovskis-Warwick-1986]. The fix is to explain the cycle, agree a plan with the client and the people around them, and respond to requests with empathy and a pointer to the plan, while still sending new or urgent symptoms for medical review.

## What it looks like

- The therapist says "That sounds like a muscle, I wouldn't worry" and the client relaxes, then returns next week with a different symptom.
- Sessions open with a symptom update and end with the therapist's opinion.
- A partner checks the client's skin or pulse several times a day on request.
- A chatbot or symptom checker is consulted many times a day and each answer prompts a follow-up question.
- Doctors order another scan to settle the worry, and the relief fades within days.

## Why it happens

Reassurance brings quick relief to both people, so it is rewarded. In an experiment, health-anxious patients who performed safety behaviors after a trigger felt better faster than those who refrained, whose anxiety and urges fell more slowly but without them [Abramowitz-Moore-2007]. Clinicians also worry that withholding an answer is cold. Tests feel decisive, but in low-risk presentations they do little to reduce illness worry [Rolfe-Burton-2013]. One clear explanation by the responsible doctor is good care; it is the repeated request that maintains the problem [Lowe-etal-2024].

## The fix

- If you notice you have started answering health questions → name it at the next session and add reassurance to the formulation as a safety behavior. Because the client can then see how relief and doubt alternate [Warwick-1998].
- If the client asks again → reflect the fear, decline to judge, and ask what the worry predicts if the question stays unanswered. Because sitting with the urge lets it fall without the safety behavior [Abramowitz-Moore-2007].
- If family members reassure → agree one short, kind sentence with the client present and practice it. Because consistent family replies were part of the original treatment [Warwick-1998].
- If doctors are part of the loop → with consent, ask them to base tests on clinical need. Because anxiety-driven investigations maintain the problem [Warwick-1998].
- If a symptom is truly new, different or severe → send it for medical review. Because the reassurance rule is not a reason to ignore possible disease (expert consensus).

Example phrasing: "I notice I've been answering these questions, and I think it may have kept the worry going. Can we look at what happened after the last time I did?"

## Sources note

The maintaining role of reassurance comes from the original cognitive behavioral account [Salkovskis-Warwick-1986] and the treatment description that includes reassurance prevention and family scripts [Warwick-1998]. The experimental evidence is from a small study of 27 patients [Abramowitz-Moore-2007]. Advice on digital tools is expert consensus.
