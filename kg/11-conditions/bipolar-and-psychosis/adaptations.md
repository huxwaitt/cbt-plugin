**Bottom line:** Keep the core cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) model but adjust pace, targets and team contact to the illness phase. Trauma-focused therapy is safe and effective in people with psychosis and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [VanDenBerg-etal-2015]. Black African and Black Caribbean clients received less CBT in a large London provider [Morris-etal-2020], and culturally adapted interventions beat usual care more the more fully they were adapted [Degnan-etal-2018]. Step up whenever mania, acute psychosis or risk appears.

## The principle

Match the work to the phase: engagement and coping during acute illness, formulation and change work when settled, relapse prevention when well. Work inside a team that includes the prescriber, and record who does what. Psychologists and counselors do not prescribe; medication questions go to the prescriber (scope of practice varies by license and country).

## Guidance

**Comorbidity**
- If a client with psychosis has PTSD → offer standard trauma-focused therapy without a long stabilization phase. Because 8 sessions of prolonged exposure or eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) reduced PTSD without more adverse events than waiting list [VanDenBerg-etal-2015].
- If substance use is present → assess it routinely and fold it into the formulation, including cannabis, caffeine and supplements. Because cannabis worsens mood swings in bipolar disorder [VADoD-Bipolar-2023], and violence risk in psychosis has an odds ratio of 2.1 without substance misuse and 8.9 with it [Fazel-etal-2009].
- If anxiety persists between bipolar episodes → offer CBT for the anxiety disorder. Because the Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guideline suggests it [VADoD-Bipolar-2023].
- If insomnia is present in bipolar disorder → use the bipolar-adapted protocol, time in bed at least 6.5 hours, with weekly mood ratings. Because sleep loss can trigger mania [Harvey-etal-2015].

**Severity and phase**
- If the client is an inpatient or acutely psychotic → use short, flexible sessions on engagement, distress and coping. Because the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) allows CBT for psychosis (CBTp) to start in the acute phase [NICE-CG178].
- If signs of mania appear (less sleep without fatigue, pressured speech, risky spending) → pause cognitive work, contact the prescriber the same or next day, and involve family per the relapse plan. Because CBT has no evidence in acute mania and medication review is urgent (expert consensus).
- If the client becomes unsafe → follow `02-risk.crisis-response` and contact the psychiatric team the same day; in an emergency, United States (US): call or text 988 (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or 911; UK: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans 116 123. Because acute risk outranks any therapy plan (expert consensus).

**Age**
- If the client is under 18 with distressing unusual experiences → include family sessions and adapt for identity and school. Because NICE advises offering family intervention to families of young people with psychosis [Serra-etal-2023], and one adolescent trial protocol combines up to 12 individual and 4 family sessions [Jolley-etal-2017].
- If first-episode psychosis → refer to a coordinated specialty care or early intervention team. Because outcomes are better there, especially with shorter untreated psychosis [Kane-etal-2016].

**Culture and ethnicity**
- If the client's community explains the experiences spiritually or culturally → explore that meaning first and include family or faith supports where wanted. Because benefit over usual care rose with the degree of adaptation [Degnan-etal-2018].
- If the client is from a group less often offered CBT → offer it actively and track whether they reach 16 sessions. Because Black Caribbean clients were less likely to receive 16 sessions (odds ratio 0.83) [Morris-etal-2020].

**Pregnancy and postpartum**
- If a client with bipolar disorder is pregnant or planning pregnancy → link with the prescriber and perinatal team early and write a postpartum relapse plan. Because relapse occurred in 66% of medication-free and 23% of medicated women [Wesseloo-etal-2016].

**Medication**
- If a client with bipolar disorder is on an antidepressant alone → raise it with the prescriber. Because antidepressant monotherapy is not recommended in bipolar I depression [Yatham-etal-2018].
- If the client is ambivalent about medication → explore pros and cons without pushing; share concerns with the prescriber with consent. Because adherence decisions belong to the client and prescriber together.
- If cognitive slowing or sedation limits sessions → shorten sessions, write summaries, repeat key points, and tell the prescriber. Because sedation may be a dose-related side effect (expert consensus).

**Non-response and referral**
- If distress and functioning have not improved by mid-course → review the formulation, adherence, substances and trauma, and discuss in supervision. Because unrecognized maintaining factors are a common reason for non-response (expert consensus).
- If a carer is exhausted → offer carer support or family work. Because the VA/DoD guideline suggests caregiver support programs [VADoD-Bipolar-2023].

## Example phrasings

- "You've told me your sleep has dropped to four hours and you feel great. That's on your amber list. Shall we call Dr. Lee together now?"
- "In your family, how do people make sense of hearing voices? I want to understand it your way first."
- "Today let's keep it to twenty minutes and one thing that would help this week."
- Avoid: "We can't do therapy until you're stable."

## Evidence

Trauma-focused therapy in psychosis rests on a randomized trial [VanDenBerg-etal-2015]; cultural adaptation on a meta-analysis of 46 studies [Degnan-etal-2018]; perinatal figures on a meta-analysis of 37 studies [Wesseloo-etal-2016]. Phase-based pacing and referral rules are expert consensus.
