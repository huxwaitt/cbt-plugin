**Bottom line:** Cognitive models of psychosis hold that unusual experiences become distressing symptoms through how they are appraised: as external, threatening and personally significant, under the influence of emotion, reasoning biases and negative beliefs [Garety-etal-2001] [Strachan-etal-2022]. Persecutory beliefs persist through worry, low self-confidence, poor sleep and safety behaviors that block disconfirmation [Freeman-2016]. In bipolar disorder, the integrative cognitive model proposes that extreme, often conflicting appraisals of internal state changes drive behaviors that push mood further up or down [Kelly-etal-2017a].

## Components

**Psychosis (Garety, Morrison, Freeman)**

| Component | What it means |
|---|---|
| Vulnerability and stress | Genetic and environmental risk, adversity, trauma, sleep loss, drugs [Freeman-2016] |
| Anomalous experiences | Intrusions, voices, perceptual changes; not pathological in themselves [Strachan-etal-2022] |
| Appraisal | Experiences judged as externally caused and threatening ("the neighbors are sending messages") [Strachan-etal-2022] |
| Reasoning biases | Jumping to conclusions, belief inflexibility, confirmation bias, external attribution [Strachan-etal-2022] |
| Negative schemas and emotion | Beliefs that self is weak and others hostile; anxiety and depression shape and fuel content [Smith-etal-2006] |
| Metacognitive beliefs | Beliefs about the voices or thoughts, such as "the voice is powerful and dangerous" [Strachan-etal-2022] |
| Safety behaviors and attention | Avoidance, hypervigilance, shouting back at voices, staying in [Strachan-etal-2022] [Freeman-2016] |

**Bipolar disorder (integrative cognitive model)**

| Component | What it means |
|---|---|
| Internal state change | Shifts in energy, arousal, mood, thought speed, often after sleep or routine disruption [Jones-2001] |
| Extreme personal appraisals | Positive ("when I'm full of energy I'm brilliant"), catastrophic ("any excitement means breakdown"), self-critical, loss of control [Kelly-etal-2017a] |
| Ascent behaviors | Efforts to raise or keep activation: chasing goals, staying up, stimulants [Kelly-etal-2017a] |
| Descent behaviors | Efforts to lower activation or low mood: withdrawal, rumination, shutting down [Kelly-etal-2017a] |
| Early experience and beliefs | Memories of past episodes, others' critical or controlling responses, shame [Kelly-etal-2017a] |

## How it keeps the problem going

- **Threat cycle (paranoia):** an ambiguous event (laughter on the bus) is appraised as threat; anxiety rises; worry rehearses the danger; the person escapes or stays in; no safety is ever learned, so conviction holds [Freeman-2016].
- **Voices cycle:** a voice is believed to be powerful; the person complies, argues or hides; distress and arousal increase voice activity; beliefs about power go untested [Strachan-etal-2022].
- **Self-esteem cycle:** low mood and negative self-beliefs go with harsher voice content and more severe, distressing persecutory beliefs, a cross-sectional link the models read as two-way [Smith-etal-2006].
- **Bipolar ascent cycle:** a lift in energy is read as "my true, creative self"; the person sleeps less and takes on more; activation climbs. Ascent behaviors partly mediated the link between positive appraisals of activated mood and activation in a diary study [PalmierClaus-etal-2016].
- **Bipolar descent cycle:** feeling flat is read as "I'm failing again"; withdrawal and rumination deepen depression [Kelly-etal-2017a].
- **Conflicting appraisals:** racing thoughts are both "genius" and "losing my mind," so the person alternates between speeding up and shutting down, adding instability [Kelly-etal-2017a].
- **Rhythm disruption:** irregular sleep and social routines destabilize mood; sleep disturbance is the most common early sign of mania (median 77%) [Jackson-etal-2003] [Jones-2001].

## Drawing it with the client

1. Start from a recent, specific episode the client chose: "Take me through Tuesday on the bus."
2. Write the situation, the experience (what was heard or felt), the meaning ("they're onto me"), feelings, body changes, and what they did.
3. Ask what happened next and draw the arrow back: "When you left, did you find out whether they were really talking about you?"
4. Add sleep, worry, drugs or mood as amplifiers around the cycle.
5. For bipolar, draw two loops: an up loop (appraisal of energy, ascent behaviors) and a down loop (appraisal of low mood, descent behaviors). Ask: "What do you tell yourself when you notice you're speeding up?"
6. Keep the client's explanation in the diagram; offer the stress or threat account as an alternative to test, not a correction.
7. Rate conviction and distress for each belief so change can be tracked.

## Treatment targets it implies

- **Psychosis:** reduce worry and arousal; improve sleep; build self-confidence; widen reasoning (consider alternatives, slow down conclusions); drop safety behaviors in behavioral experiments so safety can be learned [Freeman-2016] [Freeman-etal-2016]; reappraise beliefs about voice power.
- **Bipolar:** spot early internal state changes without catastrophizing; soften extreme positive and negative appraisals; replace ascent and descent behaviors with steadying ones; protect sleep and routine [Kelly-etal-2017a] [Jones-2001].
- **Both:** address trauma, self-esteem and substance use where they feed the cycle; link targets to the client's own goals.
