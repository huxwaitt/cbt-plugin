**Bottom line:** Family intervention is one of the most effective psychosocial treatments for preventing relapse in schizophrenia (odds ratio 0.35 at 1 year versus usual care) [Bighelli-etal-2021]. The United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends offering it to all families of people with psychosis, over 3 months to 1 year with at least 10 planned sessions [NICE-CG178]. In bipolar disorder, family-focused therapy over 21 sessions reduced relapse and improved medication adherence [Novick-Swartz-2019].

## When to use

- **Psychosis:** every family in close contact, at first episode and after relapse [NICE-CG178]. The American Psychiatric Association suggests family interventions for patients with ongoing family contact [Serra-etal-2023].
- **Bipolar disorder:** maintenance phase, especially after a recent episode or with high family conflict or criticism [Novick-Swartz-2019]. Family-focused therapy is a second-line add-on for depression and maintenance in the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) guideline [Yatham-etal-2018].
- When no relative or partner can take part, family therapy is not an option [VADoD-Bipolar-2023]; group psychoeducation keeps the advantage of a shared format [Miklowitz-etal-2021].

## How it works

Relatives often respond to symptoms with worry, criticism or taking over, which raises stress at home. Shared understanding, calmer communication and joint problem solving lower stress and help the family spot and act on early warning signs [Novick-Swartz-2019].

## Steps

Family-focused therapy for bipolar disorder runs in three phases over about 9 months [Novick-Swartz-2019]; psychosis family intervention follows a similar arc.

| Phase | Sessions (approximate) | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Engagement | 1 to 2 | Meet the family, hear each person's experience, agree goals and confidentiality |
| Psychoeducation | 3 to 9 | The illness, stress and vulnerability, medication, early warning signs, a shared relapse plan |
| Communication training | 10 to 16 | Expressing positive feelings, active listening, requesting changes, giving negative feedback calmly; role-play in session |
| Problem solving | 17 to 21 | Define a problem, brainstorm, choose, plan, review; topics such as money, chores, independence |

For psychosis, NICE specifies that family intervention should include the person with psychosis if practical, run over 3 months to 1 year with at least 10 planned sessions, and take account of whether the family prefers single-family or multi-family groups [NICE-CG178].

## Guidance

- If the client does not want family involved → respect that, explore the reasons, and revisit later; offer relatives general information that does not breach confidentiality. Because consent governs what you share (see `14-ethics-law.confidentiality-and-limits`).
- If a relative is highly critical ("he's just lazy") → validate their frustration, then link the behavior to negative symptoms or depression. Because reframing reduces blame and conflict (expert consensus).
- If the family takes over every decision → agree small areas where the client decides. Because overprotection undermines recovery and self-management (expert consensus).
- If conversations escalate in session → stop, slow turn-taking, and coach one skill at a time. Because communication training works through rehearsal [Novick-Swartz-2019].
- If there is violence, abuse or coercion at home → assess safety separately with each person, do not run joint sessions until it is safe, and follow `02-risk.safeguarding`. Because joint sessions can expose a person to retaliation (expert consensus).
- If the family is exhausted → offer carer support or a carers' group. Because the Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense guideline suggests caregiver support programs [VADoD-Bipolar-2023].

Example phrasings:
- "Everyone here wants things to go better. Can we each say what the last episode was like for us?"
- "Try telling her one specific thing she did this week that you appreciated."
- Avoid: "The family environment is causing the relapses."

## Common problems

- Running a single information session and calling it family intervention; the evidence is for structured courses.
- Sessions without the client, which can feel like conspiring.
- Skipping the shared relapse plan.

## Evidence

- Psychosis: family interventions (odds ratio 0.35), family psychoeducation (0.56) and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) at 0.45 all reduced relapse at 1 year in a network meta-analysis [Bighelli-etal-2021].
- Bipolar: family or conjoint therapy beat usual care on recurrence (odds ratio 0.30), and skills-based psychoeducation in family or group format beat individual format (odds ratio 0.12, wide interval) [Miklowitz-etal-2021]. Families high in expressed emotion benefited most from family-focused therapy [Novick-Swartz-2019].
- Evidence strength: guideline-recommended for psychosis; supported by randomized trials in bipolar disorder.
