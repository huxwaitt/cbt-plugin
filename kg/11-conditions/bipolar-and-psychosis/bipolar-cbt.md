**Bottom line:** Relapse-prevention cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for bipolar disorder typically runs about 20 sessions over 6 months plus boosters, alongside mood stabilizers [Yatham-etal-2018]; the positive Lam trial averaged 14 sessions [Lam-etal-2003]. Its core is a personal relapse signature: the client's own early signs of mania and depression, linked to a graded action plan agreed with family and prescriber. At least 80% of people can name one or more early signs, and sleep disturbance is the most common early sign of mania [Jackson-etal-2003].

## When to use

- Between episodes or in mild depression, with medication in place [Lam-etal-2003].
- Frequent relapses, poor recognition of early signs, irregular routines, or residual depression.
- Not in acute mania; stabilize first.
- Fewer prior episodes may predict more benefit; in one trial a post hoc analysis found CBT helped people with under 12 prior episodes but not those with more [Scott-etal-2006].

## How it works

Clients learn to see mood changes early, respond with steadying actions, keep sleep and routines regular, and reduce extreme appraisals of their states [Novick-Swartz-2019] [Kelly-etal-2017a]. In a component network meta-analysis, cognitive restructuring and daily rhythm regulation were the elements most associated with lower depression [Miklowitz-etal-2021].

## Steps

| Sessions | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | Life chart of past episodes, triggers and treatments; goals; baseline mood measures |
| 2 to 4 | Psychoeducation: the illness, medication, the role of sleep and stress; mood diary started [Novick-Swartz-2019] |
| 3 to 6 | Formulation of up and down cycles; daily mood, sleep and activity monitoring |
| 5 to 8 | Relapse signature for mania and depression (below) and the action plan |
| 6 to 12 | Routine regulation: fixed wake time, meals, activity; plan for disruptions such as shift work or travel |
| 8 to 14 | Cognitive work: depressive thoughts with thought records; hypomanic thinking ("I don't need sleep, my ideas are too good to wait") with delay rules |
| 12 to 16 | Long-term issues: medication ambivalence, stigma, self-esteem, goal striving at a sustainable pace |
| Final and boosters | Written staying-well plan; booster sessions over the following 6 months [Lam-etal-2003] |

**Building the relapse signature**
1. Pick the last manic and the last depressive episode. Work backwards: "What was the very first thing that changed?"
2. List signs in order (early, middle, late). Common early mania signs: less sleep, more plans, faster speech, irritability, more spending. Early depression signs: withdrawal, fatigue, poor concentration.
3. Ask a relative what they noticed first, with consent.
4. Write a three-zone plan: **green** (usual routine), **amber** (early signs: protect sleep, cut stimulation and commitments, delay big decisions 48 hours, call the prescriber), **red** (late signs: contact the team or crisis line, involve family, hand over cards or keys if agreed earlier).
5. Rehearse it, and review it after any wobble.

Teaching people to recognize early signs and seek treatment extended time to manic relapse in a randomized trial (25th centile 65 versus 17 weeks), without effect on depressive relapse [Perry-etal-1999].

## Guidance

- If the client values highs ("it's the only time I'm me") → explore pros and cons of the high openly before setting rules. Because positive appraisals of activated states drive ascent behaviors and ambivalence [Kelly-etal-2017a].
- If the client checks every mood flicker anxiously → reframe monitoring as noticing patterns over days, not minutes. Because constant vigilance can feed catastrophic appraisals of normal mood shifts [Kelly-etal-2017a].
- If sleep drops two nights running → treat it as an amber sign and contact the prescriber within 1 to 2 days (expert consensus). Because sleep disturbance is the most common early mania sign [Jackson-etal-2003].
- If the client is depressed and suicidal → complete a full risk assessment and a written safety plan, and escalate the same day if there is intent or a plan (United States: 988 or 911; United Kingdom: 999, National Health Service 111 or Samaritans 116 123; see `02-risk.crisis-response`). Because over 70% of suicide attempts and deaths in bipolar disorder happen in depressive phases [Yatham-etal-2018].
- If insomnia persists between episodes → use bipolar-adapted CBT for insomnia with time in bed no less than 6.5 hours. Because sleep loss can trigger hypomania [Harvey-etal-2015].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's be detectives about your last high. What did your partner notice before you did?"
- "When you notice your amber signs, what's one thing future-you would want you to do?"
- Avoid: "You must never get excited again."

## Common problems

- A signature too generic to act on; use the client's own words and examples.
- Plans not shared with family or the prescriber.
- Stopping without boosters.
- Activity scheduling pushed too hard in a client near hypomania (see `bipolar-and-psychosis.pitfall-sleep-loss-and-activation`).

## Evidence

- In 103 people with bipolar I and frequent relapses, cognitive therapy (about 14 sessions plus 2 boosters) led to fewer episodes, fewer days ill and fewer admissions over 12 months [Lam-etal-2003], and 110 fewer days in episodes over 30 months, though the relapse effect was mainly in the first year [Lam-etal-2005].
- A large pragmatic trial of 22 sessions found no overall effect on recurrence [Scott-etal-2006].
- Evidence strength: randomized trials and meta-analyses, mixed results; the United Kingdom National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends manualized bipolar-specific psychological interventions [NICE-CG185].
