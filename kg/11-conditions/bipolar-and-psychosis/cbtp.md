**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis (CBTp) helps clients link thoughts, feelings and actions to their experiences, re-evaluate the beliefs that make those experiences distressing, and cope differently, over at least 16 one-to-one sessions according to the United Kingdom National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [NICE-CG178]. Its stages are engagement, normalizing psychoeducation, shared formulation with targeted work, and relapse prevention [HQO-CBTp-HTA-2018]. Distress and functioning are the targets, not the removal of every belief.

## When to use

- Distressing voices, persecutory or other unusual beliefs, or negative symptoms in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, with or after medication.
- Persistent persecutory delusions despite medication: a modular threat-belief approach has the strongest recent trial [Freeman-etal-2021].
- Command hallucinations with a history of compliance [Birchwood-etal-2014].
- Not a substitute for urgent care when the client is acutely unsafe.

## How it works

Experiences become distressing when appraised as external, threatening and uncontrollable; worry, low self-confidence, poor sleep, reasoning biases and safety behaviors keep the appraisal alive [Freeman-2016] [Strachan-etal-2022]. Therapy reduces those maintaining factors and then helps the person learn safety through real-world tests [Freeman-2016].

## Steps

| Sessions | Focus | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Engagement | Client's goals ("feel safer," "go to the shops"), what helps already, baseline ratings of conviction and distress. Flexible venue and length |
| 2 to 4 | Normalizing psychoeducation | Psychoeducation that normalizes symptoms [HQO-CBTp-HTA-2018]; for example, stress, sleep loss, drugs and trauma can produce unusual experiences in many people (expert consensus) |
| 3 to 6 | Shared formulation | One recent episode mapped as a cycle; agree one or two targets |
| 5 to 14 | Targeted modules | Pick from the menu below, in the client's order [Freeman-etal-2016] |
| 12 to 16+ | Staying well | Early signs, triggers, coping plan, who to call; written blueprint [Freeman-etal-2016] |

Module menu (Feeling Safe Programme, about 20 sessions over 6 months) [Freeman-etal-2016]:
- **Worry:** postponement, noticing "what if" chains, reducing time spent rehearsing threat.
- **Sleep:** regular wake time, wind-down, light exposure.
- **Self-confidence:** positive memories and qualities, self-compassion.
- **Reasoning:** slowing conclusions, generating alternative explanations, rating each.
- **Safety learning:** list safety behaviors, predict what will happen without them, test in real settings, review.

For voices, examine beliefs about the voice's power, identity and control; test compliance and resistance beliefs gradually (expert consensus, consistent with [Birchwood-etal-2014]).

## Guidance

- If the client is convinced the belief is true → explore its impact and evidence with curiosity, not debate: "What would you need to see to feel a bit safer?" Because direct confrontation hardens conviction and harms engagement (see `bipolar-and-psychosis.pitfall-arguing-with-delusions`).
- If the client is fearful of an exposure → agree a small, specific prediction and test it together, possibly with the therapist present. Because safety is relearned by entering feared situations without safety behaviors [Freeman-2016].
- If a voice commands harm → assess risk the same day and involve the psychiatric team (imminent danger: 911 or 988 in the United States, 999 in the United Kingdom), then work on beliefs about the voice's power and the consequences of not complying. Because cognitive therapy for command hallucinations cut full compliance at 18 months from 46% to 28% [Birchwood-etal-2014].
- If worry dominates → start with the worry or sleep module before belief work. Because these maintaining factors fuel the delusion [Freeman-2016].
- If the client fears the therapist is part of the plot → name it, and offer transparency: written agendas, shared notes, choice of venue. Because trust has to come before safety learning [Freeman-etal-2016].
- If negative symptoms limit sessions → shorten sessions, use activity scheduling and concrete goals. Because motivation and energy limit standard pacing (expert consensus).

Example phrasings:
- "It sounds frightening to feel watched all the time. Can we look at what that's like day to day?"
- "Some people think it's the neighbors, others think it's stress playing tricks. Shall we keep both on the table and test them?"
- "What do you think would happen if you didn't answer the voice back tonight?"
- Avoid: "That's a delusion, it isn't real."
- Avoid: "Yes, they probably are following you."

## Common problems

- Starting belief work before engagement and formulation.
- Losing the client's goals in symptom talk; return to "feel safer, happier, more active" [Freeman-etal-2016].
- Too few sessions; NICE expects at least 16 [NICE-CG178].
- Missing trauma, substance use or depression that drive the cycle.

## Evidence

- Effects are small: target symptoms 0.40, about 0.22 in rigorous trials [Wykes-etal-2008]. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) beat other therapies for positive symptoms with g 0.16, an effect that lost significance when researcher allegiance was controlled [Turner-etal-2014].
- Feeling Safe versus befriending: delusion conviction fell more (Cohen's d -0.86) in 130 people with persistent persecutory delusions [Freeman-etal-2021].
- In 74 people not taking antipsychotics, cognitive therapy reduced symptoms more than usual care [Morrison-etal-2014].
- Evidence strength: guideline-recommended, supported by randomized trials, with modest effect sizes.
