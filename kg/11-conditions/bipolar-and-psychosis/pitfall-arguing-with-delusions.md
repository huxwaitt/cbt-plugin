**Bottom line:** Debating the truth of a delusion recruits the reasoning biases that maintain it, such as belief inflexibility and confirmation bias [Strachan-etal-2022], and agreeing with it confirms the threat. Cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis (CBTp) works on current safety, distress and goals: it builds trust first, then lets the client gather their own evidence through real-world tests [Freeman-etal-2016].

## What it looks like

- Long sessions listing reasons why the neighbors could not be spying.
- The client brings more "proof" each week, and the therapist counters each item.
- The client says "You're just like the rest of them" and misses appointments.
- Or the opposite: the therapist says "That sounds like they're out to get you" to avoid a rupture, and the client stays in more.
- Goals drift from "go to the shops" to "prove I'm not being followed."

## Why it happens

- Standard cognitive restructuring is applied too early and too hard.
- Therapists feel they must correct "false" beliefs, or fear that exploring them will reinforce them.
- Reasoning biases such as jumping to conclusions and seeking confirming evidence make direct challenge backfire [Strachan-etal-2022].
- Threat beliefs are kept going by worry and safety behaviors, not by a lack of counter-arguments [Freeman-2016].

## The fix

- If the client presents new "evidence" → reflect the fear, then ask about impact: "That sounds frightening. How is it affecting your days?" Because distress and functioning, not the belief's truth, are the treatment targets [Freeman-etal-2016].
- If you notice you are debating → name it and change direction: "I notice we're going back and forth. Could we look at what would help you feel safer this week?" Because debate engages belief inflexibility [Strachan-etal-2022].
- If the client asks "Do you believe me?" → be honest and respectful: "I believe this is really happening to you and is frightening. I'm not sure about the explanation yet. Can we look at it together?" Because collusion strengthens the threat belief and lying damages trust (expert consensus).
- If trust is established → set up a small behavioral experiment that tests a prediction while dropping one safety behavior. Because safety is relearned through experience [Freeman-2016].
- If the belief drives plans to confront, escape or harm the supposed persecutor → assess risk to self and others that session and involve the psychiatric team (see `02-risk.harm-to-others`). Because acting on threat beliefs is the main danger (expert consensus).
- If conviction stays high → focus on the past week and current safety rather than whether the belief was ever true. Because the Feeling Safe approach aims to relearn safety now by entering feared situations, not to settle the past [Freeman-etal-2016].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's keep two explanations on the table and see which one fits what happens this week."
- "What would you expect to happen if you walked to the shop without checking behind you?"
- Avoid: "There's no way anyone could be tapping your phone."
- Avoid: "It does sound like they're targeting you."

## Sources note

The emphasis on engagement and normalizing before belief work reflects the staged structure of CBTp [HQO-CBTp-HTA-2018]. The current-safety focus and gradual safety learning come from the Feeling Safe Programme [Freeman-etal-2016], which outperformed befriending in a randomized trial [Freeman-etal-2021]. The harm of direct confrontation is supported by the reasoning-bias models but has not been tested head to head; it is expert consensus.
