**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is an add-on to medication and team care in both conditions, not a replacement. For psychosis, the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends offering individual CBT for psychosis (CBTp) over at least 16 planned sessions plus family intervention [NICE-CG178] [BurgessBarr-etal-2023], and the American Psychiatric Association recommends CBTp [Serra-etal-2023]. For bipolar disorder, structured manualized psychological therapy reduces recurrence (odds ratio 0.56 versus control) [Miklowitz-etal-2021].

## When to use

- **Psychosis:** first episode, later acute episodes, recovery and persisting symptoms. NICE says CBTp can start in the acute phase, including inpatient settings [NICE-CG178].
- **People who decline antipsychotics:** NICE advises that psychological therapy works better with medication; if the person still declines, offer family intervention and CBTp and review within a set period (1 month or less) [NICE-CG178]. Evidence for CBT without antipsychotics is low or very low certainty (4 small trials) [Bighelli-etal-2024].
- **Bipolar depression:** a bipolar-specific psychological intervention or a high-intensity depression therapy such as CBT [NICE-CG185]. Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) rates CBT second-line add-on for acute bipolar depression [Yatham-etal-2018].
- **Bipolar maintenance:** a structured, manualized individual, group or family intervention to prevent relapse or for persisting symptoms between episodes [NICE-CG185]. Psychoeducation is first-line; CBT and family-focused therapy are second-line [Yatham-etal-2018].
- **Not during acute mania:** no evidence supports CBT for mania [Yatham-etal-2018]; trials recruited people who were not acutely manic [VADoD-Bipolar-2023].

## How it works

CBTp targets the appraisals, worry, reasoning and safety behaviors that turn experiences into distress (see `bipolar-and-psychosis.cbt-model`). Bipolar CBT builds illness understanding, mood monitoring, early warning detection, regular routines and cognitive skills [Novick-Swartz-2019].

## Steps

| Phase | Psychosis (CBTp) | Bipolar (relapse prevention CBT) |
|---|---|---|
| Engagement and assessment (sessions 1 to 4) | Goals in the client's terms, normalizing, baseline ratings | Mood timeline, life chart, goals, baseline measures |
| Formulation (3 to 6) | Shared maintenance cycle of one key experience | Up and down cycles, triggers, appraisals |
| Change work (5 to 14) | Coping strategies, worry and sleep work, behavioral experiments with threat beliefs, voice beliefs, self-esteem | Mood and sleep monitoring, routine regulation, cognitive restructuring for depression, prodrome coping |
| Consolidation (last 2 to 4) | Staying-well plan, early signs | Written relapse signature and action plan, boosters |

Typical dose: CBTp at least 16 planned sessions [NICE-CG178]; bipolar CBT typically about 20 sessions over 6 months, often with boosters, with trials ranging from 6 to 25 [Yatham-etal-2018] [Novick-Swartz-2019].

Team elements: first-episode psychosis care in a coordinated specialty care program improved quality of life and engagement, most when untreated psychosis was shorter [Kane-etal-2016]. Family work reduces relapse in both conditions (see `bipolar-and-psychosis.family-work`).

## Guidance

- If a client with psychosis is not yet stable on medication → still offer engagement and coping-focused CBTp. Because NICE allows CBTp to start in the acute phase [NICE-CG178].
- If a client with bipolar disorder is acutely manic → defer cognitive work, liaise with the prescriber, and keep contact brief and supportive. Because there is no evidence for CBT in mania [Yatham-etal-2018].
- If a client has had many prior bipolar episodes → still offer a psychological intervention but set modest relapse goals and consider family or group psychoeducation. Because in one large trial, a post hoc analysis found CBT helped those with under 12 prior episodes and did worse than usual care in those with more [Scott-etal-2006].
- If a client with bipolar disorder has residual anxiety between episodes → offer CBT targeting the anxiety. Because the 2023 Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guideline suggests it [VADoD-Bipolar-2023].
- If the client asks which therapy is best for bipolar disorder → explain that CBT, family therapy, interpersonal and social rhythm therapy and longer psychoeducation are all reasonable choices. Because evidence does not rank one above the others [VADoD-Bipolar-2023].

## Common problems

- Only about 24% of people with psychosis received CBTp in routine care across 9 countries (41% in early intervention services) [BurgessBarr-etal-2023].
- Offering CBT instead of medication review when the client is relapsing.
- Unstructured "support" labeled as CBT; NICE expects a manual-based protocol [NICE-CG178].

## Evidence

- **Psychosis:** effects are small. Overall symptoms g about 0.33, smaller in trials with masked assessors (about 0.15) [Jauhar-etal-2014]; target symptoms 0.40, and about 0.22 in rigorous trials [Wykes-etal-2008]. In first-episode psychosis, standardized mean difference -0.27 for overall symptoms, low certainty [Mayer-etal-2024]. CBT reduced relapse at 1 year (odds ratio 0.45) [Bighelli-etal-2021].
- **Guideline status:** NICE CG178 (2014) and the American Psychiatric Association schizophrenia guideline (2020) both recommend CBTp [NICE-CG178] [Serra-etal-2023]. The American Psychiatric Association bipolar guideline is a legacy document [APA-Psychiatry-Guidelines-Site]; use NICE CG185, CANMAT 2018 and VA/DoD 2023.
- **Bipolar:** CBT reduced relapse (odds ratio 0.506) with mild to moderate effects on depression and mania in 19 trials [Chiang-etal-2017]. The VA/DoD guideline suggests (weak for) psychotherapy added to medication [VADoD-Bipolar-2023].
