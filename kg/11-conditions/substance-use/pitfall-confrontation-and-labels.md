**Bottom line:** Arguing a client into admitting a problem backfires. A confrontational counseling style produces more resistance, and in one trial the more the therapist confronted, the more the client drank a year later [Miller-etal-1993]. Labels such as "substance abuser" also make clinicians more punitive [Kelly-Westerhoff-2010]. Replace confrontation with reflection, permission-based information and the client's own reasons.

## What it looks like

- The therapist lists the harms of use and the client answers each one with "Yes, but".
- Words such as "denial", "addict", "alcoholic", "clean" and "dirty" appear in session or in notes.
- The therapist pushes for an admission ("You need to accept you're an alcoholic") before any work starts.
- The client starts minimizing amounts, misses sessions, or leaves treatment.
- In a recording, the therapist asks many closed questions, gives unasked-for advice and rarely reflects.

## Why it happens

- The therapist sees clear danger and feels responsible for making the client see it too.
- Older addiction treatment traditions treated confrontation of denial as necessary.
- Arguing feels active; listening to reasons for using can feel like collusion.
- Defensiveness is read as a trait of "addicts" rather than a reaction to the conversation. In a meta-analysis, therapist behavior inconsistent with motivational interviewing (MI) went with more client sustain talk, and sustain talk predicted worse outcome [Magill-etal-2018].

## The fix

- If the client defends their use → reflect the defense and the concern together. Because confrontation raises resistance, which predicted poorer outcomes at one year [Miller-etal-1993].
- If you want to share a risk → ask permission first, give it briefly, and ask what the client makes of it. Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks for a supportive rather than argumentative stance [NICE-CG115].
- If the client says something in favor of change → reflect it and ask for more. Because therapist MI-consistent skills go with more change talk (r = .55) [Magill-etal-2018].
- If you write notes or referral letters → describe behavior and diagnosis in person-first terms. Because "substance abuser" made trained clinicians judge the person more culpable and deserving of punishment [Kelly-Westerhoff-2010].
- If a session has become a debate → name it and step back: "I've been pushing, and that's not helping. What matters most to you about this?"

Example phrasings:
- "You don't see your drinking as the main problem, and your partner does. That puts you in a tough spot."
- "Would it be OK if I shared what the screening shows, and you tell me what fits?"
- Avoid: "Until you admit you're an addict, nothing will change." It demands a label instead of change.

## Sources note

The harm of confrontation rests on one small randomized trial with 42 problem drinkers [Miller-etal-1993] and a process meta-analysis of 36 studies [Magill-etal-2018]. The labeling effect comes from a randomized vignette study of 516 clinicians [Kelly-Westerhoff-2010]. NICE's stance is guideline consensus [NICE-CG115].
