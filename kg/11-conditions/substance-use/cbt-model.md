**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) explains continued use as learned behavior kept going by its short-term payoff: triggers set off expectations and cravings, use brings quick relief or pleasure, and the later costs arrive too late to steer behavior [Carroll-Kiluk-2017; Larimer-etal-1999]. Map this as a functional analysis of real episodes, and add the Beck cognitive model when beliefs about using carry the episode [Beck-etal-1993]. Each link in the chain is a treatment target.

## Components

**Functional analysis** (the behavioral core of manual-based CBT for substance use) [Carroll-etal-2008; McHugh-etal-2010; VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021]:

| Link | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Place, people, time, mood, body state, money, cues such as a bottle or a street |
| Thoughts | What the client expected using would do; permission-giving thoughts |
| Feelings and urges | Emotion before use; craving strength 0 to 10 |
| Behavior | What was used, how much, how obtained |
| Short-term consequences | Relief, pleasure, belonging, sleep, escape from withdrawal |
| Long-term consequences | Health, money, relationships, work, guilt, legal problems |

**Beck cognitive model** [Beck-etal-1993; Liese-Beck-2022]: a trigger activates addiction-related beliefs. **Anticipatory** beliefs expect a reward ("It'll make the party fun"). **Relief-oriented** beliefs expect escape ("I can't cope without it"). **Permissive** beliefs license use ("Just one won't hurt", "I deserve it"). Automatic thoughts and craving follow, then the client focuses on getting the substance, and uses.

**Craving.** In the relapse prevention tradition, urges are driven both by conditioned cues once paired with use and by expectations of immediate pleasure [Larimer-etal-1999].

## How it keeps the problem going

- **Positive reinforcement.** Use reliably produces pleasure or social ease, so it is repeated. Some of alcohol's social benefits are expectancy effects rather than drug effects [Larimer-etal-1999].
- **Negative reinforcement.** Use removes distress, boredom or withdrawal quickly. This is the same escape logic as avoidance in anxiety, and it blocks learning other ways to cope.
- **Cue conditioning.** Places, people, times and moods paired with use come to trigger craving on their own [Larimer-etal-1999].
- **Delay of costs.** Negative consequences come hours or months later, so they lose to the immediate payoff unless made vivid.
- **Narrowing life.** Activities that once gave pleasure drop away and use becomes the main source of reward, which raises its value further [Larimer-etal-1999].
- **Consequences feed triggers.** Hangovers, conflict, debt and shame create the negative emotions that start the next episode.
- **Beliefs.** Permissive and relief beliefs go unchallenged because use "works" in the moment [Beck-etal-1993].

## Drawing it with the client

1. Pick one recent, specific episode, ideally from this week. Ask the client to tell it slowly, like a film.
2. Fill the six links above in their words, on paper or a whiteboard. Start at use and work backward to the trigger, then forward to consequences.
3. Separate short-term from long-term consequences in two columns. Ask "What did it do for you in the first hour?" before "And the next day?"
4. Mark the thoughts that gave permission. Ask "What did you say to yourself just before you decided?"
5. Repeat with a second episode and a time the client did not use despite a trigger. Look for the common pattern and what helped.
6. Draw the loop: long-term consequences arrow back to the next trigger.
7. Ask the client what the map suggests to change first.

Example phrasings:
- "It sounds like drinking does a real job for you at 6 p.m. Let's be fair to it and write down what it gives you, then what it costs."
- "Where on this map could you have done something different, even slightly?"
- Avoid: "So you can see this is destroying your life." Let the client draw that conclusion from their own columns.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Link | Target and method |
|---|---|
| Triggers | Stimulus control: remove supplies, change routes, plan for high-risk times [Larimer-etal-1999] |
| Expectations and permission | Cognitive restructuring of anticipatory, relief and permissive beliefs; a decision matrix of immediate and delayed effects [Beck-etal-1993; Larimer-etal-1999] |
| Craving | Coping with craving, including urge surfing [Carroll-etal-2008; Larimer-etal-1999] |
| Behavior | Refusal skills, problem solving, decision making [Carroll-etal-2008] |
| Short-term payoff | New ways to get the same function: emotion regulation, sleep, social contact |
| Narrowed life | Activity scheduling and rewarding drug-free activities [Larimer-etal-1999] |
| Lapse response | See `substance-use.relapse-prevention-model` |

The model also guides adjuncts. Contingency management adds an immediate reward for verified abstinence [McHugh-etal-2010], and medication can blunt craving or withdrawal (see `substance-use.addiction-medications`).
