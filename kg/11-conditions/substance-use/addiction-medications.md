**Bottom line:** For opioid use disorder, staying on buprenorphine or methadone sharply lowers death from any cause and from overdose [Sordo-etal-2017], and guidelines strongly recommend it [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021]. For moderate to severe alcohol use disorder, naltrexone and other medications are recommended alongside psychological treatment [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021; NICE-CG115]. Few people receive them: in 2024, 17.0 percent of people in the United States (US) with opioid use disorder and 2.5 percent with alcohol use disorder did [SAMHSA-NSDUH-2024]. A cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) therapist does not prescribe, but should support these treatments and never undermine them.

## What it is

Three groups of medicines matter in substance use care:
- **Withdrawal management:** medicines that make stopping safe, such as benzodiazepines for moderate to severe alcohol withdrawal [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
- **Maintenance or relapse prevention:** medicines taken for months or years to reduce craving, block effects, or replace an illicit opioid with a safer, prescribed one.
- **Overdose reversal:** naloxone, which can save lives after an opioid overdose [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].

## Why it matters in CBT

- CBT plus medication beat usual care plus medication (g = 0.18 to 0.28), but CBT was no better than another specific therapy alongside medication, and adding CBT on top of usual care and medication gave mixed results [Ray-etal-2020]. Medication and therapy are partners, not rivals.
- Leaving opioid treatment is dangerous. Overdose deaths were 12.7 per 1,000 person-years out of methadone treatment versus 2.6 in treatment, and risk is high in the weeks right after leaving [Sordo-etal-2017]. Completing an inpatient detoxification without ongoing treatment also left people at overdose risk from lost tolerance [Strang-etal-2003].
- A therapist's attitude to medication shapes adherence. Treating methadone as "still using" pushes clients toward stopping.

## Key details

US Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) recommendations [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021]:

| Condition | Recommended | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Opioid use disorder | Buprenorphine with naloxone, or methadone through an accredited opioid treatment program | Strong for |
| Opioid use disorder | Extended-release injectable naltrexone | Weak for |
| Opioid use disorder | Withdrawal management alone, without ongoing medication | Strong against |
| Moderate to severe alcohol use disorder | Naltrexone (oral or injectable) or topiramate | Strong for |
| Moderate to severe alcohol use disorder | Acamprosate or disulfiram | Weak for |
| Alcohol use disorder, first line unsuitable | Gabapentin | Weak for |
| Benzodiazepine withdrawal | Gradual taper | Strong for |
| Cannabis, cocaine or methamphetamine use disorder | No medication recommended (insufficient evidence) | Neither |

- The United Kingdom's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) says to consider acamprosate or oral naltrexone, combined with an individual psychological intervention, after withdrawal for moderate and severe dependence, and for milder cases that do not respond to therapy alone [NICE-CG115].
- The American Psychiatric Association's current alcohol guideline (2018) is about pharmacological treatment [APA-Psychiatry-Guidelines-Site].
- Use of another substance should not automatically end medication treatment for opioid or alcohol use disorder [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
- Who may prescribe these medicines depends on license and setting; methadone for opioid use disorder is dispensed through accredited programs [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].

## In the session

- If a client on buprenorphine or methadone says they want to come off it → explore reasons, involve the prescriber, and explain overdose risk after stopping. Because mortality rises after leaving treatment [Sordo-etal-2017].
- If a client with opioid use disorder plans detoxification only → discuss ongoing medication with the prescriber. Because withdrawal management without ongoing medication carries high relapse and overdose risk [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
- If anyone in the client's life uses opioids → make sure they have naloxone and know how to use it. Because naloxone should be offered to everyone at high risk of opioid overdose [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
- If a client with moderate to severe alcohol use disorder has never been offered medication → suggest a medical review. Because medication plus therapy is the guideline standard [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021; NICE-CG115].
- If a client misses doses → treat it like homework non-completion: explore barriers, link to cues and routines, problem-solve.

Example phrasings:
- "Methadone is a treatment, the same way insulin is. What worries you about staying on it?"
- "Before you change your dose, can we talk it through with your prescriber together?"
- Avoid: "You're not really clean while you're on that." It is inaccurate and raises overdose risk if the client stops.
