**Bottom line:** Stopping is not always the safe option. Alcohol and sedative withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium [ASAM-Alcohol-Withdrawal-2020], and people who lose opioid tolerance after detoxification, prison or leaving treatment face a spike in overdose death [Strang-etal-2003; Sordo-etal-2017]. Check withdrawal and overdose risk before agreeing any plan to stop, and again at every transition.

## What it looks like

- A client who drinks heavily every day agrees in session to "quit from tomorrow", with no medical plan.
- A client is praised for coming off benzodiazepines "cold turkey".
- A client finishes inpatient opioid detoxification and the discharge plan has no medication, naloxone or warning about tolerance.
- Morning shakes, sweating and anxiety are logged as an anxiety disorder.
- No one asks whether the client mixes alcohol, opioids and sedatives.

## Why it happens

- Abstinence is treated as the goal and assumed to be safe.
- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) training rarely covers withdrawal medicine, so therapists may not ask.
- Overdose after detoxification is counterintuitive: in one cohort, all overdose deaths in the following months were among people who had completed detoxification and the program [Strang-etal-2003].

## The fix

- If the client drinks over 15 United Kingdom (UK) units a day or scores 20 or more on the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test → arrange assessment for assisted withdrawal before they stop. Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) sets this threshold [NICE-CG115].
- If there is a history of withdrawal seizures or delirium, many prior withdrawals, sedative dependence, older age or serious illness → refer for medically managed withdrawal. Because these raise the risk of complicated withdrawal [ASAM-Alcohol-Withdrawal-2020].
- If the client is shaky, sweating or newly anxious after cutting down → arrange medical assessment the same day. If they are confused, hallucinating or have a seizure → call 911 (UK: 999). Because withdrawal usually begins 6 to 24 hours after the last drink, seizures can start within 8 hours and peak around 24 hours, and delirium can appear up to 3 to 5 days later [ASAM-Alcohol-Withdrawal-2020].
- If a client wants to stop benzodiazepines → route it through the prescriber as a gradual taper. Because guidelines strongly recommend gradual tapering [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
- If a client with opioid use disorder is about to complete detoxification, leave prison or stop medication → warn that a usual dose can now be fatal, and make sure they carry naloxone. Because overdose mortality rises after loss of tolerance and after leaving treatment [Strang-etal-2003; Sordo-etal-2017; VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
- If the client mixes alcohol with opioids → treat both and name the combined overdose risk. Because NICE notes increased mortality from the combination [NICE-CG115].
- If you assess or plan any stop → record what was asked, what was found and who was contacted. Because NICE includes unplanned withdrawal in risk assessment [NICE-CG115].

Example phrasings:
- "Because you drink every day, stopping suddenly could make you seriously unwell. Let's get a medical plan in place first."
- "After weeks without opioids, your body can't handle what it used to. If you do use, use much less, never alone, and have naloxone."
- Avoid: "Great, just stop tomorrow." Safe for many, dangerous for some.

## Sources note

Withdrawal timing and risk factors come from the American Society of Addiction Medicine guideline [ASAM-Alcohol-Withdrawal-2020]; thresholds from NICE [NICE-CG115]. The loss-of-tolerance finding is from a small cohort of 137 [Strang-etal-2003], supported by a meta-analysis of 19 cohorts [Sordo-etal-2017].
