**Bottom line:** In Marlatt and Gordon's relapse prevention (RP) model, relapse is a process, not an event. A high-risk situation meets a weak coping response, confidence drops, positive expectations of using rise, a first lapse follows, and self-blame for that lapse (the abstinence violation effect) turns it into relapse [Larimer-etal-1999]. Lifestyle imbalance and small "apparently irrelevant" choices set up the situation. Each step is a place to intervene, and RP-based treatment reduces substance use with a small effect and improves functioning more [Hendershot-etal-2011].

## Components

**Immediate determinants** [Larimer-etal-1999]:
- **High-risk situations.** Negative emotional states carry the highest relapse rates; together with interpersonal conflict they triggered over half of relapse episodes in Marlatt's analysis. Social pressure contributed to over 20 percent. Positive emotions, substance cues, testing personal control and plain craving also count.
- **Coping response.** A behavioral (leaving) or cognitive (self-talk) response in the moment. The response, not the situation, decides whether a lapse happens.
- **Self-efficacy.** Confidence that one can handle this specific situation. Coping well raises it; failing lowers it.
- **Outcome expectancies.** Expecting immediate benefit from use ("It'll calm me down") while discounting delayed costs.
- **Abstinence violation effect.** After a lapse, blaming a stable, global, internal cause ("I have no willpower") brings guilt and giving up. Blaming a specific, changeable coping failure leads to learning.

**Covert antecedents** [Larimer-etal-1999]:
- **Lifestyle imbalance** between "shoulds" and "wants", which breeds a sense of being owed ("I deserve a drink").
- **Urges and cravings**, driven by conditioned cues and expected pleasure.
- **Rationalization, denial and apparently irrelevant decisions (AIDs):** small choices, each seemingly harmless, that move the person toward risk. Marlatt's example is buying liquor "in case guests stop by".

**The dynamic revision.** Witkiewitz and Marlatt recast relapse as nonlinear: stable "tonic" risks (family history, dependence severity, long-held expectancies, global self-efficacy) set a threshold, and fast-changing "phasic" factors (craving, mood, a dip in self-efficacy or motivation) tip a person over it in the moment [Witkiewitz-Marlatt-2004; Hendershot-etal-2011]. Small changes can produce sudden large shifts in either direction.

## How it keeps the problem going

- Each lapse handled with self-blame lowers self-efficacy, which makes the next high-risk situation harder.
- Relapse brings back the stress and imbalance that create new high-risk situations.
- A rigid all-or-nothing goal raises the stakes of a single slip. In one large study, people with stronger disease-model beliefs and stronger commitment to absolute abstinence were most likely to relapse fully after a lapse [Larimer-etal-1999].
- Avoidance of all risk without practice leaves coping untested, so confidence never builds.

## Drawing it with the client

Use a recent lapse, a near miss, or a "relapse fantasy" about how it could happen [Larimer-etal-1999].
1. **Work backward.** "What was going on in the days before? What small choices put you there?" Record covert antecedents and AIDs.
2. **Name the high-risk situation**: place, people, mood, time.
3. **Rate the urge** 0 to 10.
4. **Coping.** "What did you try? What made it hard?"
5. **Confidence and expectations.** "How sure were you that you could handle it? What did you expect using would do?"
6. **After the first use.** "What did you tell yourself? How did you feel?" This shows the abstinence violation effect.
7. **Draw the alternate route**: the same map with an effective coping response, leading to higher confidence. Marlatt called this a relapse road map [Larimer-etal-1999].

Example phrasings:
- "A slip is information. What does this one tell us about the situation and what you needed?"
- "On a scale of 0 to 10, how confident are you that you could handle that same evening next week?"
- Avoid: "You've thrown away three months." It invites exactly the self-blame that turns a lapse into relapse.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Step | Intervention [Larimer-etal-1999] |
|---|---|
| Covert antecedents | Daily want-should log; rewarding activities; stimulus control |
| High-risk situations | Self-monitoring; list and rank situations; early warning signs |
| Coping | Skills training and rehearsal (refusal, assertiveness, relaxation); role-play |
| Self-efficacy | Small achievable goals; collaborative stance; feedback on success |
| Expectancies | Education and a decision matrix of immediate and delayed effects |
| Urges | Urge surfing: watch the urge rise and fall like a wave without acting |
| Lapse | Written lapse plan: stop, leave safely, call the therapist, analyze triggers |
| Abstinence violation effect | Reframe the lapse as a learning mistake about one situation |

**Evidence.** The multisite Relapse Replication and Extension Project found Marlatt's relapse-situation categories only moderately reliable and modestly predictive, but how people coped, not mere exposure to risk, predicted relapse [Larimer-etal-1999]. A 1999 meta-analysis of 26 RP studies with 9,504 participants, summarized by Hendershot and colleagues, found a small effect on substance use (r = .14) and a large effect on psychosocial functioning (r = .48), with the best results for alcohol and polysubstance use and when combined with medication [Hendershot-etal-2011]. RP effects often emerge later in follow-up [Larimer-etal-1999]. In aftercare, 8 weekly group sessions of RP or mindfulness-based relapse prevention both cut relapse versus usual care at 6 months; RP delayed first drug use, and the mindfulness version had fewer use days at 12 months [Bowen-etal-2014]. The United States Veterans Affairs and Defense guideline found insufficient evidence to recommend mindfulness-based therapies, and states that a return to use means treatment needs adjusting, not that it failed [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
