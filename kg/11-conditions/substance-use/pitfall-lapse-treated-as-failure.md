**Bottom line:** A lapse is a single episode of use; relapse is a return to the old pattern. Whether one becomes the other depends a great deal on how the client explains the lapse: blaming a stable personal flaw leads to guilt and giving up, while seeing it as a coping failure in one situation leads to learning [Larimer-etal-1999]. Therapists can feed the first explanation without meaning to. Treat each lapse as data and plan for it before it happens.

## What it looks like

- The client says "I've blown it" or "I'm back to day one", then uses more over the following days.
- The client stops attending after a slip, or reports "all fine" while family describe drinking.
- The therapist sounds disappointed, lectures, or spends the session on how serious the slip was.
- The program discharges the client for using, or makes abstinence a condition of continuing.
- There is no written lapse plan, so the client has nothing to do in the first hours.

## Why it happens

- All-or-nothing goals make any use feel like total failure. In relapse research, people with a stronger commitment to absolute abstinence and stronger disease-model beliefs were more likely to move from lapse to full relapse [Larimer-etal-1999].
- Shame drives concealment, and concealment removes the chance to learn.
- Therapists may read a lapse as proof that therapy failed, or worry that continuing implies they condone use.
- Self-efficacy falls after a lapse; in smoking research it predicted relapse more strongly when measured after the quit attempt [Hendershot-etal-2011].

## The fix

- If the client reports a lapse → thank them for telling you, then do a step-by-step analysis of the episode. Because lapses are learning opportunities about triggers and coping [Larimer-etal-1999].
- If the client says "I have no willpower" → restructure the attribution toward a specific, changeable cause. Because attributing a lapse to stable, global, internal factors predicts abandoning the goal [Larimer-etal-1999].
- If the client is working toward abstinence or cutting down → write a lapse plan with them before any lapse: stop, leave the situation safely, contact the therapist or a support person, and review what triggered it. Because Marlatt's model treats this as an emergency kit [Larimer-etal-1999].
- If your service considers discharge after use → argue to adjust the plan instead. Because the United States Veterans Affairs and Defense guideline states that return to use means treatment should be adjusted, not that it failed [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021], and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises not refusing treatment to people who do not accept abstinence [NICE-CG115].
- If the client starts hiding use → name it without blame and make honesty easy, for example by asking about use routinely at every check-in.

Example phrasings:
- "You used on Saturday, and you came in and told me. That honesty is exactly what we need to learn from it."
- "Let's look at Saturday like detectives. Where did it start, and where could it have gone differently?"
- Avoid: "I'm disappointed; we were doing so well." It invites the guilt that turns a slip into a relapse.
