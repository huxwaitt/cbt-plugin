**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for substance use teaches a functional analysis of use and a set of coping skills, usually in about 12 weekly one-hour sessions [NICE-CG115; Project-MATCH-1997]. It beats minimal or no treatment, but not other specific therapies such as motivational enhancement or contingency management, so combine rather than compete [Magill-etal-2019]. Guidelines differ by substance: CBT is an option for alcohol, cannabis and cocaine, while contingency management leads for stimulants [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021; NICE-CG51].

## When to use

- Harmful drinking or mild alcohol dependence: the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) offers CBT, behavioral or social network therapies [NICE-CG115].
- Moderate to severe dependence: after withdrawal is managed, alongside medication (see `substance-use.addiction-medications`).
- Cannabis use disorder: CBT, motivational enhancement, or both combined; avoid relying on a single brief session of 60 minutes or less [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
- Cocaine use disorder: CBT, recovery-focused therapy, or contingency management with another therapy [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].
- Methamphetamine use disorder: contingency management plus another behavioral therapy, such as CBT, as initial treatment (weak recommendation) [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021].

## How it works

CBT treats use as a learned habit with a function. The client maps triggers, thoughts, cravings and consequences, then practices new responses at each link: avoiding or changing triggers, riding out craving, refusing offers, solving the problems that drive use, and changing permission-giving thoughts [Carroll-etal-2008]. Effects are durable and sometimes grow after treatment ends, a "sleeper effect" [Carroll-Kiluk-2017].

## Steps

Each session: check use and urges since last time, review homework, teach one skill (rationale, modeling, role-play), check understanding, set homework [Carroll-etal-2008].

| Session | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | Feedback on assessment, motivational interview, goal, safety plan for withdrawal and overdose |
| 2 | Functional analysis of recent episodes; daily monitoring of use and urges |
| 3 | Coping with craving: delay, distract, urge surfing, talk it through |
| 4 | Motivation and stimulus control: decision matrix, removing supplies and cues |
| 5 | Refusal skills with role-play |
| 6 | Seemingly irrelevant decisions that lead toward use |
| 7 | Identifying and changing thoughts about using |
| 8 | Problem solving |
| 9 | Mood, sleep and emotion management |
| 10 | Rewarding drug-free activities; social support and mutual help |
| 11 | Lapse plan: what to do in the first hours after using |
| 12 | Relapse prevention plan, review, booster plan |

The core modules follow the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) CBT manual as used in its computer version, CBT4CBT: functional analysis, craving, refusal, problem solving, thoughts, and decisions [Carroll-etal-2008]. The session order is a practical sequence, not a tested one.

## Guidance

- If the client used since the last session → start with a functional analysis of that episode. Because each lapse shows a trigger and a missing skill [Carroll-etal-2008].
- If the client arrives intoxicated → check safety, do not do skills work, and reschedule. Because new learning will not hold (expert consensus).
- If homework is skipped → shrink it and practice it in session. Because homework completion tracks outcome in CBT for addiction [Carroll-Kiluk-2017].
- If the client uses stimulants → add or refer for contingency management. Because guidelines back it for stimulants [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021; NICE-CG51], and its effect was largest for cocaine and opiates (d = 0.66 and 0.65) [Prendergast-etal-2006].
- If measures show no improvement or decline → review the plan rather than repeat it. Because NICE asks for routine outcome monitoring to guide continuation [NICE-CG115].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's slow down Friday night. What was the first moment you noticed wanting a drink?"
- "If a friend offers on Saturday, what exactly will you say? Let's practice it twice."
- Avoid: "You need more willpower." Change is a skill, not a test of character.

## Common problems

- **Teaching skills without rehearsal.** Role-play until the client can do it fluently.
- **Ignoring the function of use.** If drinking manages panic or insomnia, plan a replacement.
- **Ending at 12 sessions with no aftercare.** Book boosters and link to mutual help.

## Evidence

**Effect sizes (randomized trials, meta-analyses).**
- 53 trials: small overall effect (g = 0.15), larger for cannabis (g = 0.51) and against no treatment (g = 0.80) [Magill-Ray-2009].
- 30 trials: moderate effect against minimal treatment (g = 0.58 for frequency at 1 to 6 months); small against usual care (g = 0.18) that faded after 8 months; no difference from other specific therapies [Magill-etal-2019].
- CBT plus medication beat usual care plus medication (g = 0.18 to 0.28) [Ray-etal-2020].
- In Project MATCH, 12 weeks of CBT, motivational enhancement or twelve-step facilitation gave similar drinking outcomes [Project-MATCH-1997].
- Cannabis: in 23 trials, more than 4 sessions over more than a month did better than briefer help, and CBT, motivational enhancement and especially their combination had the most consistent support [Gates-etal-2016].

**Guideline status.** NICE (United Kingdom) recommends CBT for harmful drinking and mild dependence, usually one 60-minute session a week for 12 weeks [NICE-CG115]. NICE drug guidance recommends against routinely offering drug-focused CBT for cannabis or stimulant use or during opioid maintenance, and favors contingency management, using CBT for comorbid depression and anxiety [NICE-CG51]. In the United States, the Veterans Affairs and Defense guideline gives a weak recommendation for CBT in alcohol and cannabis use disorders and a strong one in cocaine use disorder [VA-DoD-SUD-CPG-2021]. The American Psychiatric Association's current guideline covers alcohol medication only; its general substance use disorder guideline is legacy [APA-Psychiatry-Guidelines-Site].
