**Bottom line:** Three families of models explain why worry persists. The intolerance of uncertainty (IU) model says worry copes with not knowing; the metacognitive model says beliefs about worry keep it going; avoidance models say worry avoids emotion, either feared images (Borkovec) or a sudden drop into distress (contrast avoidance). All share avoidance of internal experience [Behar-etal-2009]. Head-to-head trials favor metacognitive therapy (MCT) so far, but most are small or developer-led.

## Components

| Model | Core claim | Key maintaining factors | Main evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intolerance of uncertainty [Dugas-etal-1998] | People with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) find uncertainty unacceptable, and worry is an attempt to reduce it | IU, positive beliefs about worry, negative problem orientation, cognitive avoidance | IU best separated GAD from controls; the model classified 82% of participants correctly [Dugas-etal-1998] |
| Metacognitive [Wells-2009] | Worry persists because of beliefs about worry, not its content | Positive beliefs drive Type 1 worry; beliefs about uncontrollability and danger drive Type 2 worry (worry about worry) [AfWinklerfeltHammarberg-etal-2023] | Trials of therapy built on it (see Treatment targets) |
| Cognitive avoidance (Borkovec) | Worry is mainly verbal thought that dampens feared imagery and arousal | Negative reinforcement; emotional processing of fear is blocked | Worry was mostly thought-like [Borkovec-Inz-1990]; in speech-anxious volunteers, worry before a phobic image reduced the heart rate response [Borkovec-Hu-1990] |
| Contrast avoidance [Newman-Llera-2011] | Worry keeps the person in steady negative emotion so a bad event will not bring a sharp drop | Sensitivity to sudden negative emotional shifts | Review of experimental studies [Newman-Llera-2011] |

Other models: emotion dysregulation and acceptance-based models treat worry as a way to avoid emotion and internal experience [Behar-etal-2009]. Hirsch and Mathews add attention and interpretation biases with weak attentional control, which let threat thoughts intrude [Hirsch-Mathews-2012].

## How it keeps the problem going

- **IU:** each uncertain situation prompts worry and certainty-seeking; brief relief stops the person learning that uncertainty is bearable.
- **Metacognitive:** positive beliefs start worry; failed attempts to control it confirm uncontrollability; Type 2 worry adds anxiety.
- **Cognitive avoidance:** worry lowers immediate arousal, which reinforces it, and blocks the full emotional processing that would let fear fade [Borkovec-Hu-1990].
- **Contrast avoidance:** worry creates and keeps negative emotion, so the person avoids a sudden negative shift; when nothing bad happens, worry is reinforced [Newman-Llera-2011].

**Critiques.** Hirsch and Mathews argue there is little or no evidence that verbal worry reduces negative emotional response, which weakens the original avoidance account. They also argue IU might follow from biased threat processing rather than cause it, and that some beliefs about worry may be explanations made after the fact [Hirsch-Mathews-2012]. Each model has supporters and critics; none is settled.

## Drawing it with the client

Use questions that discriminate between the models in one assessment session (expert consensus, not validated items):
- "If you knew for certain how it would turn out, even badly, would the worry ease?" A clear yes points to IU.
- "What would happen if you worried and couldn't stop?" Fear of losing control or harm points to the metacognitive model.
- "What do you see in your mind when you worry, and what happens if you let the picture run?" Avoided images point to cognitive avoidance.
- "Do you ever worry on purpose so you won't be caught off guard?" A yes points to contrast avoidance.

Then choose one model to draw (see `gad.cbt-model`). If the client's answers fit none, use a generic five-part formulation (see `03-formulation.five-part-model`) and track worry as a process.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Model | Treatment and evidence |
|---|---|
| IU | IU-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): uncertainty experiments, problem solving, imaginal exposure. Remission matched applied relaxation directly, and only CBT kept improving over follow-up [Dugas-etal-2010] |
| Metacognitive | MCT: postponement, detached mindfulness, challenging uncontrollability, danger and positive beliefs. Recovery 65% against 38% for Borkovec's CBT, with both protocol authors on the trial [Nordahl-etal-2018]; better than IU therapy on most measures (91% against 80% no longer meeting criteria) [VanDerHeiden-etal-2012] |
| Cognitive avoidance | Applied relaxation, self-control desensitization, worry exposure. Worry exposure and applied relaxation both produced large gains (high end-state functioning 48% and 56%) [Hoyer-etal-2009] |
| Contrast and experiential avoidance | Emotional exposure and acceptance-based behavior therapy (ABBT). ABBT and applied relaxation did equally well [HayesSkelton-etal-2013b]; 78% no longer met criteria after ABBT in an early trial [Roemer-etal-2008] |

**Reading the head-to-head evidence.** MCT has the best results in direct comparisons, including a pilot where it beat IU therapy on worry [AfWinklerfeltHammarberg-etal-2023]. Treat this as emerging evidence: samples are small, and the model's developers are authors on several trials. Model-specific treatments mostly beat waitlist by similar margins, so fit to the client, therapist training and the manual followed matter as much as the model chosen (expert consensus).
