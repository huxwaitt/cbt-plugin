**Bottom line:** Two cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) models lead the field for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). The intolerance of uncertainty (IU) model says worry is a way of coping with not knowing, fed by beliefs that worry helps, a threat-focused view of problems, and avoidance of feared images [Dugas-etal-1998]. The metacognitive model says worry persists because of positive beliefs about worrying and negative beliefs that worry is uncontrollable and dangerous [Wells-2009]. Draw either from one recent worry episode and let the model pick the targets.

## Components

**Intolerance of uncertainty model** [Dugas-etal-1998; Dugas-Robichaud-2007]
- **Trigger:** a situation or thought that opens a "what if" question.
- **Intolerance of uncertainty:** a stable tendency to find uncertainty unacceptable, unfair or threatening. It was the key factor separating people with GAD from controls, and the model correctly classified 82% of participants [Dugas-etal-1998].
- **Positive beliefs about worry:** worry helps me solve problems, prepares me, shows I care, or prevents bad outcomes.
- **Negative problem orientation:** problems are seen as threats, with low confidence in solving them.
- **Cognitive avoidance:** suppression, distraction and staying in verbal "what if" thinking instead of facing feared images.
- **Two kinds of worry:** about current, solvable problems and about hypothetical situations [Dugas-etal-2010].

**Metacognitive model** [Wells-2009; AfWinklerfeltHammarberg-etal-2023]
- **Type 1 worry:** worry about external events or physical symptoms, driven by positive metacognitive beliefs ("Worrying helps me cope").
- **Type 2 worry:** worry about worry ("I'll lose control", "Worry will damage my health"), driven by negative beliefs about uncontrollability and danger.
- **Unhelpful control strategies:** reassurance-seeking, avoidance, trying to suppress thoughts, distraction, and over-analysis.
- **Emotion:** anxiety, which Type 2 worry amplifies.

## How it keeps the problem going

1. **Worry gets the credit.** Most feared outcomes do not happen, and the client credits the worrying. Positive beliefs strengthen.
2. **Certainty-seeking.** Reassurance, checking, over-planning, list-making and avoiding decisions reduce uncertainty for a moment, so the client never learns that uncertainty is bearable.
3. **Failed control.** Trying to stop worrying, then failing, seems to prove that worry is uncontrollable. Type 2 worry and anxiety rise [Wells-2009].
4. **Verbal worry blocks emotional processing.** Worry is mainly thought rather than imagery [Borkovec-Inz-1990]. In speech-anxious volunteers, worrying before a phobic image reduced the heart rate response to it, which suggests worry may block the processing of fear [Borkovec-Hu-1990].
5. **Arousal and fatigue.** Tension, poor sleep and poor concentration make the client feel less able to cope and lower the threshold for the next "what if".

Behar and colleagues note that the leading models share one theme: avoiding internal experiences such as thoughts, emotions and images [Behar-etal-2009].

## Drawing it with the client

1. Pick one recent, specific worry episode. Write the trigger at the top.
2. Elicit the chain: "What was the first 'what if'? And then what?" Write two or three links.
3. Ask about uncertainty: "If you didn't know for sure how it would turn out, how would that feel?"
4. Ask about beliefs: "What are the advantages of worrying?" and "Is there any downside? What would happen if you worried and couldn't stop?"
5. Ask about actions: "What did you do to feel more certain or to stop the worry?" Then ask, "What did that do to the worry later?"
6. Draw the feedback arrows from actions and beliefs back to the worry. Summarize in the client's words.
7. Check fit: "Does this match how it usually goes for you?" Revise rather than defend.
8. Use a small test to socialize: postpone worry for a set time, or try to suppress a thought for two minutes, and review what happened.

Example phrasings:
- "It sounds as if not knowing feels almost worse than the bad outcome itself."
- "Part of you sees worry as a helper, and part of you is scared of it. Let's look at both."
- Avoid: "Your worries are irrational." The content is often realistic; the process is the problem.

Record the drawn model, belief ratings (0 to 100) for "Worry is uncontrollable" and "Worry helps me", the main safety behaviors, and the client's own summary sentence.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Maintaining factor | Target and method |
|---|---|
| Intolerance of uncertainty | Recognize it, then run behavioral experiments that build tolerance of uncertainty [Dugas-etal-2010] |
| Positive beliefs about worry | Re-evaluate the usefulness of worry [Dugas-etal-2010; Wells-2009] |
| Worry about current problems | Problem-solving training [Dugas-etal-2010] |
| Worry about hypothetical situations, cognitive avoidance | Imaginal or written exposure to the feared scenario [Dugas-etal-2010] |
| Uncontrollability and danger beliefs | Worry postponement experiments, detached mindfulness, verbal challenge [Nordahl-etal-2018; AfWinklerfeltHammarberg-etal-2023] |
| Reassurance-seeking, checking, avoidance | Drop them gradually as experiments (see `15-transdiagnostic-processes.safety-behaviors`) |

Choose one model per client and keep to it. As expert consensus (no trial has tested matching), pick the metacognitive model when "worry about worry" dominates, and the IU model when the client cannot tolerate not knowing and seeks certainty. Mixing both without a plan usually means neither is tested well (see `gad.pitfall-eclectic-anxiety-management`).
