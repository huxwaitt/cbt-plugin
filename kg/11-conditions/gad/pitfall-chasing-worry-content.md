**Bottom line:** In generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), the topic of worry changes from week to week, while the process stays the same. If therapy keeps testing or answering each new worry, it works like reassurance and leaves the drivers untouched: intolerance of uncertainty (IU) and beliefs about worry [Dugas-etal-1998; Wells-2009]. Work on the process instead.

## What it looks like

- The agenda is "this week's worry": the job, then a child's health, then money, then the car.
- Thought records end with "But what if I'm wrong?" and a new chain begins.
- The therapist offers probabilities, facts or reassurance, and the client asks for more.
- The client says "That helped" each session, yet the Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scores stay flat.
- Homework is about solving the latest worry rather than practicing a new response to worry.

## Why it happens

- GAD worry content is often realistic, so it feels right to weigh the evidence.
- In the IU model, seeking certainty is one of the behaviors that keeps worry going; answering a worry delivers a little certainty and the IU is never tested [Dugas-etal-1998].
- In the metacognitive model, analyzing worries is more worry. It also leaves positive beliefs ("Worrying helps me prepare") and beliefs about uncontrollability unchallenged [Wells-2009].
- Therapists are trained to work with thought content, and answering a distressed client feels kind.
- Without a shared model, the session follows whatever is most distressing that day.

## The fix

- If a new worry topic arrives → place it in the formulation first: "Is this the same pattern we mapped, with a new topic?" Because the targets are the process, not the topic [Dugas-etal-2010].
- If the worry is about a current, solvable problem → use one round of structured problem solving, then stop. Because the IU protocol sends current problems to problem-solving training [Dugas-etal-2010].
- If the worry is about a hypothetical situation → do not dispute it; use exposure to the feared scenario or an uncertainty experiment. Because the IU protocol handles hypothetical worries with imaginal exposure [Dugas-etal-2010; AfWinklerfeltHammarberg-etal-2023].
- If the client asks for reassurance → reflect the request and name the pattern. Because certainty-seeking keeps IU untested [Dugas-etal-1998].
- If you are using metacognitive therapy (MCT) → postpone the worry and work on the belief "I can't control it" rather than the content. Because MCT teaches clients to postpone further conceptual processing and leave thoughts alone [Nordahl-etal-2018].
- If sessions have drifted → put the formulation back on the table at the start of each session and choose the module task before discussing the week.

Example phrasings:
- "Last week it was money, this week it's your daughter. What's the same about how the worry works?"
- "We could spend the hour on this one, and next week there'd be another. Want to try something different?"
- Avoid: "Let's look at the evidence that your daughter is safe." This is reassurance in a new form.

## Sources note

The fix applies the IU protocol's split between current and hypothetical worries [Dugas-etal-2010] and the metacognitive focus on beliefs about worry rather than content [Wells-2009; Nordahl-etal-2018]. No trial has tested content-focused sessions against process-focused sessions directly; the warning is expert consensus built on these models.
