**Bottom line:** Keep the core of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and change delivery, order and pace. Treat the more severe of GAD and comorbid depression first, and treat harmful or dependent substance use before GAD [Kendall-etal-2011]. Older adults and young people benefit from adapted CBT. Step up to medication, combined treatment or specialist care when a full course has not worked or risk is high [Kendall-etal-2011].

## The principle

Adapt how CBT is delivered, not what keeps worry going. The model's targets (intolerance of uncertainty, beliefs about worry, avoidance) apply across ages and cultures, while pacing, materials, family involvement and treatment order change. Comorbidity matters most for prognosis: substance use disorder (risk ratio 0.20) and major depression (0.57) lowered the chance of recovery from GAD over 12 years [Bruce-etal-2005].

## Guidance

**Comorbidity and severity**
- If depression is comorbid and more severe → treat depression first (see `depression.treatment`), then reassess GAD. Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises treating the primary, more severe disorder first [Kendall-etal-2011].
- If alcohol or drug use is harmful or dependent → treat it first (see `substance-use.treatment`); non-harmful use is no reason to delay GAD treatment. Because NICE advises this order [NICE-CG113] and substance use slows recovery [Bruce-etal-2005].
- If insomnia persists after worry improves → add targeted sleep work (see `insomnia.treatment`). Because poor sleep is a core GAD symptom and keeps fatigue going.
- If interpersonal problems remain after CBT → address them directly. Because residual interpersonal problems predicted poorer improvement [Borkovec-etal-2002].
- If anxiety is severe with marked impairment and there is risk of self-harm or suicide, significant comorbidity, self-neglect, or no response to step 3 → refer for specialist (step 4) care; for imminent risk follow `02-risk.crisis-response`. Because NICE sets these step 4 criteria [NICE-CG113].

**Age**
- If the client is an older adult → use a structured, primary care friendly format (up to 10 weekly sessions) with relaxation, problem solving and sleep modules, and offer telephone boosters. Because this format beat enhanced usual care on worry, though not on a GAD severity scale [Stanley-etal-2009].
- If memory or hearing is limited → give written summaries, slower pacing and repetition (expert consensus). Because effects of CBT in older adults were smaller in trials with higher attrition [Hall-etal-2016a].
- If the client is a child or adolescent → use developmentally adapted CBT with parent involvement and consider a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) by preference and severity, through a prescriber. Because CBT and SSRIs have the strongest support in pediatric anxiety [Walter-etal-2020; James-etal-2020].

**Culture and context**
- If worry is expressed through dizziness, stomach upset or palpitations → work with somatic language and link it to worry. Because somatic presentations are common across cultures, and criteria that put psychological worry first can miss them [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2010].
- If worry content draws on spiritual or family obligations, such as the welfare of deceased relatives → explore its meaning before testing it. Because worry content varies by culture [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2010].
- If worry reflects real threats, such as debt, unsafe housing or immigration status → use problem solving and practical help first. Because current problems call for solutions, not exposure.

**Medication and delivery**
- If the client prefers medication or did not respond to CBT → refer for an SSRI; NICE suggests sertraline first, then another SSRI or a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI), and pregabalin if these are not tolerated [Kendall-etal-2011]. Because these have trial support [Slee-etal-2019].
- If benzodiazepines are requested → refer the question to the prescriber and explain that NICE advises against them except short-term in a crisis, and against antipsychotics for GAD in primary care [NICE-CG113]. Because benzodiazepines were effective but poorly tolerated [Slee-etal-2019].
- If a client under 30 starts an SSRI or SNRI → ask about suicidal thoughts at each session and share concerns with the prescriber. Because NICE warns of a small rise in suicidal thinking and self-harm in this group and advises weekly monitoring in the first month [NICE-CG113].
- If medication helped partly → consider adding CBT. Because NICE recommends this for partial drug response [Kendall-etal-2011].
- If the client cannot attend weekly → offer guided internet-delivered CBT. Because it has large effects on anxiety and worry [Eilert-etal-2021].

## Example phrasings

- To an older adult: "We'll go at your pace, and I'll write down the key points each time."
- On combined care: "Medication and CBT can work together. I'll stay in touch with your doctor about what helps."
- On culture: "In your family, what does it mean when someone worries a lot?"
- Avoid: "Your worries about money are just catastrophizing." Real problems need solving.

## Evidence

- Older adults: 14 randomized trials (985 participants) found CBT better than no treatment [Hall-etal-2016a]; both drug and psychological treatments helped in 27 trials [Goncalves-Byrne-2012]. In primary care, response was 40.0% with CBT against 21.9% with enhanced usual care [Stanley-etal-2009].
- Young people: across 87 studies of anxiety disorders, remission was 49.4% with CBT against 17.8% for waitlist [James-etal-2020]. GAD-specific youth trials are few (6 studies, 217 participants) [Shipp-etal-2026].
- Medication: against control, psychotherapy had g = 0.76 and medication g = 0.38, but drug trials mostly used placebo, so the authors warn against direct comparison [Carl-etal-2020]. NICE advises reviewing drugs every 2 to 4 weeks for the first 3 months and continuing at least a year if effective [Kendall-etal-2011].
- Most adaptations above are expert consensus applied to trial evidence. Few trials test them directly.
