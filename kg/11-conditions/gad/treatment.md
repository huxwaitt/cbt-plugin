**Bottom line:** Offer individual, manual-based cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or applied relaxation, usually 12 to 15 weekly one-hour sessions, when generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) has not improved with low-intensity help or impairment is marked [NICE-CG113; Kendall-etal-2011]. Follow one model: intolerance of uncertainty (IU) CBT or metacognitive therapy (MCT). Psychotherapy has a large effect against mostly waitlist controls (Hedges' g = 0.84), but gains shrink over the long term [Cuijpers-etal-2014b; VanDis-etal-2020].

## When to use

- Step 3 of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) model: marked functional impairment, or no improvement after education, monitoring and low-intensity self-help or groups [Kendall-etal-2011].
- Offer CBT, applied relaxation or medication by preference, since neither psychological nor drug treatment has been shown better [Kendall-etal-2011].

## How it works

The protocol targets what keeps worry going (see `gad.cbt-model`). IU CBT builds tolerance of uncertainty, re-evaluates beliefs that worry helps, solves real problems and exposes the client to feared hypothetical scenarios [Dugas-etal-2010]. MCT changes beliefs that worry is uncontrollable, dangerous or useful, so the client can leave worry alone [Wells-2009]. Applied relaxation trains a fast relaxation response applied at the first cues of anxiety [HayesSkelton-etal-2013a].

## Steps

**IU CBT, 12 weekly one-hour sessions** [Dugas-etal-2010; Dugas-Robichaud-2007]:

| Session | Module | Core work |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Psychoeducation and worry awareness | Share the model; daily worry log; sort worries into current problems and hypothetical situations |
| 2 to 4 | Uncertainty recognition and behavioral exposure | Spot IU in daily life (reassurance, checking, over-planning, procrastination); run experiments that invite small uncertainty |
| 5 | Re-evaluating the usefulness of worry | List and test positive beliefs about worry |
| 6 to 8 | Problem-solving training | Problem orientation, definition, alternatives, decision, action and review for current problems |
| 9 to 11 | Imaginal exposure | Build a worst-fear scenario with the downward arrow; listen to a looped recording of it 20 to 60 minutes daily until anxiety fades |
| 12 | Relapse prevention | Review gains, warning signs, a plan for setbacks |

**MCT, 8 to 12 sessions** [Wells-etal-2010; AfWinklerfeltHammarberg-etal-2023]: case formulation and socialization; challenge uncontrollability beliefs with worry postponement and detached mindfulness; stop reassurance, avoidance and thought control; challenge danger beliefs; challenge positive beliefs; relapse prevention.

**Applied relaxation, about 12 sessions** [HayesSkelton-etal-2013a; Dugas-etal-2010]: early-cue monitoring; tension-release relaxation from 16 to 4 muscle groups; release-only; cue-controlled; differential; rapid relaxation in 20 to 30 seconds; application in real situations.

Each session: Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) and Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ), homework review, one module task, new homework, summary.

## Guidance

- If the client asks you to settle a worry ("Will I lose my job?") → sort it as a current problem to solve or a hypothetical one to expose to; do not answer it. Because answering content feeds the next worry (see `gad.pitfall-chasing-worry-content`).
- If the client says worry keeps them safe → treat it as a positive belief and test it, for example with a week of postponed worry. Because positive beliefs maintain worry [Wells-2009; Dugas-etal-2010].
- If postponement fails → shrink the delay and log the successes. Because any control achieved is evidence against uncontrollability [Wells-2009].
- If an uncertainty experiment is too frightening → choose a smaller step, such as ordering an untried dish. Because the protocol builds tolerance in graded steps [Dugas-etal-2010].
- If relaxation is used to escape anxiety → frame it as moving toward relaxation while staying in the situation. Because applied relaxation includes approaching avoided situations [HayesSkelton-etal-2013a].
- If GAD-7 and PSWQ have not improved by about session 6 (expert consensus) → review the formulation and adherence; after a full course without response, discuss medication. Because NICE advises offering drug treatment after a full course of high-intensity therapy without response [Kendall-etal-2011].

Example phrasings:
- "This worry is about something that hasn't happened. Rather than solve it, let's practice living with not knowing."
- "Let's test it: if worry really protects you, what should happen this week if you postpone it?"
- Avoid: "Don't worry, that's very unlikely." Reassurance gives brief relief and keeps IU untested.

## Common problems

- Imaginal exposure turns into more worrying: keep the script specific, vivid and first-person, and stay with the image without analyzing it.
- Postponement used as suppression: set worry aside, do not push it away.
- Homework skipped because worry "makes sense": revisit the pros and cons of worrying.
- Relaxation practiced only when calm: practice it at daily triggers.

## Evidence

**Guideline status.** NICE (UK) recommends manual-based CBT or applied relaxation at step 3, delivered by trained practitioners. The guideline (last updated 2020) is current; an update is planned but not scheduled [NICE-CG113]. The American Psychiatric Association lists no current GAD guideline [APA-Psychiatry-Guidelines-Site]. The American Psychological Association also has no GAD guideline (see `04-treatment-planning.guideline-map`).

**Effect sizes (supported by randomized trials).**
- CBT-based therapies beat usual care or waitlist on clinical response (risk ratio 0.63) in a Cochrane review [Hunot-etal-2007].
- 41 studies: g = 0.84 against control [Cuijpers-etal-2014b]. 79 trials: psychotherapy g = 0.76, medication g = 0.38; drug trials mostly used placebo, so do not compare directly [Carl-etal-2020].
- Cognitive therapy for worry: d = 1.81 against no therapy and 0.63 against other treatments; 57% recovered at 12 months [Hanrahan-etal-2013].
- IU CBT against applied relaxation: remission 70% and 55% at post-treatment, not significantly different; only CBT kept improving over 24 months [Dugas-etal-2010].
- MCT recovery was 65% against 38% for Borkovec's CBT [Nordahl-etal-2018], and 91% no longer met criteria against 80% after IU therapy [VanDerHeiden-etal-2012]. In a 20-patient pilot, MCT beat applied relaxation [Wells-etal-2010]. Developers led several of these trials.
- Beyond 12 months, CBT's advantage for GAD is small (g = 0.22) [VanDis-etal-2020].
- Internet-delivered treatment: g = 0.79 for anxiety and 0.75 for worry [Eilert-etal-2021].
