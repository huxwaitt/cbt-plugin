**Bottom line:** Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) responds to coherent, manual-based treatment, not a menu of coping tips. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks for cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or applied relaxation based on trial manuals and delivered by trained practitioners [Kendall-etal-2011]. Choose one model, follow its phases, measure worry every session, and change course when scores stall.

## What it looks like

- Each session teaches a new technique: breathing, a thought record, sleep hygiene, a mindfulness exercise.
- Relaxation is taught as a calming exercise for the evening, never applied to early anxiety cues.
- No one can name which model the therapy follows or which module comes next.
- Session 8 arrives with Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scores near baseline.
- The plan is "more of the same" or an open-ended number of sessions.

## Why it happens

- GAD looks diffuse, so a broad toolkit seems to fit.
- Therapists trained in general anxiety management may not know the GAD protocols.
- Without a formulation, the session follows the week's crisis.
- Relaxation is easy to teach and pleasant, so it can replace the harder work of exposure and uncertainty experiments.
- Scores are not reviewed, so drift goes unnoticed.

## The fix

- If no model guides the plan → choose the intolerance of uncertainty (IU) or metacognitive model, share it, and map the remaining sessions to its modules. Because NICE expects treatment based on the manuals used in trials [NICE-CG113; Kendall-etal-2011].
- If relaxation is used → teach the full applied relaxation sequence through to rapid relaxation applied at early cues. Because applied relaxation is a structured protocol, not a single exercise [HayesSkelton-etal-2013a].
- If the protocol is IU CBT → keep its order: worry awareness, uncertainty experiments, beliefs about worry, problem solving, imaginal exposure, relapse prevention. Because this sequence was tested in 12 weekly sessions [Dugas-etal-2010].
- If you have not measured worry → give the PSWQ and GAD-7 each session and look for a 7-point PSWQ or 4-point GAD-7 reliable change. Because these are the change criteria used in trials and services [AfWinklerfeltHammarberg-etal-2023; Gyani-etal-2013].
- If scores are flat by mid-treatment → review the formulation, adherence and differential, then discuss stepping up. Because services that followed the NICE-based model and stepped up more had higher recovery [Gyani-etal-2013].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's step back and look at what's keeping your worry going, so we use the rest of our sessions on that."
- "Your worry scores haven't moved much. That tells us to change something, not to try harder at the same thing."
- Avoid: "Let's try another technique this week and see if it helps."

## Sources note

In England's services, recovery was lower when a treatment not recommended by NICE was given, and higher where clients had more sessions on average and services stepped up more often [Gyani-etal-2013]. Cognitive therapy that targets worry has large effects [Hanrahan-etal-2013]. No trial has compared an eclectic toolkit with a GAD protocol directly; the warning applies these findings and is expert consensus.
