**Bottom line:** Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) is excessive, hard-to-control worry about several everyday matters, with tension and arousal, lasting at least 6 months under the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) [APA-DSM5TR-2022]. It usually starts in adulthood, waxes and wanes for years, and rarely comes alone: about 8 in 10 people with lifetime GAD have another disorder [Ruscio-etal-2017]. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is guideline-recommended, first as low-intensity self-help, then as individual therapy [NICE-CG113].

## Diagnostic essentials

DSM-5-TR requirements, paraphrased [APA-DSM5TR-2022; Scheeringa-Burns-2018]:

- **Core feature:** excessive anxiety and worry (apprehensive expectation) about a number of events or activities, such as work, health, money or family, present more days than not for at least 6 months.
- **Control:** the person finds the worry difficult to control.
- **Associated symptoms:** at least 3 of 6 in adults, only 1 in children. The six are restlessness or feeling keyed up, tiring easily, poor concentration or mind going blank, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbance.
- **Impact:** clinically significant distress or impairment.
- **Exclusions:** not caused by a substance (drug of abuse, medication) or a medical condition such as hyperthyroidism, and not better explained by another disorder, for example worry about panic attacks, negative evaluation, contamination, separation, weight, illness or trauma reminders.
- **Specifiers:** none. The International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) code is F41.1 [APA-DSM5TR-2022].

**International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11), code 6B00.** Key differences:
- Duration is "at least several months" rather than a fixed 6 months [Shevlin-etal-2023; Domschke-Zwanzger-2025].
- The core feature can be general apprehension ("free-floating anxiety") or excessive worry about several everyday domains [Shevlin-etal-2023].
- There is no minimum symptom count. Additional features include muscle tension or restlessness, autonomic overactivity, nervousness, poor concentration, irritability and sleep disturbance [Shevlin-etal-2023; Domschke-Zwanzger-2025].
- The tenth revision's (ICD-10) rule that excluded GAD when panic, phobic, obsessive-compulsive or hypochondriacal criteria were met was dropped [Domschke-Zwanzger-2025].

## Course and prevalence

- **United States:** adult past-year prevalence 2.7% (women 3.4%, men 1.9%) and lifetime 5.7% (National Comorbidity Survey Replication, DSM-IV criteria). Adolescent lifetime prevalence is 2.2% [NIMH-GAD-Statistics].
- **Worldwide (26 countries, DSM-5 criteria):** lifetime 3.7%, 12-month 1.8%. Lifetime prevalence is 5.0% in high-income and 1.6% in low-income countries [Ruscio-etal-2017].
- **Onset:** median age 39 (interquartile range 25 to 53); only 5% of cases begin by age 13 [Ruscio-etal-2017]. Clients often say they have "always been a worrier"; ask when it became a problem.
- **Primary care:** 7.6% of patients in 15 US clinics had GAD [Kroenke-etal-2007].
- **Course:** chronic and fluctuating. Nearly half (48.8%) of people with lifetime GAD had it in the past year [Ruscio-etal-2017]. In a 12-year clinical cohort, the probability of recovery was 0.58, and 0.45 of those who recovered had a recurrence [Bruce-etal-2005].
- **Burden:** among people with GAD in the past year, 50.6% had severe role impairment and 49.2% received any treatment [Ruscio-etal-2017].

## Differential and comorbidity

Lifetime comorbidity is 81.9%: mood disorders 63.0% and other anxiety disorders 51.7% [Ruscio-etal-2017]. Comorbid substance use (risk ratio 0.20) and major depression (0.57) make recovery less likely [Bruce-etal-2005].

| Presentation | What points away from GAD [APA-DSM5TR-2022] |
|---|---|
| Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) | Intrusive, unwanted thoughts, urges or images, often with neutralizing or compulsions |
| Illness or health anxiety | Fear centers on having or getting a serious disease |
| Panic disorder | Fear centers on panic attacks and their consequences |
| Social anxiety disorder | Worry centers on negative evaluation |
| Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) | Anxiety is tied to trauma memories and reminders |
| Depression | Repetitive thinking is mainly about past failures and loss, with low mood |
| Substance or medical cause | Caffeine, stimulants, withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives, thyroid disease |
| Normal worry | Less pervasive, easier to control, less distressing, fewer physical symptoms |

## CBT approach in brief

- Formulate with an intolerance of uncertainty or metacognitive model (see `gad.cbt-model`).
- The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) uses stepped care: education and monitoring, then low-intensity CBT-based self-help or groups, then individual CBT or applied relaxation, usually 12 to 15 weekly one-hour sessions, or medication by preference [NICE-CG113; Kendall-etal-2011].
- In a meta-analysis of 41 studies, psychotherapy (mostly CBT) beat control conditions, mostly waiting lists, with a large effect (Hedges' g = 0.84) [Cuijpers-etal-2014b]. Details are in `gad.treatment`.

## Risk notes

Estimates of suicide risk differ. A meta-analysis of 42 observational studies, rated low to moderate quality, found anxiety disorders linked to suicidal ideation (odds ratio 2.89), attempts (2.47) and suicide deaths (3.34) [Kanwar-etal-2013]. A meta-analysis of longitudinal prediction found weaker links (ideation 1.49, attempts 1.64) and none for deaths (1.01) [Bentley-etal-2016]. Weigh comorbid depression and substance use heavily.

- If a client with GAD reports hopelessness, low mood or heavy alcohol or sedative use → ask directly about suicidal thoughts and follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`. Because anxiety disorders are linked to suicidal ideation and attempts [Kanwar-etal-2013].
- If risk is raised but not imminent → write a safety plan with the client, including restricting access to lethal means (see `02-risk.safety-planning`), and review risk each session. Because anxiety alone is a weak predictor, so risk rests on the whole picture [Bentley-etal-2016].
- If risk is imminent → do not leave the client alone, arrange same-day emergency assessment, and follow `02-risk.crisis-response` (US: 911, or call or text 988; UK: 999, NHS 111, Samaritans 116 123). Because acute risk needs same-day escalation, not routine CBT.
- If the client uses benzodiazepines or alcohol to manage worry → record amounts, liaise with the prescriber, and never advise stopping suddenly (see `12-medical-pharmacology.anxiolytics-and-hypnotics`). Because NICE advises against benzodiazepines for GAD except short-term in a crisis [NICE-CG113], and abrupt withdrawal from either can be medically dangerous.
