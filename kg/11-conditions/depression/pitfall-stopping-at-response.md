**Bottom line:** Feeling better is not the same as being well. Response (about a 50% drop in symptoms) can leave several symptoms in place, and residual symptoms predict earlier relapse [WHO-CDDR-2024; Breedvelt-etal-2021]. Aim for remission, such as a Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score of 4 or less held for at least a month [VA-DoD-MDD-2022], then end with a written relapse plan, spaced sessions and a route back [NICE-NG222].

## What it looks like

- The PHQ-9 falls from 20 to 9, the client says "I'm much better," and therapy ends the next week.
- Sleep is still poor, the client still avoids some tasks, and self-criticism is still there, but none of it is discussed.
- No written plan for warning signs; the last session is a friendly review of progress.
- Months later the client is back with a new episode and says, "I didn't notice it coming."

## Why it happens

- Service pressure and fixed session limits push discharge at the first good score.
- Both client and therapist feel relief and want to end on a high.
- "Response" is mistaken for recovery. The VA/DoD (United States Departments of Veterans Affairs and Defense) guideline defines response as a 50% PHQ improvement and remission as a score of 4 or less for at least a month [VA-DoD-MDD-2022].
- Relapse prevention is treated as optional homework instead of a treatment phase.

## The fix

- If the PHQ-9 has dropped but is still 5 or above → name the remaining symptoms and agree on whether to keep working toward remission. Because 5 is the developers' lower bound for mild depression [Kroenke-etal-2001] and residual symptoms predict relapse [Breedvelt-etal-2021].
- If the client has relapse risk factors (recurrent episodes, residual symptoms, rumination, ongoing stressors) → discuss continuing therapy, with at least 4 more sessions focused on staying well. Because the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends this for people at higher risk [NICE-NG222].
- If you are ending → write the plan together: what helped, warning signs, triggers in the next 12 months, and a step-by-step response. Because NICE lists these as core relapse prevention content [NICE-NG222].
- If the client has had three or more episodes → offer mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) or relapse-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). Because MBCT reduced relapse versus usual care (hazard ratio 0.69) [Kuyken-etal-2016b].
- If the service allows → taper session frequency and book a booster. Because continuation cognitive therapy reduced relapse by 21% versus assessment only [Vittengl-etal-2007].

Example phrasing: "You've made real progress. Before we stop, let's make sure we know what the first signs would look like and exactly what you'd do."

Avoid: "You're fine now, just call if you need anything." It gives no plan and no route back.

Record: final PHQ-9 and residual symptoms, relapse risk factors, the written plan given to the client, and follow-up arrangements.

## Sources note

Recurrence is common: about half recur within 5 years [WHO-CDDR-2024], and 54% of cognitive therapy responders relapse within 2 years without continuation [Vittengl-etal-2007]. Relapse plan content follows NICE [NICE-NG222] and Beck's termination chapter [Beck-2021]. The "below 5" target matches the developers' lower bound for mild depression [Kroenke-etal-2001] and the VA/DoD remission definition [VA-DoD-MDD-2022]; other sources define remission differently, so state yours. See `09-ending.pitfall-ending-with-residual-symptoms` for the general version of this pitfall. Evidence strength: guideline-recommended, supported by randomized trials and individual patient data meta-analyses.
