**Bottom line:** In moderate to severe depression, start with behavior. Thought records demand concentration, energy and hope that the client does not yet have, and an unfinished worksheet becomes evidence for "I can't do anything right." Behavioral activation (BA) works as well as the full cognitive package and holds up better in severe depression [Jacobson-etal-1996; Dimidjian-etal-2006]. Bring in cognitive work once the client is moving.

## What it looks like

- Session 2 or 3 introduces a seven-column thought record as homework.
- The client returns with nothing written, apologizes, and says, "I told you I'm hopeless."
- Sessions turn into debates about whether the client is worthless, while the client still spends most of the day in bed.
- Phrases like "I know it's irrational, but I still feel it" repeat, with no change in mood scores.
- The therapist starts to see the client as "resistant" or "not psychologically minded."

## Why it happens

- Training often stresses cognitive techniques, so therapists reach for them first.
- Negative thoughts are vivid and easy to target; inactivity looks like a symptom rather than a maintaining process.
- Depression slows thinking and narrows attention, and rumination impairs problem solving and action [Nolen-Hoeksema-etal-2008]. Abstract written tasks are hardest at this point.
- Therapists may believe behavioral work is "too simple" for the client's problems. Yet a component trial found BA alone as effective as full cognitive therapy, and it shifted negative thinking too [Jacobson-etal-1996].

## The fix

- If the client is slowed, exhausted or mostly inactive → start with activity monitoring and one or two scheduled, graded activities. Because BA matched antidepressant medication in more severe depression, where cognitive therapy did less well [Dimidjian-etal-2006].
- If cognitive work is already under way and stalling → say so openly and reset. Example: "I think I gave you the hardest tool first. Let's start with what you do in a day, and come back to the thoughts." Because activity scheduling sits early in standard cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [Beck-2021].
- If negative thoughts block activation ("What's the point?") → test them with the activity itself as a small behavioral experiment. Because doing, then rating mood, gives evidence the client can feel.
- If the client completes activities → ask what thoughts came up before and after. Because this bridges naturally into identifying automatic thoughts.
- If homework is not done → shrink the task, set a time and place, and rehearse it in session (see `05-session-structure.homework-design`). Because a planned, specific task is more likely to happen.

Avoid: "If you don't do the thought records, therapy won't work." It adds pressure and confirms failure.

Record: activity level, homework completed, and PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9) trend as you shift the plan.

## Sources note

The sequence of behavioral techniques before cognitive ones for more severely depressed patients goes back to Beck's original manual [Beck-etal-1979]. The trial evidence for leading with behavior comes from a component analysis [Jacobson-etal-1996] and a placebo-controlled trial that found the severity effect [Dimidjian-etal-2006]. BA delivered by junior workers was non-inferior to CBT [Richards-etal-2016], and the United Kingdom (UK) National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) lists individual BA as an option at all severities [NICE-NG222]. Evidence strength: supported by randomized trials. The specific advice on when to add cognitive work is expert consensus.
