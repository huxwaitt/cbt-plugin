**Bottom line:** Two models drive cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for depression. Beck's cognitive model says negative beliefs, activated by stress, produce biased automatic thoughts about self, world and future [Beck-etal-1979; Beck-2008]. The behavioral model says loss of reward, withdrawal and avoidance keep mood low [Dimidjian-etal-2011]. In practice they combine into a few vicious cycles (inactivity, avoidance, rumination, biased thinking, hopelessness) that you draw with the client from a recent low moment and then break, starting with behavior.

## Components

| Component | What it is | Typical depression content |
|---|---|---|
| Early experiences | Loss, criticism, neglect, trauma that shape beliefs | "Nothing I did was good enough" |
| Core beliefs (schemas) | Stable beliefs about self, others, world | "I'm worthless", "I'm unlovable", "I'm a failure" |
| Intermediate beliefs | Rules and assumptions that protect against core beliefs | "If I'm not perfect, I'm a failure" |
| Critical incident | Stressor that matches the belief and activates it | Job loss, breakup, illness [Beck-2008] |
| Automatic thoughts | Quick negative appraisals on the cognitive triad: self, world, future | "I've ruined everything", "It will never get better" [Beck-etal-1979] |
| Information-processing biases | Noticing failures, discounting successes, overgeneralizing | Remembering the one critical remark |
| Behavior | Withdrawal, inactivity, avoidance, staying in bed, reassurance seeking | Cancels plans, stops exercise |
| Rumination | Repetitive, abstract dwelling on causes and meanings of low mood | "Why am I like this?" [Nolen-Hoeksema-etal-2008] |
| Emotions and body | Sadness, guilt, shame, emptiness; fatigue, sleep and appetite change | |

Beck's later model treats early experiences and dysfunctional beliefs as predisposing, and congruent later stressors as precipitating [Beck-2008]. The behavioral model, rooted in Lewinsohn's work, focuses on reduced positive reinforcement and avoidance [Dimidjian-etal-2011].

## How it keeps the problem going

- **Inactivity cycle.** Low energy → doing less → fewer rewards and achievements → "I'm lazy and useless" → lower mood and energy.
- **Avoidance cycle.** Avoiding hard tasks or people brings short-term relief, then problems pile up and isolation grows, confirming "I can't cope" [Dimidjian-etal-2011].
- **Rumination cycle.** Dwelling on "why" deepens low mood, strengthens negative thinking, impairs problem solving and displaces action [Nolen-Hoeksema-etal-2008]. Abstract, evaluative rumination is the unhelpful kind; concrete, specific thinking is more constructive [Watkins-2008].
- **Biased-processing cycle.** Negative beliefs filter what is noticed and remembered, so each day seems to confirm them.
- **Hopelessness cycle.** "Nothing will change" → no attempts → nothing changes → belief confirmed. Hopelessness also signals suicide risk [Beck-etal-1985].

Behavior change alone shifts thinking: in a component trial, behavioral activation (BA) alone matched full cognitive therapy and changed negative thinking as much [Jacobson-etal-1996]. So the cycles can be entered at any point.

## Drawing it with the client

1. Pick one recent, specific moment when mood dropped. Ask: "Where were you? What went through your mind? What did you feel in your body? What did you do next?"
2. Write it as a five-part diagram: situation, thoughts, emotions, body, behavior (see `03-formulation.five-part-model`).
3. Ask what happened after the behavior: "And when you stayed in bed, what happened to your mood by evening?" Draw that arrow back to mood. That is the first vicious cycle.
4. Add a second example and look for the same pattern. Name it in the client's words ("the duvet trap").
5. Later, once trust and energy allow, add beliefs and early experiences in a longitudinal diagram [Beck-2021] (see `03-formulation.cognitive-conceptualization-diagram`).
6. Check fit: "Does this match how it works for you? What would you change?"

When depression is severe and concentration poor, draw one cycle only, use few words, and give the client a copy.

Example phrasings:
- "It sounds like the tiredness tells you to rest, and resting makes sense, but then the day feels empty and the thoughts get louder. Is that right?"
- "If this cycle keeps going round, where could we break in first?"
- Avoid: "Your negative thinking is causing your depression." It blames the client and skips the behavioral loop.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Cycle | Target | Main methods |
|---|---|---|
| Inactivity and avoidance | Re-engage with rewarding, valued and necessary activity | Activity monitoring and scheduling, graded tasks [Beck-2021; Dimidjian-etal-2011] (`behavioral.behavioral-activation`) |
| Automatic thoughts and biases | Test and balance hot thoughts | Thought records, Socratic questioning, behavioral experiments (`cognitive.cognitive-restructuring`) |
| Rumination | Shift from abstract "why" to concrete "how" and action | Rumination-focused CBT helped residual depression in a phase II trial [Watkins-etal-2011] (`cognitive.worry-and-rumination-techniques`) |
| Hopelessness | Early small wins, risk monitoring, safety plan | Activation plus risk review (`02-risk.safety-planning`) |
| Rules and core beliefs | Modify the vulnerability that drives relapse | Belief work later in treatment (`cognitive.core-belief-work`) |
| Relapse vulnerability | Notice early warning signs and respond differently | Relapse plan, mindfulness-based approaches (`depression.relapse-prevention-mbct`) |

Order matters: start with the behavioral cycle and hopelessness, then thoughts, then beliefs (see `depression.treatment`).
