**Bottom line:** Schema therapy extends cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for clients whose problems are chronic, pervasive and part of who they are, adding childhood origins, experiential techniques, the therapy relationship and coping styles [Young-etal-2003]. Consider it for borderline personality disorder (BPD) and for cluster C, paranoid, histrionic or narcissistic personality disorder, where trials show it beats usual care [Arntz-etal-2022] [Bamelis-etal-2014]. It is long and needs specific training, so refer or train rather than improvise.

## The principle

Young developed schema therapy for "treatment failures" of standard CBT [Young-etal-2003]. Such clients often break the assumptions CBT relies on: they may not do homework, may not access thoughts and feelings, hold beliefs rigidly, find a working alliance hard, and present with vague, lifelong problems rather than clear targets [Young-etal-2003].

The model has 18 early maladaptive schemas in 5 domains, three coping styles (surrender, avoidance and overcompensation) and schema modes, the shifting states that matter most in BPD [Young-etal-2003]. Treatment has an assessment and education phase, then a change phase that blends cognitive work (building a case against the schema, flash cards), experiential work (imagery and dialogues), behavioral pattern-breaking, and the relationship. Two relationship stances are central: empathic confrontation and limited reparenting, meaning meeting unmet needs within professional limits [Young-etal-2003]. It treats chronic characterological problems, not acute symptoms [Young-etal-2003].

## Guidance

- If acute symptoms dominate, such as a full major depressive episode or recurrent panic attacks → treat them first with disorder-specific CBT and revisit schema work afterward. Because schema therapy is designed for chronic characterological aspects once acute symptoms have abated [Young-etal-2003].
- If a client with BPD is choosing a structured therapy and wants to work on childhood patterns and identity → offer schema therapy as an evidence-based option alongside dialectical behavior therapy (DBT). Because the American Psychiatric Association (APA) lists schema-focused therapy among psychotherapies that beat comparison treatments on some outcomes [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].
- If reducing frequent self-harm is the main goal → prefer DBT first. Because DBT has the most trials and reduced self-harm compared with usual care [Storebo-etal-2020] and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends considering it for women with BPD when this is the priority [NICE-CG78].
- If the client has avoidant, dependent, obsessive-compulsive, paranoid, histrionic or narcissistic personality disorder → consider about 50 sessions of schema therapy. Because it produced more recovery than usual care in a 323-patient trial [Bamelis-etal-2014].
- If standard CBT stalls because the client cannot identify thoughts, avoids feelings or keeps re-enacting the same relationship pattern with you → add schema-informed formulation (schemas, coping styles, modes) and discuss referral. Because these are the patterns schema therapy was built for [Young-etal-2003].
- If you are not trained in schema therapy → do not run imagery rescripting of childhood abuse on your own; refer, or get training and supervision. Because therapists trained with practical exercises got better results than lecture-trained ones [Bamelis-etal-2014].
- If antisocial personality disorder is the main problem → set modest expectations and consider group-based cognitive and behavioral programs aimed at impulsivity and offending. Because NICE recommends considering these [NICE-CG77], and evidence for any psychological therapy, including schema therapy, is low or very low certainty [Gibbon-etal-2020].
- If a client fears the end of a long therapy → plan a tapered ending. Because in the largest schema therapy trial sessions tapered to monthly over year 2, and improvement continued after treatment ended [Arntz-etal-2022].

**Dose to expect**

| Format | Dose |
|---|---|
| Individual, BPD | Twice weekly for 3 years [GiesenBloo-etal-2006] |
| Individual plus group, BPD | Twice weekly in year 1, tapering to monthly by the end of year 2 [Arntz-etal-2022] |
| Cluster C and some other personality disorders | 50 sessions [Bamelis-etal-2014] |

## Example phrasings

- Explaining the model: "Some of the rules you live by were learned early, when they protected you. Schema therapy looks at where they came from and helps you meet the needs underneath in new ways."
- Naming a mode: "It sounds like a very harsh voice showed up just now, telling you that you deserved it. Can we look at that voice together?"
- Empathic confrontation: "I can see why you expect me to drop you; that has happened a lot. And when you cancel before I can, you never get to find out if I would."
- Discussing referral: "We've made progress on the panic. The pattern in your relationships seems older and deeper. A therapy built for that could help. Can I tell you about it?"
- Avoid: "Your schemas are sabotaging therapy."

## Evidence

- **BPD, individual:** over 3 years, more schema therapy patients recovered (relative risk 2.18) or reliably improved than with transference-focused psychotherapy, and fewer dropped out [GiesenBloo-etal-2006].
- **BPD, group formats, 495 patients in 5 countries:** combined individual and group schema therapy beat usual care (Cohen d 1.14) and predominantly group schema therapy (d 0.84); group-only did not differ from usual care [Arntz-etal-2022].
- **Other personality disorders:** schema therapy beat usual care and clarification-oriented psychotherapy on recovery at 3 years, and had lower dropout than usual care; differences showed on interview measures, not self-report [Bamelis-etal-2014].
- **Across BPD therapies:** no type was clearly superior to another [Storebo-etal-2020]. Treat schema therapy as one good option, not the only one.
