**Bottom line:** For clients who grew up with emotions dismissed, a fast move to "let's look at the evidence" can feel like one more invalidation and can escalate the very emotion you want to reduce [Crowell-etal-2009]. Validate what is valid first, then move to change, and keep switching between the two [Linehan-2025]. The goal is balance, not validation instead of change.

## What it looks like

- The client describes a painful rejection, and the therapist's first response is "What's the evidence he doesn't care?"
- The client is crying or furious, and the therapist moves to the thought record or agenda.
- The client says "So it's my fault?" or "You sound like my mother," then goes silent, leaves early or skips the next session.
- Homework is set but not done, and the therapist labels the client "non-compliant" or "not ready."
- The reverse error also occurs: the therapist only soothes and validates, and nothing changes for months.

## Why it happens

- Standard cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) assumes clients can quickly access and report thoughts and will follow procedures; many clients with personality disorders cannot yet [Young-etal-2003].
- In the biosocial model, invalidation helps create and maintain emotion dysregulation, so dismissal in therapy re-enacts the problem [Crowell-etal-2009].
- Therapists under time pressure or anxious about risk rush to fix.
- Intense emotion and anger toward the therapist can provoke defensiveness.

## The fix

1. **Lead with validation when arousal is high.** Reflect the emotion and name what makes sense about it. "Of course you were hurt. You'd been waiting all evening and he didn't call."
2. **Validate the valid, not the invalid.** You can validate the feeling and the wish without agreeing that the client is worthless or that self-harm was the answer.
3. **Bring arousal down before cognitive work.** Use a distress tolerance skill if needed (see `personality-disorders.dbt-informed-skills`).
4. **Then turn to change, with permission.** "Can we look at what happened next, so you have more options next time?"
5. **Balance acceptance and change throughout.** This balance is a core dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) strategy [Linehan-2025].
6. **Use the relationship.** When the client reacts to you, show empathy for the reaction while pointing out how it reflects an old pattern, which schema therapy calls empathic confrontation [Young-etal-2003]. The relationship is a vehicle for change, not only a context [Beck-etal-2015].
7. **Offer explanations for interventions,** respectful and validating attitudes, and judicious feedback [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].

Avoid: "That's catastrophizing." Try: "It makes sense your mind jumped to the worst. It's been through a lot of real losses. Let's see if this one fits the pattern."

## Sources note

The balance of validation and change comes from DBT [Linehan-2025] and the biosocial model [Crowell-etal-2009]. Schema therapy describes the same tension through empathic confrontation [Young-etal-2003]. The American Psychiatric Association (APA) treats alliance building as a focus from the first session in borderline personality disorder [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024]. These sources support the stance; trials have not isolated validation timing as a single active ingredient.
