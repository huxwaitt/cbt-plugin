**Bottom line:** In borderline personality disorder (BPD), reacting to every crisis with a new plan, a new drug or an admission replaces the treatment it was meant to protect. Agree a framework and crisis plan at the start, keep medication time-limited and reviewed, and take your own reactions to supervision [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024]. Crises then become material for the planned work.

## What it looks like

- Sessions are spent on the latest emergency; agreed goals and skills practice are never reached.
- After each crisis a new medication or dose increase is added; the client is on several drugs with no clear target.
- Admissions follow each crisis, often without an agreed purpose or length.
- Contact grows: long calls at night, extra sessions, messages answered at all hours, then sudden withdrawal when the therapist burns out.
- Team members disagree sharply about the client, some protective and some punitive.

## Why it happens

- Risk anxiety: doing something feels safer than holding to a plan.
- Strong reactions in clinicians, from wanting to rescue to frustration and anger, are expected in BPD work and can bend decisions [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].
- Splitting: polarized views of a client across a team weaken shared decisions [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].
- Half or more of patients with BPD receive several medications at once [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024], so adding another feels routine.

## The fix

1. **Set the framework early:** session times, cancellations, your after-hours availability, and a written crisis plan [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024]. The plan names triggers, self-management steps and out-of-hours numbers, and is shared with the primary care doctor [NICE-CG78].
2. **Use the plan in crises.** Follow the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) crisis principles: calm, validating, understand before solving, explore options before admission, and agree follow-up [NICE-CG78].
3. **Admit only for a reason.** Try crisis resolution or home treatment first; agree the purpose and length of any admission in advance [NICE-CG78].
4. **Keep medication in its place.** No drug treats BPD itself [StoffersWinterling-etal-2022]. Any medication should be time-limited, aimed at a measurable target symptom and adjunctive to therapy, and reviewed at least every 6 months [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024]. Crisis sedation should last no longer than 1 week [NICE-CG78]. Frequent dose changes in response to crises are rarely effective [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].
5. **Return to the plan next session.** Do a chain analysis of the crisis and pick the skill or belief target it reveals.
6. **Set contact limits you can keep.** If you do not take after-hours calls, say who does [Linehan-2025].
7. **Get support.** Use supervision or a consultation team; NICE asks teams to review their tolerance of high-risk clients at least yearly [NICE-CG78]. Coordinate one plan across all clinicians [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].

Example: "Last night sounded awful, and I'm glad you used the crisis number. Let's spend 15 minutes on what happened and what helped, then get back to the plan we agreed, because that's what will make nights like that rarer."

## Sources note

Framework, countertransference, splitting and medication principles come from the American Psychiatric Association (APA) 2024 guideline, mostly rated 1C or 2C [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024]. Crisis, admission and team support rules are NICE recommendations [NICE-CG78]. The Cochrane drug review supports the limited role of medication [StoffersWinterling-etal-2022].
