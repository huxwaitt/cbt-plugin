**Bottom line:** Clients with borderline personality disorder (BPD) prefer to be told their diagnosis, telling them does not lower satisfaction, and discussing it is part of good practice [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024]. Naming it, with compassion and with the facts that most people improve, opens the door to matched treatment. Never use the label to blame or to exclude [NICE-CG78].

## What it looks like

- The notes or referral letter say "BPD traits" or "emotionally unstable," but no one has told the client.
- The client is treated for depression, anxiety or bipolar disorder for years with little benefit. Misdiagnosis leads to drug treatments that rarely succeed [Gunderson-etal-2018].
- Staff describe the client as "manipulative," "attention seeking" or "a borderline."
- The diagnosis or self-harm history is used to refuse a service or end care.
- The client finds the diagnosis in their records and feels betrayed.

## Why it happens

- Clinicians fear upsetting the client, harming the relationship or exposing them to stigma [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].
- Old beliefs that BPD is untreatable or permanent persist.
- Stigma is widespread, including discrimination within health care [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].
- Frustration with self-harm or anger leaks into language.

## The fix

1. **Discuss the diagnosis collaboratively,** as the American Psychiatric Association (APA) recommends, with psychoeducation about the disorder and treatment [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].
2. **Link it to the client's experience.** Use their examples: relationships, sense of self, emotional reactions. Compassionate psychoeducation validates these experiences [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024].
3. **Give hope with facts.** In a 10-year study of former inpatients with BPD, 93% had a remission lasting at least 2 years [Zanarini-etal-2010], and BPD-tailored psychotherapy reduces severity compared with usual care [Storebo-etal-2020]. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks clinicians to explain that recovery is possible [NICE-CG78].
4. **Invite their view.** Ask what fits, what doesn't, and what the word means to them.
5. **Keep access open.** NICE states that people with BPD should not be excluded from any health or social care service because of the diagnosis or self-harm [NICE-CG78].
6. **Watch your language.** Describe behaviors and their functions, not character.

Example: "From what you've told me and the questionnaires, what you're describing fits something called borderline personality disorder. The name isn't great; it doesn't mean you're on the border of anything. It describes intense emotions, fear of being left, and acting fast to stop the pain. The important part is that it's treatable, and most people get much better. What do you make of that?"

Avoid: "You have a personality disorder, so there's not much we can do."

## Sources note

The case for disclosure rests on APA statement 4, rated 1C (low-certainty evidence, strong recommendation) [APA-BPD-Guideline-2024], and on NICE principles of hope and non-exclusion [NICE-CG78]. Recovery figures come from a 10-year follow-up of former inpatients; only 50% reached full recovery with good functioning [Zanarini-etal-2010], so balance hope with honesty.
