**Bottom line:** For adults with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) means teaching and rehearsing compensatory skills (organizing, planning, managing distraction), then working on the thoughts that block their use. The best-tested protocol is 12 weekly individual sessions [Safren-etal-2010]; a 12-session group alternative exists [Solanto-etal-2010]. It is supported by randomized trials, mostly as an add-on to medication, but the evidence is mostly low quality [Lopez-etal-2018].

## When to use

- Residual impairment despite stable medication (the population in the main trials) [Safren-etal-2010].
- The adult declines medication, struggles to adhere, cannot tolerate it or finds it ineffective, or has residual impairment on it. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) then advises considering non-drug treatment: at minimum a structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD, with regular follow-up, which may include CBT [NICE-NG87].
- Comorbid low mood or anxiety linked to repeated failure; CBT reduced depression and anxiety versus waitlist [Lopez-etal-2018].

## How it works

Skills are the active ingredient. The strongest programs were short (about 10 to 12 sessions), manualized, used handouts or workbooks, drilled specific compensatory skills with practice between sessions, and addressed thoughts and feelings only as they blocked skill use [Knouse-Safren-2010]. Psychoeducation alone is not enough [Knouse-Safren-2010]. See `adhd.cbt-model`.

## Steps

Outline based on the trial protocol, 12 sessions of about 50 minutes [Safren-etal-2010]:

| Sessions | Module | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 4 | Psychoeducation, organizing and planning | ADHD education; one calendar and one task list; prioritizing; problem solving; breaking overwhelming tasks into steps |
| 5 to 6 | Distractibility | Time your attention span; break tasks into chunks that fit it; write distractions down instead of acting on them |
| 7 to 9 | Adaptive thinking | Thought records, thinking errors, rational responses |
| 10 | Procrastination (optional) | Apply all skills to a delayed task |
| 11 | Family member (optional) | Partner or relative joins for support |
| 12 | Relapse prevention | Review what worked; plan for setbacks |

Unused optional sessions become boosters on earlier skills [Safren-etal-2010].

Each session: set the agenda together, review symptoms and medication adherence, review homework, introduce one new skill, and practice it [Knouse-Safren-2010].

## Guidance

- If the client arrives without the planner → spend the first minutes rebuilding it in session and set a fixed daily review time. Because consistent use of a single system, not knowing about it, drives change [Knouse-Safren-2010].
- If homework is not done → shrink the task, rehearse the first step in session, and agree a cue. Because in the group trial each completed home exercise predicted more improvement, while attendance did not [Solanto-etal-2010].
- If the client says "I'll do it this weekend" with no plan → ask for the day, time and first five-minute step, and write it into the calendar before leaving. Because vague intentions are the point where avoidance enters (see `adhd.cbt-model`).
- If the client wants to add many topics → keep to the core modules. Because extra modules were dropped from the protocol for being overwhelming [Knouse-Safren-2010].
- If medication changes during treatment → note it and interpret progress cautiously. Because the main trials required stable medication [Safren-etal-2010].

## Common problems

- Treating it as talk therapy about ADHD; skills must be practiced in session and at home.
- Measuring only symptoms; also track skill use and functioning (on-time bills, deadlines met).
- Expecting thoughts to change first. Start with behavior; cognitive work follows in sessions 7 to 9.

## Evidence

- **Key trials:** in 86 medicated adults, CBT beat relaxation with educational support; response 53% versus 23% by clinician global rating, and responders kept their gains at 6 and 12 months [Safren-etal-2010]. In 31 medicated adults, 56% responded to CBT versus 13% on medication alone [Safren-etal-2005]. In 88 adults, 12 two-hour group sessions of meta-cognitive therapy beat supportive therapy (odds ratio 5.41) [Solanto-etal-2010].
- **Reviews:** a Cochrane review of 14 trials (700 participants) found short-term benefit on core symptoms with low-quality evidence; CBT plus medication beat medication alone on clinician ratings (standardized mean difference, SMD, -0.80; very low quality) [Lopez-etal-2018]. Effects are larger against waitlist (SMD 0.76) than against active controls (SMD 0.43) [Young-etal-2020a]; another meta-analysis found Hedges' g = 0.65 against controls, smaller with active controls [Knouse-etal-2017]. Gains lasted at least 12 months in follow-up studies [LopezPinar-etal-2018].
- **Rater dependence:** in a 2025 network meta-analysis, CBT, mindfulness and psychoeducation improved clinician-rated but not self-rated symptoms; only stimulants and atomoxetine helped on both [Ostinelli-etal-2025].
- **Group format caution:** a large German trial found structured group therapy no better than individual clinical management on blinded symptom ratings, while methylphenidate beat placebo [Philipsen-etal-2015].
- **Guideline status:** NICE NG87 offers adults medication first, with non-drug treatment considered after or alongside it [NICE-NG87]. The United States (US) has no adult ADHD guideline yet; the American Professional Society of ADHD and Related Disorders (APSARD) guideline entered final review in August 2026 and was unpublished at review [APSARD-Guidelines].
