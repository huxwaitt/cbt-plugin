**Bottom line:** For children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), the best-supported psychosocial treatments work through the adults around the child: behavioral parent training, behavioral classroom management and organization skills training [Evans-etal-2014]. Parent training is first-line for preschoolers; from age 6, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends medication plus parent training and classroom interventions, preferably both [Wolraich-etal-2019]. Child-focused cognitive therapy is not a substitute.

## When to use

- **Preschool (4 to 5):** parent training in behavior management (PTBM), and classroom intervention where available, first; methylphenidate only if moderate to severe problems persist [Wolraich-etal-2019]. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the United Kingdom (UK) recommends an ADHD-focused group parent-training program first-line for under-5s [NICE-NG87].
- **Ages 6 to 11:** medication plus PTBM and classroom behavioral intervention (preferably both), with school supports such as an Individualized Education Program (IEP) or 504 plan [Wolraich-etal-2019].
- **Ages 12 to 18:** medication with the adolescent's assent; school-functioning skills training is encouraged [Wolraich-etal-2019].
- **Age 5 and over in the UK:** NICE advises brief ADHD-focused support for all parents, a parent-training program when there are oppositional or conduct symptoms, and a course of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for young people with residual impairment despite benefiting from medication [NICE-NG87].

## How it works

Behavior management changes the antecedents and consequences adults provide, so desired behavior is cued and rewarded and gains generalize as contingencies are faded into natural ones [Evans-etal-2014]. Training interventions (organization skills) teach the child skills, with parents and teachers prompting and rewarding their use [Evans-etal-2014].

## Steps

**Behavioral parent training.** Typical group programs run 8 to 12 weekly sessions of 2 to 2.5 hours [Evans-etal-2014]. They teach age-appropriate expectations, parent-child relationship skills and ways to manage problem behavior [Wolraich-etal-2019]. A common sequence (expert consensus):
1. Psychoeducation on ADHD and why consistent consequences matter.
2. Special time and praise for specific positive behavior.
3. Clear, brief instructions given up close.
4. A home reward system (points or tokens) for target behaviors.
5. Planned ignoring of minor misbehavior; brief time-out or loss of privileges for serious misbehavior.
6. Planning for public places and school links.

**Classroom management.** Daily report cards, point systems, training interventions and academic remediation [Wolraich-etal-2019]. A daily report card names 3 to 5 target behaviors, the teacher rates them each day, and parents give home rewards for meeting goals (expert consensus).

**Organizational skills training.** Twenty individual clinic sessions over 10 to 12 weeks teach tracking assignments, managing materials, time management and task planning, with parents and teachers prompting and rewarding skill use [Abikoff-etal-2013]. A school-delivered version runs sixteen 20-minute sessions over 11 weeks [Evans-etal-2014].

**Adolescents.** Training focused on school functioning helps consistently [Wolraich-etal-2019]. For teens aged 14 to 18 with residual symptoms on medication, an adapted adult CBT protocol with parent involvement is an option [Sprich-etal-2016].

## Guidance

- If parents feel blamed → say "ADHD isn't caused by parenting, but parenting strategies can make a big difference day to day." Because engagement falls if training is heard as blame (expert consensus).
- If the child behaves well in clinic but not at home or school → work through parent and teacher, not just the child. Because generalization comes from changing contingencies where behavior happens [Evans-etal-2014].
- If the school will not engage → help parents request an evaluation for an IEP or 504 plan. Because the AAP calls school supports a necessary part of treatment [Wolraich-etal-2019].
- If a parent asks for social skills groups → explain that clinic social skills training has not been shown to work, and offer peer interventions in natural settings. Because it was rated "tested and does not work" [SCCAP-ADHD-Site].
- If medication is started → keep behavioral treatment going. Because behavioral effects tend to persist, while medication effects stop when it stops [Wolraich-etal-2019].

## Common problems

- Rewards set too far away; young children need frequent, immediate rewards.
- Inconsistent follow-through between parents or between home and school.
- Parent depression or ADHD undermining consistency; screen parents and refer when needed (expert consensus).

## Evidence

- Behavioral parent training, classroom management, peer interventions and organization training were rated well established; neurofeedback possibly efficacious; cognitive training experimental [Evans-etal-2014]. A 2018 update found effects increasingly nuanced by age and treatment features [Evans-etal-2018].
- With blinded raters, behavioral interventions improved parenting and conduct problems but not core ADHD symptoms [Daley-etal-2014] [SonugaBarke-etal-2013]; a broader meta-analysis of 174 studies found large effects on behavior [Fabiano-etal-2009].
- In the 14-month Multimodal Treatment Study of ADHD (MTA, 579 children), managed medication beat behavioral treatment for core symptoms; combined treatment added modest gains in oppositional behavior, social skills, parent-child relations and reading [MTA-Group-1999].
- In 158 children in grades 3 to 5, 60% no longer met organizational impairment criteria after either skills training or a contingency-based program without skills teaching, versus 3% on waitlist [Abikoff-etal-2013].
