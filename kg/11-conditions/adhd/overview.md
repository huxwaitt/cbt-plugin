**Bottom line:** Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder: a persistent pattern of inattention, hyperactivity-impulsivity or both, beginning in childhood, present in more than one setting and impairing daily life [APA-DSM5TR-2022] [WHO-ICD11-2022]. About 5.9% of young people and 2.5% of adults meet criteria worldwide [Faraone-etal-2021]. Medication treats core symptoms best; cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for adults and behavioral parent and classroom programs for children target the functional impairment that remains [Faraone-etal-2021] [Wolraich-etal-2019].

## Diagnostic essentials

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) requires, paraphrased [APA-DSM5TR-2022] [Epstein-Loren-2013] [APA-ADHD-Page]:
- **Two symptom lists of nine each:** inattention (for example losing track of details, poor sustained attention, disorganization, forgetfulness) and hyperactivity-impulsivity (for example fidgeting, restlessness, talking excessively, interrupting).
- **Count:** at least 6 symptoms from either list up to age 16; at least 5 from 17 years on. Symptoms must be inconsistent with developmental level and present for at least 6 months.
- **Onset:** several symptoms present before age 12 (raised from age 7 in the fourth edition, DSM-IV).
- **Settings:** several symptoms present in two or more settings, such as home, school or work, with friends or relatives.
- **Impairment:** clear evidence that symptoms interfere with or reduce the quality of social, academic or occupational functioning.
- **Exclusions:** not occurring only during schizophrenia or another psychotic disorder, and not better explained by another mental disorder such as a mood, anxiety, dissociative or personality disorder, or substance intoxication or withdrawal. Symptoms are not merely defiance or failure to understand instructions. Autism is no longer an exclusion, so both can be diagnosed together.
- **Specifiers:** combined, predominantly inattentive, or predominantly hyperactive-impulsive presentation (DSM-IV called these subtypes); in partial remission; current severity mild, moderate or severe.

DSM-5-TR (2022) kept the substance of the DSM-5 (2013) ADHD criteria [APA-DSM5TR-2022]. The 2013 changes were the onset age, the adult threshold, adult examples, and allowing comorbid autism [Epstein-Loren-2013].

**International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) differences.** ICD-11 codes ADHD as 6A05 with the same three presentations (6A05.0 inattentive, 6A05.1 hyperactive-impulsive, 6A05.2 combined) plus other specified and unspecified. It also requires at least 6 months of symptoms, onset before age 12 and effects across settings, but sets no symptom count; it asks for symptoms outside the normal range for age and intellectual functioning [WHO-ICD11-2022].

## Course and prevalence

- **Children:** 5.9% of youths meet criteria in pooled studies; ADHD is about twice as common in boys as in girls [Faraone-etal-2021]. In the United States (US), parents report a current diagnosis for 11.7% of children aged 3 to 17 (2024 survey), higher than research prevalence [CDC-ADHD-Data].
- **Adults:** 2.5% to 2.8% in meta-analyses [Faraone-etal-2021]. In a 2023 US survey, 6.0% of adults reported a current diagnosis and 55.9% of them were diagnosed in adulthood [Staley-etal-2024].
- **Course:** hyperactivity often eases with age while inattention persists (expert consensus). About 1 in 6 youths still meets full criteria at 25, and about half show residual impairment [Faraone-etal-2021].

## Differential and comorbidity

Ask whether the problem began in childhood and is present across settings, or began with a mood, anxiety, sleep or substance episode.
- **Depression and anxiety:** poor concentration that starts with the episode and lifts when mood improves (expert consensus); see `depression.overview` and `gad.overview`.
- **Bipolar disorder:** episodic distractibility and overactivity, with a change from the usual self (expert consensus); see `bipolar-and-psychosis.overview`.
- **Learning disorders, sleep apnea, thyroid disease, head injury, and substance or steroid use** can mimic ADHD [APA-ADHD-Page] [Wolraich-etal-2019].
- **Missed or late diagnosis:** girls and women are under-recognized and may be misdiagnosed with mood or personality disorders; see `adhd.underdiagnosis-in-women-and-adults`. For emotional dysregulation, sleep and later life, see `adhd.emotional-dysregulation-and-associated-features`.

Comorbidity is the rule. Nearly 78% of US children with ADHD have at least one other condition, most often behavior problems and anxiety [CDC-ADHD-Data]. ADHD often co-occurs with depression, bipolar disorder, autism, anxiety, oppositional defiant and conduct disorder, eating disorders and substance use disorders [Faraone-etal-2021]. People with ADHD are almost three times as likely to be nicotine dependent and 50% more likely to have a drug or alcohol use disorder [Faraone-etal-2021].

## CBT approach in brief

- **Adults:** skills-based CBT (organizing and planning, managing distractibility, adaptive thinking, procrastination) improves symptoms and function, usually as an add-on when medication leaves residual problems. See `adhd.cbt-model`, `adhd.treatment` and `adhd.adult-cbt-modules`.
- **Children:** behavioral parent training is first-line for preschoolers; for ages 6 to 11, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends medication with parent training and/or classroom behavioral interventions, and for adolescents medication with the teen's assent plus training or behavioral interventions [Wolraich-etal-2019]. See `adhd.children-and-parents`.
- **Medication:** see `adhd.medication-and-cbt`.

## Risk notes

- **Suicide:** meta-analyses find about twice the rate of suicide attempts, more than three times the rate of suicidal ideation and more than six times the rate of suicide in people with ADHD; risk rises steeply with a further psychiatric diagnosis [Faraone-etal-2021]. Screen at intake and when mood drops, and follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`.
- **Accidents:** 40% to 50% higher risk of accidental injury, and a small rise in premature death, mostly from accidents [Faraone-etal-2021]. Ask about driving, speeding and injuries.
- **Crisis contacts:** if risk is present, make a safety plan and arrange urgent help. US: call or text 988 (free, 24 hours) [Lifeline-988], or 911 for immediate danger. United Kingdom (UK): Samaritans 116 123, free and open 24 hours [Samaritans-Site], National Health Service (NHS) 111, or 999.
