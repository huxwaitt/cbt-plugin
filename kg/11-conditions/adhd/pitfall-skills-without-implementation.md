**Bottom line:** Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a problem of doing what one knows, not of knowing what to do [Knouse-Safren-2010]. Explaining strategies is not treatment. Skills must be rehearsed in session, cued at the moment they are needed, and checked every session until they are habits [Ramsay-Rostain-2015] [Solanto-etal-2010].

## What it looks like

- The client says "That's a great idea" to each strategy and returns without the planner.
- Homework review takes a minute ("I forgot") and the session moves on to new material.
- Several tools are introduced at once: an app, a paper planner, a whiteboard.
- Sessions turn into discussion of the week's stresses, with no skill practice.
- Progress is judged by insight ("I understand myself better") rather than by deadlines met.

## Why it happens

- Clients with ADHD are often articulate and knowledgeable about their condition, so understanding is mistaken for change.
- Therapists trained in talk-based cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) under-use rehearsal and in-session practice.
- Forgetting homework is itself an ADHD symptom, and both parties avoid naming it.
- Broad programs feel richer but dilute practice; narrower skill-focused programs performed best [Knouse-Safren-2010].

## The fix

1. **One system.** Agree a single calendar and a single task list, and stop adding tools [Safren-etal-2010].
2. **Rehearse in session.** Have the client enter this week's tasks into the system before leaving, and practice the first step of the hardest task.
3. **Cue the point of performance.** Link daily review to an existing habit (after coffee, before opening email) and use phone alarms or visual reminders [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
4. **Write if-then plans.** "If it's 8:30 on a weekday, then I check my task list" [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
5. **Review first, every time.** Open each session with homework review and troubleshooting before new content; in the group trial completed home exercises predicted improvement [Solanto-etal-2010].
6. **When homework is missed, analyze it.** Ask what got in the way at the moment it was due, and adjust the cue, the size of the task or the thought that permitted delay.
7. **Measure use.** Track how many days the system was checked and how many tasks were completed, not just symptom scores.

Example: "Let's not add anything new today. Show me the planner, and let's make this week's plan together right now."

Avoid: "Remember to use the planner this week." A reminder to remember is the very thing ADHD makes unreliable.

## Sources note
