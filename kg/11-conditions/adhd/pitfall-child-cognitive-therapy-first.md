**Bottom line:** For children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), the effective psychosocial treatments work through parents and teachers: behavioral parent training, classroom management and organization skills training [Evans-etal-2014]. Individual cognitive or talk therapy with a young child does not change behavior where it happens, and clinic-based social skills training has been tested and found not to work [SCCAP-ADHD-Site].

## What it looks like

- Weekly individual sessions with a 7-year-old about feelings or "stop and think" self-talk; parents wait outside.
- The child does well in the therapy room, but reports from home and school are unchanged.
- A referral to a social skills group as the main treatment.
- No contact with the school; no daily report card; no home reward system.

## Why it happens

- Therapists trained in individual cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) default to it.
- Parents may hope the child can be "fixed" in sessions without changes at home.
- Children often behave well in short, novel, one-to-one settings, which looks like progress.
- School contact takes time and consent.

## The fix

1. **Start with parents.** Offer behavioral parent training; for preschoolers it is first-line in United States (US) pediatric guidance [Wolraich-etal-2019], and a group parent-training program is first-line for under-5s in United Kingdom (UK) guidance [NICE-NG87].
2. **Bring in the school.** Set up a daily report card or point system with the teacher, and support requests for school plans; the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) calls school supports a necessary part of treatment [Wolraich-etal-2019].
3. **Teach organization skills with adult support.** For school-age children, organization training with parent and teacher prompting is well established [Evans-etal-2014].
4. **Plan for generalization.** Change consequences in the places behavior happens, then fade them into natural ones [Evans-etal-2014].
5. **Reserve adapted CBT for adolescents.** For teens on medication with residual symptoms, an adapted adult protocol with parent involvement has trial support [Sprich-etal-2016]. UK guidance also suggests CBT for young people with residual impairment on medication [NICE-NG87]. Even then, AAP notes that cognitive approaches have not shown meaningful functional gains, while school-functioning skills training has [Wolraich-etal-2019].

Say to parents: "The most effective help for ADHD at this age happens at home and at school, so most of our work will be with you and, if you agree, with the teacher."

Avoid: "We'll work with him on his own, and you'll see the change at home." It promises an effect the evidence does not support.

## Sources note

Evidence levels come from systematic reviews using clinical child psychology task force criteria [Evans-etal-2014] [SCCAP-ADHD-Site] and the AAP guideline [Wolraich-etal-2019]. Child-directed CBT for comorbid anxiety or depression is a separate question; see `13-context-culture-development.children-and-adolescents`.
