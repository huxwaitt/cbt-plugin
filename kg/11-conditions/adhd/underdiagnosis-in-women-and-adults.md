**Bottom line:** Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is diagnosed less often and later in girls and women, partly through referral bias, quieter symptoms, masking, and anxiety or depression treated in its place [Young-etal-2020b] [Kooij-etal-2025]. Many adults of every gender are now diagnosed for the first time [Staley-etal-2024]. A cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) therapist keeps ADHD on the differential when internalizing problems never fully resolve, and helps late-diagnosed clients rework a lifetime of self-blame.

## What it is

Underdiagnosis means ADHD is present but unrecognized; late diagnosis means it is recognized only in adolescence or adulthood. Clinic referrals of boys exceed girls by ratios from 3:1 to 16:1, against about 3:1 in community samples, which suggests many girls are missed [Young-etal-2020b]. In adult samples the sex ratio is closer to 1:1 [Kooij-etal-2019]. In a 2023 United States (US) survey, 55.9% of adults with a current ADHD diagnosis were diagnosed in adulthood [Staley-etal-2024]. In the United Kingdom (UK), the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) says ADHD is thought to be under-recognized in girls and women: they are less likely to be referred, and may be more likely to have undiagnosed ADHD or an incorrect diagnosis of another condition [NICE-NG87].

## Why it matters in CBT

Women with unrecognized ADHD often seek help for anxiety or depression instead [Kooij-etal-2025]. If ADHD is missed, CBT treats downstream problems while the source keeps producing them. Women diagnosed late describe years of having their struggles blamed on character, which they came to believe: "different" and "not good enough" [Babinski-Libsack-2025]. That belief is a formulation target in its own right.

## Key details

- **Presentation.** Hyperactive-impulsive symptoms are often milder in girls, so the picture can look mainly inattentive: distracted, disorganized, overwhelmed. Inattentive girls are more often missed [Young-etal-2020b].
- **Internalizing symptoms.** Low self-esteem, anxiety and mood disorders are frequent in females with ADHD and may be read as primary conditions [Kooij-etal-2019].
- **Masking and compensation.** Many girls and women camouflage difficulties with compensatory strategies, which can lead clinicians to underestimate them [Young-etal-2020b]. Coping often fails at transitions such as leaving school, starting work or managing relationships [Kooij-etal-2019]. High intelligence, gifted programs, family help with homework and people-pleasing can also hide ADHD [Kooij-etal-2019] [Babinski-Libsack-2025].
- **Referral bias.** In vignette studies, teachers more often referred identical behavior when the child had a boy's name, and parents may underrate girls' hyperactivity [Young-etal-2020b].
- **Hormones (emerging evidence).** Symptoms may worsen across the menstrual cycle, in pregnancy and at menopause, but evidence does not support adjusting treatment to the cycle [Young-etal-2020b]. In a self-report study of 209 women with ADHD, 45% reported premenstrual depressive symptoms (28% in the general population) and 58% postpartum depressive symptoms (19%). Mothers with ADHD have 5 to 6 times the risk of postpartum depression and anxiety disorders. Reviewers call current knowledge "relatively limited," and no study has tracked executive function across menopause in women with ADHD [Kooij-etal-2025].
- **Misdiagnosis.** Low self-esteem, mood lability and irritability can be mistaken for dysthymia, cyclothymia, bipolar disorder or borderline personality disorder (BPD). In ADHD, anger is usually brief and unplanned, and self-harm, identity disturbance and abandonment fears are less intense than in BPD, but both are trait-like and can co-occur [Kooij-etal-2019].
- **The experience of late diagnosis.** Across 35 qualitative studies, adults commonly felt relief, then identity questions, emotional turmoil and regret at years without diagnosis; no study reported regret about the diagnosis itself [Ginapp-etal-2022]. In a study of 14 women diagnosed as adults, 64% said it changed their life for the better [Babinski-Libsack-2025].
- **Scope of practice.** In the UK, only a specialist or other appropriately qualified clinician with ADHD training should diagnose [NICE-NG87]. In the US, check your license and state rules.

## In the session

- If an adult has had several partly helpful courses of treatment for anxiety or depression plus lifelong disorganization → screen for ADHD and arrange a full assessment (`adhd.assessment`). Because women often present with these conditions first and ADHD is missed [Kooij-etal-2025].
- If no one noticed problems in childhood → ask how the client got by: extra hours, parental help, perfectionism, avoiding hard subjects. Because compensation masks symptoms, yet several symptoms before age 12 are still required [Kooij-etal-2019] [APA-DSM5TR-2022].
- If the family informant may also have ADHD → ask for specific examples rather than judgments of what was "normal." Because ADHD is highly heritable and affected relatives may misjudge typical behavior [Young-etal-2020b].
- If focus or mood worsens around puberty, after childbirth or around menopause → have the client track symptoms through that period and share the record with the prescriber. Because tracking establishes patterns [Young-etal-2020b].
- If a BPD or bipolar diagnosis coexists with lifelong inattention → review the developmental history, not only current affect, and consider both conditions. Because the features overlap and can co-occur [Kooij-etal-2019].
- If the client is newly diagnosed → make room for relief and grief, then re-examine old evidence: "You called yourself lazy. What were you actually up against?" Because diagnosis offers new language and less self-blame, while regret for lost years is common [Babinski-Libsack-2025] [Ginapp-etal-2022].
- If low mood around childbirth, menstruation or menopause includes suicidal thoughts or self-harm → assess risk that day with `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment` and involve the prescriber or perinatal team. Because postpartum depression risk is raised [Kooij-etal-2025]. US: call or text 988, or 911 [Lifeline-988]; UK: Samaritans 116 123 [Samaritans-Site], National Health Service (NHS) 111 or 999 [NHS-Urgent-Help].
- Avoid: "Everyone's a bit ADHD these days." It dismisses the impairment that brought the client in.
