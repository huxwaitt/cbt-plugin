**Bottom line:** Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is diagnosed from a clinical history, not a test: symptoms meeting the count, childhood onset, presence in two or more settings, impairment, and other causes ruled out [APA-DSM5TR-2022] [Wolraich-etal-2019]. Rating scales screen and track severity; informants and records establish onset and pervasiveness. In many settings only specialist or licensed clinicians may make the diagnosis [NICE-NG87].

## When to use

- A child or adolescent aged 4 to 18 has academic or behavior problems with inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity; the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says to start an evaluation [Wolraich-etal-2019].
- An adult describes lifelong disorganization, lateness, missed deadlines or restlessness, or screens positive.
- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for another problem stalls because the client cannot keep appointments or track homework.

## Steps

1. **Screen.** Adults: the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS). The 6-item ASRS v1.1 screener had sensitivity 68.7% and specificity 99.5% in a population sample [Kessler-etal-2005a]. The weighted 6-item version for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) scores 0 to 25, positive at 14 or higher: sensitivity 91.4% and specificity 96.0% in the population, specificity 74.0% in a specialty clinic [Ustun-etal-2017]. Children: the National Institute for Children's Health Quality (NICHQ) Vanderbilt Assessment Scales, parent and teacher forms.
2. **Score the Vanderbilt correctly.** A symptom counts when rated 2 or 3 (often, very often). Inattentive presentation needs 6 of the 9 inattention items; hyperactive-impulsive needs 6 of the 9 hyperactivity items; combined needs both. Impairment is also required: a 4 on at least two performance items, or a 5 on at least one. Built-in screens flag oppositional, conduct and anxiety or depression problems. The scales follow DSM-IV symptom lists, were validated for ages 6 to 12, and must not be used alone to diagnose [NICHQ-Vanderbilt].
3. **Interview.** Use a structured or semi-structured interview, such as the Diagnostic Interview for ADHD in Adults (DIVA), the Conners Adult ADHD Diagnostic Interview for DSM-IV (CAADID) or the adult ADHD module of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5), to check symptoms, onset, settings and impairment [Ramsay-2017].
4. **Establish childhood onset.** Several symptoms before age 12 [APA-DSM5TR-2022]. Adults often recall onset poorly [Epstein-Loren-2013], so ask a parent or older sibling and look for school reports, teacher comments and old assessments, the "paper trail" [Ramsay-2017].
5. **Get current collateral.** Children: information mainly from parents or guardians, teachers and other school staff [Wolraich-etal-2019]. Adults: a partner, roommate or coworker. Informants see different settings, so disagreement does not invalidate a report [Ramsay-2017].
6. **Rule out look-alikes and screen comorbidity.** AAP lists anxiety, depression, oppositional defiant and conduct disorders, substance use, learning, language and autism spectrum disorders, tics and sleep apnea [Wolraich-etal-2019]. Ask:
   - "When did this start? Was it there before the low mood, the worry, or the drinking?"
   - "Is it there on good weeks, on vacation, in every setting?"
   - "Do you snore or wake unrefreshed?"
   - "Are there days or weeks when you need little sleep and feel unusually driven?"
7. **Measure impairment and set a baseline.** Use a symptom scale plus an impairment or executive function measure, such as the Weiss Functional Impairment Rating Scale (WFIRS) or the Barkley Deficits in Executive Functioning Scale (BDEFS), and real-world markers (late fees, missed deadlines) [Ramsay-2017].

## What to record

- Each symptom endorsed, by list and by informant; presentation and severity.
- Age and evidence of onset, with source (informant, records).
- Settings affected and concrete impairment examples.
- Differential conclusions: what was ruled out and why; comorbid diagnoses.
- Baseline scores (screener, symptom scale, impairment measure), with dates.
- Substance use and, if medication is considered, the prescriber's plan.

## Guidance

- If an adult scores positive on the ASRS → say "This tells us a full assessment is worth doing; it is not a diagnosis," and complete steps 3 to 6. Because specificity falls to 74.0% in a specialty clinic, so many clinic attenders without ADHD also screen positive [Ustun-etal-2017].
- If no informant or record can confirm childhood symptoms → record the gap, weigh the client's account cautiously and avoid a firm diagnosis. Because onset before 12 is a required criterion [APA-DSM5TR-2022].
- If concentration problems began with a depressive or anxious episode → treat that episode and reassess attention afterwards. Because executive function can drop during a mood episode and recover as mood improves [Ramsay-2017].
- If the request centers on obtaining stimulants, or there is current substance misuse → complete the full assessment and share the history with the prescriber. Because the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) asks prescribers to assess misuse risk before prescribing [FDA-Stimulant-Warning-2023].
- If you plan to automate the interview or build it into a digital tool → check the license first. Because the licensed DIVA-5 must stay clinician-led, may not be converted into an artificial intelligence (AI) or chatbot interview, and may not be reworded or shortened without written approval [DIVA-Site].

## Common problems

- **Diagnosing from a checklist.** Scales do not establish onset, pervasiveness or exclusions. See `adhd.pitfall-diagnosis-from-self-report-alone`.
- **Missing ADHD in women and late-diagnosed adults.** See `adhd.underdiagnosis-in-women-and-adults`.
- **Single-setting picture.** Symptoms in one setting only suggest context, conflict or learning problems; gather the other setting.
- **Scope of practice.** In the United Kingdom (UK), the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) says only a specialist psychiatrist, pediatrician or other appropriately qualified clinician with ADHD training should diagnose [NICE-NG87]. In the United States (US), check your license and state rules.
