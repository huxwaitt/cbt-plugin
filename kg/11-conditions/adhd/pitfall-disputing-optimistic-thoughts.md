**Bottom line:** Adults with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) often avoid tasks through thoughts that sound positive ("I work best at the last minute," "It'll only take 30 minutes"), not only through negative ones [Knouse-Mitchell-2015]. These thoughts bring quick relief and so keep procrastination going. Look for them deliberately, and work on what they do, not on whether they are true.

## What it looks like

- Thought records before avoided tasks come back blank or say "I felt fine about it."
- The therapist challenges optimism ("What's the evidence you work better under pressure?") and the client defends it; the session becomes a debate.
- Goals are set far above the client's baseline, for example 30 minutes a day of a task currently done for zero minutes [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
- Skills are taught well but the client "decided to start tomorrow" again.

## Why it happens

- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) training emphasizes negative automatic thoughts. Optimistic thoughts do not look like a problem, and they feel good to both client and therapist.
- These thoughts relieve the discomfort a task triggers, so they are negatively reinforced each time [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
- Some are partly true (the client has met deadlines at the last minute), so testing accuracy goes nowhere [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
- In community samples they correlated with functional impairment, time-management problems and avoidant coping [Knouse-etal-2019], so missing them leaves a key maintaining process untouched.

## The fix

1. **Ask for them by name.** "Some people tell themselves something reassuring right before they put a task off. What do you say to yourself?"
2. **Map the function.** Use Trigger, Response, Avoidance Pattern (TRAP): the task, the feeling, the thought, the delay, and the relief [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
3. **Name the cost, not the error.** "In the moment, 'later' felt better. What did it cost by Thursday?" This avoids arguing over how much optimism is too much [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
4. **Label red flag thoughts.** Help the client spot their recurring phrases in real time with reminders, a written card or a short mantra [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
5. **Plan the Alternative Coping (TRAC).** Write an if-then plan: "If I hear myself say 'only 30 minutes,' then I start a 10-minute timer right now" [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
6. **Measure active coping.** Count episodes of starting on time, not the absence of optimistic thoughts [Knouse-Mitchell-2015].
7. Keep standard restructuring for self-critical and hopeless thoughts, which also feed avoidance in the adult ADHD model [Knouse-Safren-2010].

Avoid: "That's just an excuse." It shames the client and invites defense.

## Sources note

The functional approach is described with case material and practical steps rather than tested in a dedicated trial [Knouse-Mitchell-2015]. The link between these thoughts and impairment comes from questionnaire studies in community samples [Knouse-etal-2019].
