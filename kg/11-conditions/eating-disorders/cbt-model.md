**Bottom line:** The leading cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) model of eating disorders is Fairburn's transdiagnostic theory: overevaluation of shape, weight and their control is the core that keeps anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and other eating disorders going [Fairburn-etal-2003]. Strict dieting, binge eating, purging, body checking and the effects of low weight all feed back into that core. In some clients four additional processes block change: clinical perfectionism, core low self-esteem, mood intolerance and interpersonal difficulties [Fairburn-etal-2003].

## Components

- **Core psychopathology:** judging self-worth largely or only by shape, weight and control over eating [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Strict dieting:** multiple extreme, specific dietary rules (when, how much, what to eat), including delayed eating and avoided foods [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Binge eating:** the one feature not a direct expression of the core; mostly a reaction to breaking dietary rules [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Compensatory behavior:** self-induced vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, driven exercise.
- **Body checking and avoidance, and "feeling fat":** repeated weighing, mirror scrutiny, comparison, or refusing to look; feeling fat mislabels emotions and body sensations [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Events and mood:** life difficulties and mood change make restraint hard and eating a way to cope [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Low weight effects:** in underweight clients, delayed stomach emptying causes fullness after small meals, preoccupation with food rises, and social withdrawal removes other sources of self-worth [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Four additional maintaining processes** (the "broad" model): clinical perfectionism, core low self-esteem, mood intolerance and interpersonal difficulties [Fairburn-etal-2003].

## How it keeps the problem going

1. **Diet and binge cycle.** Rigid rules are broken almost inevitably; the client reads the slip as proof of poor control, abandons restraint, and binges. The binge heightens shape concern and leads to more dieting [Murphy-etal-2010].
2. **Binge and purge cycle.** Belief that vomiting or laxatives prevent weight gain removes a deterrent to bingeing [Murphy-etal-2010].
3. **Mood and binge cycle.** Binge eating briefly relieves negative mood and distracts from problems, so it becomes a coping method [Murphy-etal-2010].
4. **Checking cycle.** Scrutinizing body parts magnifies apparent flaws, and comparing only with thin people biases conclusions, which keeps dissatisfaction high [Murphy-etal-2010]. Frequent self-weighing makes normal daily fluctuations look like gain, while total avoidance of weighing leaves fears untested [Waller-Mountford-2015].
5. **Starvation cycle.** Low weight produces fullness, preoccupation and withdrawal, which keep restriction going and shrink the rest of life [Murphy-etal-2010].
6. **Additional processes.** Perfectionism turns dieting into a performance standard; low self-esteem makes failure feel like confirmation; mood intolerance prompts bingeing, vomiting or self-harm to shut off emotion; interpersonal problems trigger episodes [Fairburn-etal-2003].

Each client has only the loops that apply. A person with binge-eating disorder may show dieting, binge and mood loops only; someone with binge-purge anorexia may show nearly all [Murphy-etal-2010].

## Drawing it with the client

- **When:** usually in the first session, as a working hypothesis to revise [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Start with what the client wants to change**, often binge eating, and work backward to dieting and forward to consequences [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Use their words** ("food rules", "the voice", "feeling huge") and draw only the boxes that fit. Leave the four additional processes off unless they clearly maintain the problem.
- **Ask, do not tell:** "What happens after a binge? What do you decide about tomorrow?"
- **Show the loops as self-perpetuating and changeable.** The aim is that the client sees the behavior as understandable and maintained by processes treatment can break [Murphy-etal-2010].
- **Extend later:** a pie chart of what the client's self-worth rests on, then an extended formulation linking overevaluation to dieting, checking, feeling fat and a narrowed life [Murphy-etal-2010].

Example phrasing: "So the plan to eat almost nothing until evening sets up the hunger, and when the rule breaks, it feels like it's all gone wrong anyway. Does that fit?"

Avoid: "Your bingeing is caused by low self-esteem from childhood." Belief-level and historical explanations come late, if at all.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Maintaining process | Target and method |
|---|---|
| Dieting and delayed eating | Regular eating: 3 meals and 2 or 3 snacks, rarely over 4 hours apart [Murphy-etal-2010] |
| Weight fears and checking | Collaborative weekly in-session weighing and graphing [Murphy-etal-2010] |
| Purging belief | Education that laxatives and diuretics do not reduce calorie absorption [NICE-NG69] |
| Food avoidance and rules | Behavioral experiments reintroducing avoided foods [Murphy-etal-2010] |
| Overevaluation | Develop other domains of self-worth; reduce body checking and avoidance; relabel feeling fat [Murphy-etal-2010] |
| Event and mood triggers | Proactive problem solving and other ways to manage mood [Murphy-etal-2010] |
| Low weight | Weight regain as the client's own decision [Murphy-etal-2010] |
| Perfectionism, low self-esteem, interpersonal problems | Broad modules only when pronounced and maintaining [Murphy-etal-2010] |

Guidelines echo this: individual eating-disorder-focused CBT (CBT-ED) builds a personal plan from the processes that maintain the eating problem [NICE-NG69]. For binge-eating disorder, advise against dieting during treatment because it is likely to trigger binges [NICE-NG69]. In one trial, clients with marked additional processes did better with the broad form; others did better with the focused form [Fairburn-etal-2009].
