**Bottom line:** In the cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) model, strict dieting is a main driver of binge eating, so adding a diet to treatment feeds the problem it is meant to fix [Murphy-etal-2010]. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises telling clients that binge-eating treatment has a limited effect on weight, that weight loss is not a therapy target, and that dieting during treatment is likely to trigger binges [NICE-NG69]. Make stopping binge eating the goal, and route weight-loss care through obesity services afterward.

## What it looks like

- A calorie limit or "healthy eating plan" is set alongside regular eating.
- Weekly weigh-ins are read as success or failure.
- The client skips breakfast "to make up for" last night's binge, and the therapist accepts it.
- A referral letter lists "weight loss" as the treatment aim.
- Binges fall early, then return when the client tightens food rules.

## Why it happens

- Many clients with binge-eating disorder carry excess weight and want to lose it; physicians may push for it.
- Clinicians feel that ignoring weight is unhelpful or neglectful.
- The dieting and binge cycle is not visible until the client sees it in their own records.

## The fix

- If the client wants to lose weight → acknowledge the wish, explain the dieting and binge cycle with their own records, and agree to postpone weight loss. Because rule breaking after strict dieting triggers binges [Murphy-etal-2010], and NICE advises against dieting during treatment [NICE-NG69].
- If the client asks whether treatment will change their weight → say it has a limited effect on weight, though stopping binge eating may help in the long term. Because NICE asks clinicians to explain this [NICE-NG69].
- If a referrer wants weight loss → explain that binge eating is the target and that weight management is handled separately. Because NICE separates the two and refers to obesity guidance [NICE-NG69].
- If the client adds hidden rules (no carbs after 6 p.m.) → treat them as dietary rules to test. Because breaking rules as experiments is a core CBT procedure [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If a behavioral weight loss program is proposed instead of CBT → note that CBT reduced binge eating more. Because CBT outperformed behavioral weight loss on binge outcomes [Linardon-etal-2017] [Hilbert-etal-2019].

Example phrasing: "I hear how much you want your weight to change. The trouble is that the strict rules are what set up the binges. Can we get the binges under control first, then look at weight with your doctor?"

Avoid: "Let's aim for 1,200 calories a day so you can lose a bit while we work on the bingeing."

## Sources note

The dieting and binge link comes from the cognitive behavioral account [Murphy-etal-2010] and guideline advice [NICE-NG69]. Meta-analyses found CBT superior to behavioral weight loss on binge outcomes [Linardon-etal-2017] [Hilbert-etal-2019], though Hilbert and colleagues rated evidence quality very low. NICE gives the same advice for bulimia nervosa: psychological treatment has a limited effect on weight [NICE-NG69].
