**Bottom line:** Enhanced cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-E) has four stages: an intensive start that establishes formulation, self-monitoring, weekly weighing and regular eating; a brief review; the main body that tackles shape and weight concern, dietary rules and mood-related eating; and an ending focused on relapse prevention [Murphy-etal-2010]. The focused form is the default; the broad form adds modules for perfectionism, low self-esteem or interpersonal problems only when they clearly maintain the disorder [Murphy-etal-2010].

## When to use

With adult outpatients with any eating disorder [Murphy-etal-2010]; the evidence also covers older adolescents [Atwood-Friedman-2020], and a 40-session adolescent version has cohort data [DalleGrave-etal-2013]. Delay the start if physical features of concern, severe depression, significant substance misuse, a major crisis or competing commitments would stop the client using treatment; address them first [Murphy-etal-2010].

## How it works

Each stage targets a link in the client's formulation. Eating changes come before belief work, and feared consequences are tested by planned experiments, not reassurance [Murphy-etal-2010].

## Steps

**Stage one (about 8 sessions, twice weekly over 4 weeks)** [Murphy-etal-2010]
1. Draw the personal formulation in session 1, starting with what the client wants to change.
2. Start real-time self-monitoring: what was eaten, when, where, binges, vomiting or laxatives, and thoughts and feelings, written at the time, not later.
3. Begin collaborative weekly weighing: weigh together in session, plot the number on a graph, interpret trends over several readings, and ask the client not to weigh at other times.
4. Give personalized education: body weight regulation, fluctuations, the ineffectiveness of vomiting and laxatives, the effects of dieting. Assign authoritative reading.
5. Establish regular eating by about session 3: 3 planned meals plus 2 or 3 planned snacks, rarely more than 4 hours apart, with nothing in between. Help the client ride out binge urges with incompatible activities and "urge surfing".

**Stage two (2 sessions, a week apart)** [Murphy-etal-2010]
6. Review progress jointly, using the monitoring records and weight graph. Praise change; identify barriers; revise the formulation; decide whether to use broad modules; plan stage three.

**Stage three (about 8 weekly sessions)** [Murphy-etal-2010]
7. **Overevaluation of shape and weight:** draw a pie chart of what self-worth rests on, name its problems, and build an extended formulation. Help the client develop other domains of life.
8. **Body checking and avoidance:** monitor checking, explain how scrutiny and selective comparison magnify flaws, reduce checking, and gradually expose the client to looking at their body.
9. **Feeling fat:** identify triggers (boredom, low mood, fullness, sweating) and ask "What else am I feeling?" when the feeling arises.
10. **Dietary rules:** list rules and the beliefs behind them, then break them as behavioral experiments. Reintroduce avoided foods systematically.
11. **Event- and mood-related eating:** teach proactive problem solving and functional ways to manage mood.
12. **Origins:** late in stage three, review how the problem began and why it may no longer serve the client.
13. **Broad modules** if chosen: clinical perfectionism, core low self-esteem, or interpersonal problems (handled with interpersonal psychotherapy techniques in the same sessions).

**Stage four (3 sessions, about 2 weeks apart)** [Murphy-etal-2010]
14. Stop self-monitoring; move to weekly weighing at home.
15. Write a personal maintenance plan to the review session about 20 weeks later.
16. Frame the problem as an "Achilles heel": catch setbacks early, treat a lapse as a lapse, and restart what worked.

**Underweight clients (body mass index 17.5 or below):** 40 sessions over 40 weeks. Phase one builds motivation; phase two adds weight regain while tackling eating disorder thinking; phase three prevents and corrects setbacks [Fairburn-etal-2013]. The aim is that the client decides to regain weight [APA-ED-Guideline-2023].

## Guidance

- If monitoring records come back blank or written at the end of the day → review why, simplify the form, and practice recording a meal in session. Because in-the-moment recording is what lets the client change behavior as it happens [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If the client asks to skip weighing → explain the purpose, weigh together, and read trends over several weeks, not single numbers. Because collaborative weighing corrects misreading of the scale and reduces fear [Waller-Mountford-2015].
- If binges continue after regular eating is in place → look for gaps over 4 hours, delayed eating and avoided foods. Because food avoidance is a major contributor to binge eating [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If the stage two review shows little change → revise the formulation and remove barriers before moving on. Because the review exists to catch obstacles early [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If perfectionism or interpersonal problems are mild → stay with the focused form. Because the broad form helped only clients with marked additional problems [Fairburn-etal-2009].
- If a client with binge-eating disorder plans a diet → advise against it during treatment. Because dieting is likely to trigger binges [NICE-NG69].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's look at four weeks on the graph, not today's number."
- "What would you predict happens if you eat the dessert at lunch? How could we test that this week?"

## Common problems

- Reassurance instead of experiments: answer "Will I gain weight?" with graph data and a planned test.
- Starting stage three before regular eating is stable.
- Monitoring kept going indefinitely; it stops in stage four [Murphy-etal-2010].

## Evidence

In two trials of the 20-session version, remission at end of treatment was 67% and 66%, analyzed the same way [Fairburn-etal-2015]. Trial therapists had about six months of training, weekly supervision and recorded sessions [Fairburn-etal-2015]. Evidence strength: supported by randomized trials for clients with body mass index above 17.5; uncontrolled cohort data for the 40-session version [Fairburn-etal-2013].
