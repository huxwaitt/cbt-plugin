**Bottom line:** Weight status is a poor guide to how ill someone with an eating disorder is. People with atypical anorexia nervosa can have medical complications as severe as those with low weight [Vo-Golden-2022], and people with bulimia nervosa who are not underweight can present in life-threatening emergencies [RCPsych-CR233]. Judge risk from the rate and amount of weight loss, behaviors and physical findings, not body mass index (BMI) alone [NICE-NG69].

## What it looks like

- "Your BMI is fine, so you don't meet criteria for our service."
- A client who lost a fifth of their body weight in six months is praised for "getting healthy."
- No vital signs or blood tests are ordered because the client "looks well."
- Vomiting several times a day is noted but electrolytes are never checked.
- The client concludes they are "not sick enough" and disengages.

## Why it happens

- Clinicians and services equate eating disorders with visible thinness. The American Psychiatric Association warns that atypical anorexia nervosa is overlooked when BMI is normal or higher [APA-ED-Guideline-2023].
- Weight loss in larger bodies is socially rewarded, so it is not read as a symptom.
- Admission and access rules historically used BMI cutoffs.

## The fix

- If a client at normal or high weight reports rapid weight loss → ask how much and how fast and request a medical review. Because the Royal College of Psychiatrists' Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders (MEED) guidance rates rapid weight loss at any weight as red [RCPsych-CR233], and the rate and amount of loss, not presenting weight, predict complications such as low phosphate [Vo-Golden-2022].
- If the client purges → ask frequency and arrange electrolyte checks. Because people with bulimia nervosa who are not underweight can have life-threatening electrolyte disturbance [RCPsych-CR233].
- If a service uses BMI to decide eligibility → raise the guideline. Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises against single measures such as BMI or duration of illness when deciding whether to offer treatment [NICE-NG69].
- If an admission decision hinges on a BMI number → argue from the whole clinical picture. Because NICE advises against absolute weight or BMI thresholds for admission [NICE-NG69].
- If the client says "I'm not thin enough to have a problem" → validate the confusion and name the risk. Because feeling "not sick enough" can block help-seeking (expert consensus).
- If you hear yourself complimenting weight loss → stop and ask how it was achieved. Because the methods, not the number, carry the medical risk [Vo-Golden-2022].

Example phrasing: "Eating disorders happen at every weight. What matters for your health is what you're doing to your body and how fast things have changed, so I'd like you checked properly."

Avoid: "You don't look like someone with an eating disorder."

## Sources note

Similar rates of bradycardia have been found in atypical and typical anorexia nervosa [Vo-Golden-2022], though the evidence base for atypical anorexia is small. Mortality is raised across eating disorders, not only in anorexia nervosa [Arcelus-etal-2011]. The fix rests on guideline recommendations and expert consensus.
