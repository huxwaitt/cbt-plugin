**Bottom line:** Ask every client about eating, weight and shape, because eating disorders are often missed and clients under-report [APA-ED-Guideline-2023]. When a screen is positive, take a full eating and weight history, weigh the client, count behaviors per week, and complete the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q) and Clinical Impairment Assessment (CIA). Arrange a medical check in parallel, and never let a screening score alone decide whether someone has a disorder [NICE-NG69].

## When to use

At every intake, and whenever the client, family, school or a physician reports weight change, dieting, binge eating, vomiting, laxative use, driven exercise, food avoidance, or worry about shape. The American Psychiatric Association (APA) recommends screening in every initial psychiatric evaluation (strength 1C) [APA-ED-Guideline-2023].

## Steps

1. **Screen.** Ask, for example "Have you or others worried that your preoccupation with weight, body shape, or food is excessive?" [APA-ED-Guideline-2023]. The five-item SCOFF questionnaire scores one point per "yes"; 2 or more flags a likely case (sensitivity 100%, specificity 87.5% in the original clinic study) [Morgan-etal-1999]. It detects binge-eating disorder, atypical anorexia and cases in diverse groups less well, so also ask about eating far more than most people would, with loss of control [APA-ED-Guideline-2023]. Use any screen to prompt assessment, not to diagnose [NICE-NG69].
2. **Take the eating and weight history.** Highest and lowest weight, recent change and its speed; restriction, food avoidance and dietary variety; binge eating; vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, diet pills, insulin omission, driven exercise; time spent preoccupied; prior treatment and response; impairment; family history [APA-ED-Guideline-2023].
3. **Quantify.** Weigh the client in light clothing without shoes and count each behavior over the past 28 days: objective binges (large amount, loss of control), subjective binges (loss of control, ordinary amount), vomiting, laxative and diuretic use, fasting days, exercise hours [APA-ED-Guideline-2023]. APA calls sharing the number unclear and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) says to consider it, but cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) protocols weigh openly [APA-ED-Guideline-2023] [NICE-NG69] [Waller-Mountford-2015].
4. **Measure.** EDE-Q global score (past 28 days, scored 0 to 6): the mean in Australian women aged 18 to 42 was 1.52 (standard deviation 1.25) [Mond-etal-2006]. A global score of 2.3 plus any objective binges or exercise for weight control best identified community cases, but from only 13 cases [Mond-etal-2004]. Trials of enhanced CBT (CBT-E) define remission as an Eating Disorder Examination (EDE) interview global score below 1.74 [Fairburn-etal-2015]; EDE-Q scores run higher, so use 1.74 only as a rough EDE-Q benchmark [Mond-etal-2004]. Add the 16-item CIA (range 0 to 48); 16 or more best predicted a clinical case (sensitivity 76%, specificity 86%) [Bohn-etal-2008]. The free Eating Disorder Assessment for DSM-5 (EDA-5) interview supports diagnosis [APA-ED-Guideline-2023]. The EDE-Q is copyrighted and free for noncommercial research use; check terms for clinical or commercial use (see `08-measurement.instrument-licensing`) [CREDO-Site].
5. **Check medical safety.** Ask about dizziness, fainting, palpitations, chest pain, cold intolerance, weakness, vomiting blood and missed periods. Ensure a medical clinician checks vital signs, including orthostatic pulse and blood pressure, blood count, metabolic panel and, for restriction, severe purging or QT-prolonging drugs, an electrocardiogram (ECG) [APA-ED-Guideline-2023]. Follow `eating-disorders.medical-risk`.
6. **Screen comorbidity and risk.** Depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), self-harm, suicidal thinking and alcohol or drug use [NICE-NG69].
7. **Gather collateral.** Family or partners often see what the client does not report; with young people, involve parents and, where appropriate, school staff [APA-ED-Guideline-2023]. Correct the family's misconceptions [NICE-NG69].
8. **Decide and refer.** In the UK, refer a suspected eating disorder at once to an age-appropriate eating disorder service [NICE-NG69].

**Differential questions:** "What would happen if you ate more?" (fear of fatness suggests anorexia nervosa; fear of choking or disgust suggests avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder). "Does the food rule protect you from something other than weight?" (OCD). "Is the worry about weight or one feature?" (body dysmorphic disorder). "Did appetite loss come with low mood?" (depression).

## What to record

- Height, weight, body mass index (BMI, or BMI percentile for youth), weight history and rate of loss.
- Weekly counts of each behavior, meal pattern, fluid intake and exercise.
- Screening, EDE-Q and CIA scores with dates; medical symptoms, who monitors physical health, and the last check.
- Comorbidity, suicide and self-harm risk, substance use, collateral sources, consent to share, and the referral decision.

## Guidance

- If the screen is negative but family report skipped meals or bathroom visits after eating → assess fully. Because clients under-report [APA-ED-Guideline-2023].
- If the client refuses to be weighed → explain that weight is the key safety and progress measure, agree how it will be shared, and keep returning to it; if refusal continues, make sure the medical clinician obtains weight. Because avoidance of weight maintains weight concern [Waller-Mountford-2015].
- If the client says binges are "huge" → ask exactly what and how much was eaten. Because objective and subjective binges are recorded separately and change the diagnosis.
- If weight is normal but loss is fast → treat the loss as the risk signal (see `eating-disorders.pitfall-normal-weight-assumed-safe`). Because NICE warns against deciding from BMI alone [NICE-NG69].
- If self-induced vomiting or laxative use is reported → ask for an electrolyte check. Because purging causes electrolyte imbalance [NICE-NG69].

Example phrasing: "Many people feel embarrassed talking about eating. I ask everyone these questions."

Avoid: "You don't look like you have an eating disorder."

## Common problems

- **Under-reporting** from shame or ambivalence: return gently to counts and collateral.
- **Assessment without weighing** leaves no baseline and no safety data.
- **Diagnosing from the screen alone** [NICE-NG69].
