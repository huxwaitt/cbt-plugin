**Bottom line:** Therapist drift is failing to deliver the evidence-based treatment despite having the tools, and it is an important reason evidence-based cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) works less well in routine practice than it should [Waller-Turner-2016]. In eating disorders it usually means dropping the behavioral core: regular eating, self-monitoring, weighing and food experiments. Early change predicts outcome, so drift in the first weeks is especially costly [Murphy-etal-2010].

## What it looks like

- Monitoring records are no longer reviewed; homework is not set.
- Weighing has stopped "because it upset her."
- Sessions follow whatever the client brings, week after week.
- "We need to build motivation first" for months, with no eating change attempted.
- Long exploration of childhood or self-esteem while binges and vomiting continue.
- No measure has been repeated since assessment.

## Why it happens

Therapists' beliefs, emotions and behaviors drive drift: anxiety about the client's distress, fear of harming the alliance, belief that "this client is too complex for the protocol," and preference for familiar techniques [Waller-Turner-2016]. Eating disorder work is uncomfortable for both people, and avoiding the hard tasks relieves the therapist's own anxiety, which maintains the drift [Waller-Turner-2016].

## The fix

- If you notice you are not setting eating tasks → return to the formulation and the stage one procedures next session. Because early behavior change predicts outcome [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If you think the client is too complex for the protocol → check with supervision before abandoning it. Because believing a client is unsuitable is a common driver of drift [Waller-Turner-2016].
- If progress has stalled → hold a structured review like stage two of enhanced CBT (CBT-E), checking records and the weight graph and naming barriers. Because the review exists to catch obstacles and revise the plan [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If you have no recent outcome data → repeat the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q). Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks clinicians to use standardized outcome measures [NICE-NG69].
- If you are not sure you are on protocol → record sessions and bring them to supervision. Because NICE asks clinicians to monitor competence with recordings and supervision [NICE-NG69], as trial therapists did [Fairburn-etal-2015].
- If you feel uneasy about a task such as weighing → treat your own avoidance as a target. Because a CBT approach to the therapist's cognitions and emotions is proposed to halt drift [Waller-Turner-2016].

Example phrasing (to the client): "I've noticed we've moved away from the eating plan in the last few sessions. That's on me as much as you. Can we look at the records together and pick up where it stalled?"

Avoid: "Let's leave the food side for now and just talk about how your week went."

## Sources note

The drift concept rests on a narrative review [Waller-Turner-2016]. Support for keeping to the protocol comes from trial delivery with manuals, training and supervision [Fairburn-etal-2015] and from findings that early change predicts response, including in 10-session CBT [Waller-etal-2018]. NICE asks that treatment content, structure and length follow eating disorder manuals [NICE-NG69].
