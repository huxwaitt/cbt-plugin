**Bottom line:** Collaborative weekly weighing in session is a core cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) procedure for eating disorders, not an optional extra [Murphy-etal-2010]. Therapists vary widely in whether they weigh, partly from their own reluctance [Waller-Mountford-2015]. Skipping weighing, or weighing without telling the client the number, leaves weight fears untested and safety data missing.

## What it looks like

- Weight is "left to the primary care physician" or the dietitian, and the therapist never sees the trend.
- The client is weighed facing away and never told the number ("blind weighing").
- The client weighs daily at home, or never, and the therapist does not ask.
- Sessions discuss "feeling huge" without any data on actual weight change.
- Weight loss is discovered weeks late at a medical review.

## Why it happens

- Therapists fear distressing the client or harming the alliance, or feel weighing is a medical task [Waller-Mountford-2015].
- Clients ask not to know, and agreeing feels kind.
- Protocols have changed over time, so practice is inconsistent [Waller-Mountford-2015].
- The guidelines are not uniform. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) says to consider sharing results [NICE-NG69], and the American Psychiatric Association calls whether to share "unclear" and dependent on the approach [APA-ED-Guideline-2023].

## The fix

- If you are starting CBT for an eating disorder → weigh the client in session once a week, together, and plot it on a graph. Because weekly weighing addresses both excessive checking and avoidance of weight [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If the client asks not to see the number → explain the reasons, agree how the number will be shared, and weigh openly. Because the client needs accurate data to test weight fears [Waller-Mountford-2015].
- If the client weighs often at home → ask them to stop between sessions. Because clients tend to misread the numbers on the scale, and the protocol asks them not to weigh at other times [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If a single reading shows a rise → interpret the trend over several weeks. Because single readings are misread [Murphy-etal-2010].
- If the client has binge-eating disorder → still weigh weekly and share the record. Because NICE includes weekly weight monitoring with the record shared [NICE-NG69].
- If treatment is ending → move to weekly weighing at home. Because this is part of stage four [Murphy-etal-2010].

Example phrasing: "We'll weigh together at the start of each session and put it on this graph. The single number matters less than the pattern over four weeks, and we'll read it together."

Avoid: "Don't worry about the number, just step on and I'll keep track."

## Sources note

The case for open weighing rests on the enhanced CBT (CBT-E) protocol [Murphy-etal-2010] and a narrative review [Waller-Mountford-2015], not trials comparing open with blind weighing. Body checking and avoidance are maintaining processes in the transdiagnostic model [Fairburn-etal-2003]. Evidence strength: expert consensus within protocols supported by randomized trials.
