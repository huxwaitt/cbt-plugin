**Bottom line:** Antidepressants usually sit comfortably alongside cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), but as-needed benzodiazepines and the belief that "the pill did it" can undermine exposure and raise relapse risk after medication stops. CBT, in turn, protects against relapse when medication ends. Map each medicine's role in the case formulation, keep doses stable during exposure blocks where possible, help clients credit their own efforts, and plan any stopping with the prescriber.

## The principle

Medicines and CBT interact in four ways:

1. **Add.** Both help, as with CBT plus an antidepressant, a first option for more severe depression in the 2022 UK depression guideline [Kendrick-etal-2022].
2. **Do not interfere.** In obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), exposure and ritual prevention plus clomipramine did not differ from exposure and ritual prevention alone, and both beat clomipramine alone [Foa-etal-2005].
3. **Interfere.** Benzodiazepines taken as needed can work as a safety behavior and block new learning. In panic disorder with agoraphobia, alprazolam combined with exposure weakened improvement after treatment ended [Marks-etal-1993], and as-needed users gained least from CBT [Westra-etal-2002]. Canadian guidelines note that a meta-analysis of three trials found no added benefit from combining benzodiazepines with psychotherapy, and that the combination may be inferior to behavior therapy alone at follow-up [Katzman-etal-2014].
4. **Shape meaning.** Clients who credited improvement to alprazolam rather than themselves had more withdrawal symptoms and lost more gains [Basoglu-etal-1994].

Side effects also matter: sleepiness on mirtazapine (54% versus 18% on placebo) [FDA-Mirtazapine-Label] or next-day impairment after a z-drug [FDA-Zolpidem-Label] can reduce concentration, homework and activity levels.

## Guidance

- If a client takes an as-needed benzodiazepine before feared situations → include it in the safety behavior list and plan, with the prescriber, how exposure tasks will be done without it. Because as-needed use predicted the fewest CBT gains [Westra-etal-2002].
- If a client is on a stable antidepressant at the start of CBT → keep it stable during the active phase unless the prescriber has a reason to change. Because changing two things at once makes it impossible to know what helped (expert consensus), and exposure works alongside serotonergic medication [Foa-etal-2005].
- If a client wants to stop medication mid-therapy → encourage them to discuss timing with the prescriber, ideally after CBT skills are established and with relapse prevention in place. Because relapse after stopping antidepressants was 56% versus 39% on continuation within a year [Lewis-etal-2021].
- If a client says "it's only the tablets working" → review session by session what they did differently, and run a behavioral experiment on a skill. Because drug attribution predicted relapse [Basoglu-etal-1994].
- If a client is sedated in sessions or cannot complete homework → note the timing of doses and tell the prescriber. Because sleepiness and next-day impairment are labeled effects the prescriber may be able to adjust [FDA-Mirtazapine-Label; FDA-Zolpidem-Label].
- If a client asks about "memory pills" that boost exposure, such as D-cycloserine → explain that the overall effect was small and they are not routine care. Because an individual participant data meta-analysis of 21 trials found a small effect (d = -0.25) at post-treatment and only a borderline effect at follow-up [Mataix-Cols-etal-2017].
- If CBT is ending while medication continues → tell the prescriber and share the relapse prevention plan. Because CBT's protective effect persists after treatment ends [Hollon-etal-2005].
- If a client wants to come off antidepressants and prevent relapse → mention that mindfulness-based cognitive therapy with tapering support performed similarly to maintenance antidepressants. Because a large randomized trial found no significant difference (hazard ratio 0.89) [Kuyken-etal-2015].
- If a client is tapering a benzodiazepine → offer CBT skills alongside the taper. Because in a small randomized trial CBT plus taper led 76% to stop versus 25% with taper alone [Otto-etal-1993].

## Example phrasings

- "Let's add the tablet in your bag to our map of what you do to feel safe. Then we can ask your doctor how to plan the next exposure steps."
- "You've cut your checking from hours to minutes. Which part of that came from what you practiced?"
- "Stopping your medication is your and Dr. Green's decision. What I can do is make sure your relapse plan is ready first."
- Avoid: "You don't need those pills anymore now that you're doing therapy."

## Evidence

The interference evidence comes mainly from randomized trials in panic disorder with alprazolam [Marks-etal-1993; Basoglu-etal-1994] and a naturalistic study of benzodiazepine use during CBT [Westra-etal-2002]. Evidence that serotonergic medicines do not impair exposure comes from OCD trials [Foa-etal-2005]. The enduring effect of CBT is supported by randomized trials [Hollon-etal-2005; Kuyken-etal-2015]. Advice on timing and stable dosing is expert consensus.
