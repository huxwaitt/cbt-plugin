**Bottom line:** A non-prescribing therapist does not advise starting, changing or stopping medication. The fix is a fixed response: acknowledge the question, explain whose decision it is, help the client take it to the prescriber, and offer to share progress data. Abrupt changes can cause seizures, withdrawal or relapse [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020; Lewis-etal-2021].

## What it looks like

- "You're doing so well, you probably don't need the tablets anymore."
- "Try taking half and see how you feel."
- Agreeing that the client can skip doses "on good days."
- Suggesting a specific drug the client should ask for, or commenting that a dose is "too high."
- Telling a client to stop a medicine because of a side effect, instead of asking them to contact the prescriber.

## Why it happens

- Wanting to support the client's autonomy or their wish to be "medication free."
- A belief that cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) alone should be enough, or skepticism about medication.
- The prescriber is hard to reach, so the therapist fills the gap.
- Overconfidence from general knowledge of drugs.

## The fix

- If a client asks "Should I stop?" → say whose decision it is and offer help. "That's a decision for you and your prescriber. I can help you prepare what to ask, and send your progress scores if that helps." Because the patient guide for a common antidepressant says not to stop without talking to a prescriber and to taper under supervision [FDA-Sertraline-Label].
- If a client has already stopped a benzodiazepine suddenly → contact the prescriber the same day with consent. Because abrupt stopping can cause seizures and long withdrawal [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020], and tapering should be individualized and may take months to years [Brunner-etal-2025].
- If a family plans to pause an attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) medicine such as guanfacine → advise talking to the prescriber first. Because stopping suddenly can cause rebound high blood pressure [FDA-Guanfacine-Label].
- If the client wants to come off an antidepressant → help them plan with the prescriber and prepare a relapse plan. Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) advises a staged, shared taper [Kendrick-etal-2022] and relapse within a year was 56% after discontinuation versus 39% with maintenance [Lewis-etal-2021].
- If you notice yourself forming views about drugs or doses → take them to supervision, not the client. Because prescribing sits outside your license (scope of practice).
- If you record the conversation → note the client's question and that you advised discussing it with the prescriber. Because a clear record shows you stayed within scope (expert consensus).

Avoid: "Your dose seems high, maybe ask to halve it." Instead: "It sounds like the side effects are bothering you. That's really worth telling Dr. Kim about. Shall we write down what you've noticed?"

## Sources note

Withdrawal and seizure risks come from United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) safety communications and labels [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020; FDA-Sertraline-Label; FDA-Guanfacine-Label]; tapering principles from the 2025 joint benzodiazepine guideline [Brunner-etal-2025] and the NICE 2022 depression guideline [Kendrick-etal-2022]; relapse figures from a randomized trial [Lewis-etal-2021]. Prescribing authority varies by license and jurisdiction; a few prescribing psychologists and nurse practitioners will have different scope.
