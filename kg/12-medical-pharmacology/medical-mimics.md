**Bottom line:** Thyroid disease, anemia, sleep apnea, neurological illness, heart rhythm problems, alcohol or drug use and withdrawal, and many prescribed medicines can produce symptoms that look like depression, anxiety or panic. A cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) therapist does not diagnose these, but should know them, ask about them, and refer when clues appear. A physical cause rarely ends the psychological work; often both need treatment.

## What it is

A medical mimic is a physical condition, substance or medicine whose effects resemble or drive a psychological problem. It may cause the symptoms (an overactive thyroid producing anxiety), aggravate a disorder (caffeine provoking panic), or coexist with it (depression after a stroke).

## Why it matters in CBT

- If a physical process drives the symptoms, exposure or restructuring cannot fix it, and the client may conclude they "failed therapy."
- Misattribution can last years. Among 107 people with paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT, a fast heart rhythm), 67% met criteria for panic disorder; where the rhythm was missed, nonpsychiatric physicians blamed panic, anxiety or stress in 54%, and the median delay to diagnosis was 3.3 years [Lessmeier-etal-1997].

## Key details

| Mimic | What it can look like | Clues to ask about |
|---|---|---|
| Overactive thyroid | Anxiety, irritability, emotional lability, poor concentration; anxiety disorders in about 60% of hyperthyroid patients [Hage-Azar-2012] | Weight loss, tremor, palpitations, heat intolerance |
| Underactive thyroid | Depression, apathy, slowed thinking; overt hypothyroidism in 1% to 4% and subclinical in 4% to 40% of people with mood disorders [Hage-Azar-2012] | Weight gain, cold intolerance, fatigue, a known thyroid history |
| Iron deficiency anemia | Fatigue, low mood, poor sleep; adjusted hazard ratio 1.52 for any psychiatric disorder in a national cohort [Lee-etal-2020] | Heavy periods, pregnancy, restricted diet, breathlessness |
| Obstructive sleep apnea | Daytime fatigue, low mood, poor concentration; continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) improved depressive symptoms across 19 trials (standardized mean difference 0.31) [Povitz-etal-2014] | Loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, daytime sleepiness |
| Parkinson's disease and stroke | Major depressive disorder in 17%, and clinically significant depressive symptoms in 35%, of people with Parkinson's [Reijnders-etal-2008]; depression in 31% after stroke [Hackett-Pickles-2014] | Tremor, stiffness, falls, new weakness, speech or vision change |
| Heart rhythm problems | Panic-like attacks [Lessmeier-etal-1997] | Attacks that start and stop abruptly, very fast pulse, fainting |
| Delirium | An acute change in attention, awareness and cognition [Wilson-etal-2020] | Sudden confusion or fluctuating alertness, especially in older or medically ill clients |
| Caffeine | 400 to 750 mg triggered panic attacks in 51% of people with panic disorder across nine studies, none after placebo [Klevebrant-Frick-2022]; 150 mg did not raise anxiety in a 2025 trial [Hoppe-etal-2025] | Energy drinks, four or more cups of coffee |
| Alcohol withdrawal | Anxiety, poor sleep, vivid dreams and a fast heart rate starting 6 to 24 hours after stopping or cutting down; seizures from 8 to 48 hours, peaking near 24 hours [ASAM-Alcohol-Withdrawal-2020] | Morning shakes, drinking to settle nerves |
| Corticosteroids | Euphoria or hypomania on short courses, depression on long ones; severe reactions in nearly 6% and mild to moderate in about 28%, rising with dose [Warrington-Bostwick-2006] | Recent course of prednisone or similar |
| Montelukast (asthma and allergy) | Boxed warning for agitation, depression, sleep disturbance and suicidal thoughts [FDA-Montelukast-DSC-2020] | Mood change after starting it |
| Hormonal contraception | Associated with first antidepressant use (relative risk 1.23 for combined pills; higher in adolescents) [Skovlund-etal-2016] | Mood change in the months after starting |

Not every suspected medicine is guilty: across 15 randomized trials, beta-blockers did not significantly increase depressive symptoms, though fatigue rose slightly [Ko-etal-2002].

## In the session

- If symptoms began within weeks of starting or changing a medicine or supplement → ask the client to raise it with the prescriber and record the timeline. Because corticosteroids and montelukast can cause mood and anxiety symptoms [Warrington-Bostwick-2006; FDA-Montelukast-DSC-2020].
- If attacks start and stop abruptly with a very fast pulse, or include fainting → ask for a medical review of heart rhythm before treating them as panic disorder. Because PSVT is often misread as panic [Lessmeier-etal-1997].
- If a heavy drinker plans to stop → advise medical assessment before stopping, never sudden unsupervised abstinence. Because withdrawal can bring seizures [ASAM-Alcohol-Withdrawal-2020].
- If fatigue and low mood come with loud snoring or witnessed pauses in breathing → suggest a primary care assessment for sleep apnea. Because treating apnea improves depressive symptoms [Povitz-etal-2014].
- If caffeine intake is high and panic is the target → put caffeine in the case formulation and test reducing it as a behavioral experiment. Because high doses provoke panic in people with panic disorder, while a single ordinary cup may not [Klevebrant-Frick-2022; Hoppe-etal-2025].
- If a physical cause is found → keep treating the psychological part alongside it. Because depression is common after stroke and in Parkinson's disease [Hackett-Pickles-2014; Reijnders-etal-2008].
- If the client has health anxiety → agree one proportionate medical review with the primary care clinician rather than repeated tests; see `health-anxiety.overview`. Because repeated testing can work as reassurance seeking (expert consensus).

Example phrasing: "Before we treat this as anxiety alone, I'd like to be sure your body has been checked. When did you last see your doctor, and did any medicine change around the time this started?"
