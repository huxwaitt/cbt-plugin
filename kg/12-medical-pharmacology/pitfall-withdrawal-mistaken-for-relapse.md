**Bottom line:** Withdrawal usually starts within days of a reduction or missed dose, includes unfamiliar physical sensations, and eases quickly when the dose is restored; relapse usually takes weeks and repeats the original pattern. Misreading withdrawal as relapse can lead to unnecessary long-term medication [Horowitz-Taylor-2019], lost confidence in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) skills, or a sense that the client "can't cope without pills."

## What it looks like

- A client who stopped sertraline last week reports dizziness, irritability, poor sleep and low mood, and says "the depression is back."
- Mood scores jump the week after a dose reduction, and the therapist adds sessions for "relapse."
- A client missing doses on weekends reports a pattern of "bad Mondays."
- The client restarts the full dose, feels better within a day, and concludes they need the medicine forever.

## Why it happens

- Withdrawal and relapse share symptoms: anxiety, low mood, poor sleep and irritability [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024; FDA-Sertraline-Label].
- Withdrawal has been underestimated; estimates range from about 15% attributable to the drug to more than half of people stopping [Henssler-etal-2024; Davies-Read-2019], and misreading it as relapse can prolong prescribing [Davies-Read-2019].
- Short tapers have been standard, although they may offer little over abrupt stopping [Horowitz-Taylor-2019].
- Therapists do not ask about recent dose changes when scores rise.

## The fix

- If scores rise or symptoms change → ask first: "Has anything changed with your medicine in the last two weeks, including missed doses?" Because withdrawal usually starts within days of a reduction or missed dose [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].
- If the timing fits and there are new sensations such as dizziness or electric-shock "zaps" → name possible withdrawal and ask the client to contact the prescriber. Because these point toward withdrawal and the pace can be adjusted [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].
- If the client takes fluoxetine → remember symptoms can be delayed by days or weeks. Because of its long half-life [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].
- If the client stopped or cut down a benzodiazepine → treat withdrawal as potentially serious and contact the prescriber. Because abrupt stopping can cause seizures [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020], and protracted withdrawal can last months and be hard to tell from returning anxiety [FDA-Alprazolam-Label].
- If symptoms return weeks after stopping in the original pattern → reassess with your usual measures, follow the relapse plan and inform the prescriber. Because relapse usually takes weeks to months to appear [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].
- If the client concludes "I can't cope without it" → explore the evidence, separating body adjustment from coping ability. Because how symptoms are interpreted shapes confidence (expert consensus).

Example phrasing: "These zaps and the dizziness started three days after the dose drop. That timing fits withdrawal more than depression returning. Let's let your prescriber know so they can adjust the pace."

## Sources note

The withdrawal versus relapse distinctions come from Royal College of Psychiatrists patient guidance [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024], consistent with the label's list of discontinuation symptoms [FDA-Sertraline-Label]. Incidence estimates disagree between meta-analyses [Henssler-etal-2024; Davies-Read-2019]. The point about misdiagnosis leading to unnecessary long-term use is made by Horowitz and Taylor [Horowitz-Taylor-2019].
