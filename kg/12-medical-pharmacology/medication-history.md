**Bottom line:** Ask about every medicine the client takes, not just psychiatric ones, plus supplements, contraception, caffeine, alcohol and drugs. For each psychiatric medicine record the purpose, dose as the client reports it, prescriber, start date, adherence, side effects, past trials and what the client believes it does. Recheck at every review, because a stale list hides the most common medical explanations for change.

## When to use

- At intake for every client.
- At formal reviews and whenever symptoms change unexpectedly.
- After any hospital visit, new diagnosis or new prescriber.

## Steps

1. **Ask open, then specific.** "What medicines do you take, for anything at all?" Then prompt: inhalers, steroid courses, contraception, sleep aids, pain relief, over-the-counter products, herbal remedies and supplements. Corticosteroids, montelukast and hormonal contraception are all linked to mood or anxiety change [Warrington-Bostwick-2006; FDA-Montelukast-DSC-2020; Skovlund-etal-2016], and St John's wort interacts with many medicines [NCCIH-St-Johns-Wort].
2. **For each psychiatric medicine, ask:**
   - What is it for, and who prescribes it?
   - What dose, and when do you take it?
   - When did you start, and has the dose changed recently?
   - "Most people miss doses sometimes. How many times in the last week?"
   - Side effects, including sexual side effects, which clients rarely volunteer [FDA-Sertraline-Label].
3. **Ask about past treatments** and how well each worked, because further treatment choices depend on what has been tried [Kendrick-etal-2022].
4. **Ask about substances:** caffeine (coffee, energy drinks, pre-workout products), alcohol, cannabis, nicotine, stimulants and any medicine taken in ways not prescribed. High caffeine doses provoke panic in people with panic disorder [Klevebrant-Frick-2022]. For stimulant medicines, misuse risk must be reassessed throughout treatment [FDA-Lisdexamfetamine-Label].
5. **Ask what the client believes the medicine does.** "What difference do you notice when you take it, or forget it?" Clients who credited their gains to medication rather than their own efforts lost more ground after stopping [Basoglu-etal-1994].
6. **Ask about plans:** any planned change, a wish to stop, or a prescriber review date.
7. **Verify when needed.** With consent, compare with the prescriber's or pharmacy record when the account is unclear or the stakes are high.

## What to record

- A table: medicine, purpose, dose and timing as reported, prescriber, start date, recent changes, adherence, side effects, client's view of benefit.
- Supplements, contraception and over-the-counter products.
- Substance use with amounts and frequency.
- Past psychiatric medicines and response.
- Consent to contact the prescriber, and the date the list was last checked.
- Label everything as client report unless verified.

## Guidance

- If the client cannot name their medicine → ask them to bring the packets or a pharmacy list next session. Because accurate names and doses are needed to recognize side effects and interactions (expert consensus).
- If the client says "I only take it when I feel bad" about a regular-dose medicine → record it and tell the prescriber with consent. Because irregular use changes both benefit and side effects, and for benzodiazepines as-needed use predicted weaker cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) gains [Westra-etal-2002].
- If symptoms began after a new medicine → note the timeline and refer the question to the prescriber. Because medicines such as corticosteroids can cause psychiatric effects [Warrington-Bostwick-2006].
- If the client asks you whether to keep taking something → explain that it is the prescriber's decision and help them frame the question. Because dose advice is outside a non-prescriber's scope (scope of practice; see `12-medical-pharmacology.pitfall-advising-on-medication-changes`).

## Common problems

- **Asking only about psychiatric drugs.** The steroid course, contraceptive or asthma tablet is missed.
- **Judgmental tone about substances.** Clients under-report. Ask matter-of-factly and normalize.
- **One-time list.** Medicines change between reviews; recheck at each formal review.
- **Interpreting instead of referring.** Record and pass on; do not diagnose side effects yourself.
