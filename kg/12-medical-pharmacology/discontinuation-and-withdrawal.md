**Bottom line:** Stopping antidepressants or benzodiazepines should be a planned, gradual taper led by the prescriber. Withdrawal symptoms are common, usually begin within days, and are easily mistaken for relapse [Henssler-etal-2024; RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024]. The therapist's job is to help the client plan with the prescriber, recognize withdrawal, keep cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) skills active, and have a relapse plan ready.

## The principle

**How common is antidepressant withdrawal?** Estimates disagree. A 2024 meta-analysis of 79 studies (21,002 patients) found at least one discontinuation symptom in 31% after stopping antidepressants versus 17% after stopping placebo, estimated that about 15% have symptoms attributable to the drug, and found severe symptoms in 2.8% [Henssler-etal-2024]. An earlier review of 14 studies estimated incidence at 56%, with 46% of those affected rating them at the most severe level offered, and some lasting months [Davies-Read-2019]. A 2025 reanalysis by critics of the 2024 review, pooling only five studies that measured withdrawal systematically, found 55% [Moncrieff-etal-2025]. Part of the gap reflects the kinds of studies each review included (industry trials with short treatment versus surveys and systematic symptom checklists). Plan for withdrawal as a real possibility either way.

**How tapering works.** The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) 2022 guidance advises reducing in steps, each a proportion of the previous dose (for example 50%), with smaller steps (for example 25%) as the dose gets lower, slower tapers for short half-life drugs, and liquid forms when tablets cannot be split finely enough [Kendrick-etal-2022]. The rationale is that effect on the serotonin transporter falls steeply at low doses, so equal-sized cuts hit hardest at the end [Horowitz-Taylor-2019]. The Royal College of Psychiatrists gives example plans of about 50% every 2 to 4 weeks, or about 10% for a slower plan, and advises returning to the last comfortable dose if symptoms develop [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].

**Benzodiazepines.** Abrupt stopping can cause seizures [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020]. Protracted withdrawal can last weeks to more than 12 months, and the label warns it can be hard to tell from the original anxiety returning [FDA-Alprazolam-Label]. The 2025 joint guideline advises starting with about 5% to 10% reductions every 2 to 4 weeks, typically no faster than 25% every 2 weeks, pausing if symptoms emerge, and offering CBT or CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) during the taper [Brunner-etal-2025].

**Withdrawal or relapse?**

| Feature | Withdrawal | Relapse |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Within days of a reduction or missed dose | Usually weeks to months later |
| Symptoms | New sensations such as dizziness, electric-shock "zaps" and nausea, plus anxiety, poor sleep and low mood | The original pattern of symptoms returns |
| Response to restarting | Improves within hours to days | Takes weeks |

Source for the table: [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024]. Fluoxetine's long half-life can delay withdrawal by days or weeks [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].

## Guidance

- If a client wants to stop → help them prepare questions for the prescriber: taper speed, what to do if symptoms appear, and how CBT fits. Because NICE frames stopping as a shared, staged decision [Kendrick-etal-2022].
- If new dizziness, zaps or nausea appear within days of a dose cut or missed doses → name possible withdrawal and ask the client to contact the prescriber, rather than treating it as relapse. Because withdrawal usually starts within days and eases quickly on restarting [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].
- If the client skips doses to "test" whether they still need the medicine → explain that skipping can provoke withdrawal and confuse the picture. Because irregular dosing makes withdrawal more likely [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].
- If the original symptoms return weeks after stopping → reassess with your usual measures, review the relapse plan, and inform the prescriber. Because relapse after discontinuation is common: 56% versus 39% with continued medication over a year [Lewis-etal-2021].
- If the client is anxious about tapering → use CBT for the taper itself: predictions, coping cards, and graded steps. Because CBT plus taper led 76% to stop benzodiazepines versus 25% with taper alone [Otto-etal-1993].
- If suicidal thoughts emerge during a reduction → assess risk now, contact the prescriber urgently, and follow `02-risk.crisis-response` if risk is imminent. Because suicidal thoughts can be a withdrawal symptom or returning depression, and guidance advises immediate prescriber contact [RCPsych-Stopping-Antidepressants-2024].
- If a client with recurrent depression wants an alternative to long-term medication → mention mindfulness-based cognitive therapy with tapering support. Because it performed similarly to maintenance antidepressants (hazard ratio 0.89) [Kuyken-etal-2015].

## Example phrasings

- "Feeling dizzy with those electric-shock sensations three days after a dose drop sounds more like withdrawal than your depression coming back. Your prescriber can help with the pace."
- "What would be your early signs that low mood is returning, rather than your body adjusting?"
- Avoid: "That's just your depression coming back."

## Evidence

Incidence estimates come from systematic reviews that disagree on size [Henssler-etal-2024; Davies-Read-2019; Moncrieff-etal-2025]. Proportional tapering rests on pharmacological reasoning and guideline committee consensus rather than trials [Horowitz-Taylor-2019; Kendrick-etal-2022]. Relapse risk after stopping comes from a randomized trial [Lewis-etal-2021]. CBT support for benzodiazepine discontinuation is supported by a randomized trial [Otto-etal-1993] and recommended in the 2025 guideline with low-certainty evidence [Brunner-etal-2025].
