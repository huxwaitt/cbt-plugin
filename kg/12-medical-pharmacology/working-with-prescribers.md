**Bottom line:** A cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) therapist should treat the prescriber as a teammate: get consent early, agree who does what, share brief structured updates with measure scores, and pass on side effects, adherence concerns and risk promptly. Never advise on starting, changing or stopping a medicine; help the client take those questions to the prescriber. Collaborative care, where roles and information are shared by design, improves depression and anxiety outcomes compared with usual care [Archer-etal-2012].

## When to use

- At intake, whenever a client takes psychiatric medication or has a primary care clinician who prescribes.
- When a client reports side effects, missed doses, a wish to stop, or new risk.
- At planned reviews, non-response, and the end of therapy.

## Steps

1. **Identify the prescriber.** Name, role (psychiatrist, primary care physician, nurse practitioner, physician assistant), contact route and the client's preferred level of sharing. Prescribing authority depends on license and jurisdiction; check yours and theirs.
2. **Get informed consent.** Explain what you will share and why. In the United States (US), the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule lets a covered provider disclose information for another provider's treatment activities without a separate authorization [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-506], but asking first protects the therapeutic alliance. Psychotherapy notes need written authorization [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-508]; medication monitoring, session times, test results and summaries of diagnosis, symptoms, plan and progress are not psychotherapy notes [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-501]. A state privacy law that is more stringent than HIPAA still applies, so check yours [HIPAA-45-CFR-160-203]. Records from federally assisted substance use disorder programs fall under Part 2 of Title 42 of the Code of Federal Regulations (42 CFR Part 2), which allows a single written consent for treatment, payment and health care operations [CFR-42-2-33]. In the United Kingdom (UK), share under UK data protection law and local National Health Service (NHS) policy, normally with the client's knowledge.
3. **Agree roles in writing.** Who monitors which measure, how often you will update each other, who manages risk out of hours, and how urgent concerns are passed on.
4. **Send structured updates** using situation, background, assessment, request (SBAR), a format borrowed from medical handovers (expert consensus):
   - Situation: "I'm writing about our shared patient, Ms. A, seen weekly for CBT for panic disorder."
   - Background: start date, sessions so far, current medicine as the client reports it.
   - Assessment: measure scores and trend, side effects the client describes, adherence, risk status.
   - Request: one clear question or piece of information you need.
5. **Call rather than write** when a concern is same-day (see `12-medical-pharmacology.when-to-refer-medically`).
6. **Close the loop** at discharge with a summary and relapse prevention plan.

## What to record

- Consent: what the client agreed to share, with whom, and any limits.
- Each contact: date, method, what was shared, the reply, and actions.
- Client reports about medication (name, dose, side effects, adherence) labeled as client report.
- Any advice you gave, which should be "discuss with your prescriber," never dose advice.

## Guidance

- If a client wants to stop or change a medicine → encourage them to speak to the prescriber first and offer to share progress data. Because the 2022 depression guideline frames stopping as a shared decision with a gradual taper [Kendrick-etal-2022], and the patient guide says not to stop without talking to a prescriber [FDA-Sertraline-Label].
- If a client reports side effects but is embarrassed to raise them (for example sexual effects) → help them rehearse what to say or, with consent, mention it in your update. Because the label advises asking about sexual function, which patients often do not volunteer [FDA-Sertraline-Label].
- If a client plans to stop a benzodiazepine on their own → contact the prescriber with their consent. Because abrupt stopping can cause seizures and the taper must be individualized [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020].
- If the prescriber and you disagree about the plan → discuss directly, base it on shared measures, and avoid putting the client in the middle. Because mixed messages from the team confuse the client (expert consensus).
- If you have not heard back about a non-urgent concern within an agreed time → follow up once, then escalate through the practice. Because unacknowledged messages are a common failure point in shared care (expert consensus).

Example phrasing to the client: "Your doctor is part of your team. Would it be OK if I send her a short note with your scores each month, so you don't have to repeat everything?"

## Common problems

- **Silos.** Each clinician assumes the other is monitoring. Collaborative care, in which clinicians share one plan and a case manager coordinates, is designed to prevent this; across 79 trials it improved depression outcomes at short, medium and long term (standardized mean differences about 0.28 to 0.35) [Archer-etal-2012].
- **Over-sharing.** Sending full session content. Send what the prescriber needs: scores, risk, side effects and your question.
- **Scope creep.** Recommending drugs or doses. See `12-medical-pharmacology.pitfall-advising-on-medication-changes`.
- **Stale medication lists.** Recheck at reviews; see `12-medical-pharmacology.medication-history`.
