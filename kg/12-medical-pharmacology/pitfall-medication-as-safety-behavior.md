**Bottom line:** An as-needed pill taken before or carried during exposure can work like any other safety behavior: it prevents the client from learning that they can cope and that the feared outcome does not happen. As-needed benzodiazepine users gained least from cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for panic [Westra-etal-2002]. Put the medicine in the case formulation, and plan any change with the prescriber.

## What it looks like

- Exposure tasks are completed "successfully," but the client took a tablet beforehand or kept one in a pocket.
- The client says "I could only do it because I had my pill."
- Anxiety ratings fall in session but avoidance returns between sessions.
- Gains vanish when the prescription runs out or is tapered.
- The medicine never appears in the case formulation or the safety behavior list.

## Why it happens

- Therapists do not ask about as-needed medicines, only regular ones.
- Clients see a prescribed drug as treatment, not as a way of avoiding.
- Therapists worry that discussing medication oversteps their role.
- Prescriptions may be written for as-needed use, which Canadian guidelines advise against for anxiety disorders [Katzman-etal-2014].

## The fix

- If you are building a safety behavior list → ask specifically: "Do you take or carry anything, like a tablet, water or a phone app, to get through these situations?" Because as-needed benzodiazepine use predicted the fewest CBT gains, while regular scheduled users did about as well as unmedicated clients [Westra-etal-2002].
- If the pill is part of the pattern → add it to the formulation and plan graded exposure steps without it, agreed with the prescriber. Because gains made with alprazolam were lost after stopping, while exposure gains lasted [Marks-etal-1993].
- If the client credits the pill for coping → review the evidence of their own actions after each task. Because attributing improvement to medication predicted relapse [Basoglu-etal-1994].
- If the client and prescriber decide to taper → offer CBT skills alongside. Because in a small randomized trial CBT plus taper led 76% to stop versus 25% with taper alone [Otto-etal-1993], and the 2025 tapering guideline recommends offering CBT [Brunner-etal-2025].
- If the client is not ready to change the medicine → keep it stable and visible, and plan exposure steps where its use is recorded each time, rather than ignoring it (expert consensus).

Example phrasing: "When you went into the supermarket, what did you have with you that helped you feel safe? I wonder what we'd learn if, at some point, you tried a step without it."

Avoid: "You need to throw those pills away before we can do exposure."

## Sources note

The core evidence is a naturalistic CBT study of benzodiazepine use [Westra-etal-2002] and a randomized trial of alprazolam and exposure with its attribution follow-up [Marks-etal-1993; Basoglu-etal-1994]. These involve benzodiazepines in panic disorder with agoraphobia; extending the principle to other as-needed medicines, such as beta-blockers before performances, is expert consensus.
