**Bottom line:** Benzodiazepines and z-drug sleeping pills work fast but carry risks of dependence, withdrawal, falls, next-day impairment and dangerous interactions with opioids and alcohol. As-needed use for anxiety predicted poorer cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) outcomes in panic disorder [Westra-etal-2002], and gains made on alprazolam were lost after stopping [Marks-etal-1993]. CBT gains last, and adding CBT to a slow taper roughly tripled the rate of coming off a benzodiazepine [Otto-etal-1993].

## What it is

| Group | Examples | Main uses |
|---|---|---|
| Benzodiazepines | Alprazolam, clonazepam, lorazepam, diazepam | Short-term relief of acute anxiety, panic, agitation; sometimes sleep |
| Z-drugs | Zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon | Short-term treatment of insomnia [FDA-Zolpidem-Label] |
| Others | Buspirone, hydroxyzine, pregabalin, propranolol, sedating antidepressants, orexin receptor antagonists | Varied; ask the prescriber about each client's drug |

In 2019 about 92 million benzodiazepine prescriptions were dispensed in the United States (US); alprazolam, clonazepam and lorazepam made up most of them [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020].

## Why it matters in CBT

- **Safety.** In 2020 the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) extended the class boxed warning to cover abuse, misuse, addiction, physical dependence and withdrawal reactions. Stopping abruptly can cause seizures, and combined with opioids or other sedatives, benzodiazepines can cause fatal breathing suppression [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020]. Protracted withdrawal symptoms can last weeks to more than 12 months [FDA-Alprazolam-Label].
- **Exposure.** In a London and Toronto trial of panic disorder with agoraphobia using high-dose alprazolam (5 mg a day), exposure had about twice the effect of alprazolam on non-panic measures; alprazolam gains were lost after the taper, exposure gains lasted, and combining them weakened improvement after treatment ended [Marks-etal-1993]. Clients who credited the tablets rather than their own efforts lost more ground [Basoglu-etal-1994]. In routine CBT, as-needed users made the fewest gains, while regular scheduled users did about as well as unmedicated clients [Westra-etal-2002].
- **Guideline position.** Canadian anxiety guidelines advise restricting benzodiazepines to short-term use and dosing them regularly rather than as needed, with caution in substance use disorders and in people over 65 because of falls and fractures [Katzman-etal-2014]. For insomnia, the American College of Physicians recommends CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) as initial treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia disorder (strong recommendation), with medicines considered through shared decision-making only if CBT-I alone has not worked (weak recommendation) [Qaseem-etal-2016].
- **Coming off.** In a small randomized trial (33 patients), 76% of people with panic disorder came off benzodiazepines with CBT plus a slow taper versus 25% with taper alone [Otto-etal-1993]. The 2025 joint tapering guideline recommends offering CBT or CBT-I during a taper [Brunner-etal-2025].

## Key details

- **Z-drug warning.** Complex sleep behaviors such as sleepwalking and sleep-driving have caused serious injury and death; the drug should be stopped after any episode. Next-day impairment can occur without the person feeling it [FDA-Zolpidem-Label].
- **Taper pace.** The joint guideline suggests starting with reductions of 5% to 10% every 2 to 4 weeks, typically not exceeding 25% every 2 weeks, and full tapering may take months to years after long, high-dose use [Brunner-etal-2025].
- **Scope.** Only the prescriber changes the drug or dose. The therapist maps the drug's role in the case formulation and supports the plan.

## In the session

- If a client carries a benzodiazepine "just in case" → map it as a possible safety behavior and discuss a plan with the prescriber. Because as-needed use predicted the fewest CBT gains [Westra-etal-2002].
- If a client takes a regular scheduled dose during exposure therapy → keep the dose stable rather than starting a taper mid-course, and plan any reduction with the prescriber. Because regular users did about as well as unmedicated clients [Westra-etal-2002], and unplanned changes confuse what the exposure is teaching (expert consensus).
- If a client attributes progress to the tablets → review the evidence of their own coping in each exposure. Because drug attribution predicted relapse [Basoglu-etal-1994].
- If a client asks for a sleeping pill → explain that CBT-I is the recommended first treatment and the medicine question belongs to the prescriber. Because CBT-I is the initial treatment in guidelines [Qaseem-etal-2016].
- If a client also uses opioids or drinks heavily → raise it with the prescriber promptly. Because the combination can stop breathing [FDA-Benzodiazepine-DSC-2020].
- If a client reports sleepwalking, sleep-eating or driving with no memory on a z-drug → tell them to contact the prescriber the same day. Because the drug should be stopped [FDA-Zolpidem-Label].

Example phrasing: "Lots of people keep a tablet in their bag as insurance. Part of what we're testing is what happens when you face the situation without it. Would you be willing to talk with your doctor about a plan for that?" Avoid: "You should stop taking those."
