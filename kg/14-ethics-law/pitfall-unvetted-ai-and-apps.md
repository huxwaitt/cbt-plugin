**Bottom line:** Any vendor that handles protected health information (PHI) for a covered clinician needs a written business associate agreement [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-502], and client data should not enter an artificial intelligence (AI) tool without the client's informed consent [BACP-Ethical-Framework-2026]. Consumer chatbots are not therapists, and several states now penalize presenting them as such [BakerDonelson-2025] [CO-HB26-1195].

## What it looks like

- Pasting a session summary with names into a general AI assistant to "tidy up" a note.
- Switching on an ambient scribe in a free trial before any contract is signed.
- Messaging clients through a consumer chat app because it is convenient.
- Telling a client to "talk to the chatbot when you can't reach me", including in a crisis.

## Why it happens

- Documentation burden and time pressure.
- Assuming a well-known technology brand is compliant by default.
- Not knowing that consumer apps may use data for advertising or model training.

## The fix

- If you want AI help with notes → use only a tool under a signed business associate agreement in the United States (US), or data processing terms in the United Kingdom (UK), and remove identifiers otherwise. Because vendors handling PHI need written assurances [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-502].
- If client data would enter an AI or digital tool → explain the tool, its risks and data handling, and get consent first. Because members of the British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy (BACP) must have informed consent before inputting personal data and must understand how data are stored [BACP-Ethical-Framework-2026].
- If a client asks about using a chatbot for support → present it, at most, as an adjunct, and give human crisis routes (988 or 911 in the US; 999 or Samaritans 116 123 in the UK). Because chatbots should not replace a qualified clinician [APA-AI-Advisory-2025].
- If you recommend a mental health app → check what it does with user data. Because some states restrict sharing chatbot users' health information and using it for ads [UT-HB452-2025].
- If a tool claims its conversations are as confidential as therapy → treat that as a red flag. Because Colorado treats such claims as deceptive [CO-HB26-1195].

## Sources note

The rules come from the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), state statutes, professional frameworks and the American Psychological Association (APA) advisory. Evidence on harm from data leaks in therapy is limited; this pitfall rests on law and expert consensus.
