**Bottom line:** Since the 1976 Tarasoff ruling, United States (US) therapists who determine, or should determine, that a client poses a serious danger of violence to another person must use reasonable care to protect the intended victim [Tarasoff-1976]. States now differ: in the 2022 National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) chart, 28 states make the duty mandatory, 16 permissive, 3 mixed, and 3 impose none, while the District of Columbia is permissive [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022]. Know your state's trigger and approved actions before you need them, consult the same day, and document. The United Kingdom (UK) has no Tarasoff statute; disclosure is a public interest judgment.

## When to use

- A client makes a serious threat of physical violence against a clearly or reasonably identifiable person.
- A client describes intent and the means to harm someone, even without a direct threat.
- A collateral source reports that the client has threatened someone.

## Steps

1. **Assess the threat clinically** using `02-risk.harm-to-others`: target, intent, plan, means, history, and what is stopping the client.
2. **Check your state's rule.** Mandatory states require action once the trigger is met; permissive states allow disclosure on professional judgment; some states also cover threats to property or self-harm [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022]. The NCSL chart, updated March 2022, lists Maine, North Carolina and North Dakota as having no duty, and Delaware, Illinois and Georgia as mixed or unclear [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022]. Check the current statute, since charts age.
3. **Identify the trigger** in your statute. The common one is an explicit or serious threat of physical violence against an identifiable victim, with apparent intent and ability [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022].
4. **Consult the same day** with a supervisor, risk manager or attorney unless danger is immediate.
5. **Choose the protective action** your state recognizes. Options named in statutes include reasonable efforts to warn the victim, notifying police near the client or victim, and seeking voluntary or involuntary hospitalization; some states also accept other reasonable precautions such as a revised treatment plan [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022].
6. **Disclose the minimum.** Under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), you may disclose in good faith to someone reasonably able to prevent or lessen a serious and imminent threat, including the target [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-512]. Ethics codes permit disclosure to protect others where law allows [APA-Ethics-2017] and limit it to what is needed [NASW-Code-2021].
7. **If danger is imminent**, call 911 (US) or 999 (UK) and stay with the protective plan.
8. **Continue treatment** where safe and tell the client what you did, unless doing so increases risk.

## What to record

The client's exact words, your risk formulation, the statute or rule you applied, who you consulted, what you disclosed, to whom, when and why, and the follow-up plan.

## Guidance

- If a client says "I could kill him" in frustration with no intent or plan → explore it calmly, assess intent, and document why no duty arose. Because the duty turns on a serious threat with apparent intent and ability, not on angry speech [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022].
- If the threat is serious and specific → act the same day using a route your statute names. Because once the therapist determines the danger, reasonable care to protect the victim is owed [Tarasoff-1976].
- If you practice by telehealth across state lines → apply the law of the state where the client is, and know its emergency numbers. Because duty-to-warn rules differ by state [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022].
- If you work in a permissive state and are unsure → consult and record the reasoning either way. Because counselors consult when in doubt about an exception [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If you practice in the UK → weigh the risk of serious harm to others against the consequences of sharing, seek consent where safe, and record your reasons. Because regulators expect a justified, recorded public interest decision [HCPC-Confidentiality-Guidance], and practitioners must know their legal obligations on serious crime [BACP-Ethical-Framework-2026].

Example phrasing: "What you just said worries me. I need to understand it properly, because if someone is in real danger I have to act to keep them safe, and I would rather work that out with you."

## Common problems

- Assuming every state has a mandatory duty, or that none applies.
- Warning without assessing, consulting or recording; or freezing because confidentiality feels absolute.
- Not telling clients about this limit at intake (see `14-ethics-law.confidentiality-and-limits`).
