**Bottom line:** What a client says in therapy stays private except in a short list of situations that the client should hear about at the start: serious risk of harm to self or others, suspected abuse of a child or adult at risk, a court order or other legal requirement, and routine sharing for supervision, billing or care coordination. Discuss these limits at the outset and again when circumstances change [APA-Ethics-2017]. When disclosure is needed, share only what the purpose requires, with someone able to act, and record why.

## The principle

Confidentiality is an ethical and legal duty. Codes require clinicians to protect confidential information, to explain its relevant limits and foreseeable uses at the start of the relationship, and to revisit them as new circumstances arise [APA-Ethics-2017]. Clients have the right to be told how supervisors and treatment teams are involved, and to hear about the limits throughout therapy, not only at the start [ACA-Ethics-2014]. Exceptions exist where disclosure is required by law or needed to protect the client or identified others from serious and foreseeable harm [ACA-Ethics-2014], or to prevent "serious, foreseeable, and imminent harm" in social work terms, sharing the least information needed [NASW-Code-2021].

The usual limits, in plain terms:

| Limit | What it means | Read next |
|---|---|---|
| Danger to others | A serious threat against someone may trigger a duty to warn or protect, or a permitted disclosure | `14-ethics-law.duty-to-warn-and-protect` |
| Danger to self | Disclosure to protect the client is permitted when law allows [APA-Ethics-2017] | `02-risk.crisis-response` |
| Abuse | Suspected child abuse must be reported; adult abuse duties vary by state | `14-ethics-law.mandatory-reporting` |
| Legal process | Court orders and other legal requirements [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-512] | `14-ethics-law.record-keeping` |
| Routine operations | Supervision, consultation without identifying details, billing and insurance [APA-Ethics-2017] | `14-ethics-law.hipaa-and-gdpr` |
| Minors | Parents may have access, depending on state law | `14-ethics-law.informed-consent` |

Records of federally assisted substance use disorder programs carry extra United States (US) protection under Title 42 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) Part 2, including limits on use in proceedings against the patient [CFR-42-2-33]. In the United Kingdom (UK), disclosure without consent rests on a public interest judgment weighing harm from not sharing against the consequences of sharing [HCPC-Confidentiality-Guidance], and practitioners must tell clients about foreseeable limits, including legal duties and data-collecting platforms [BACP-Ethical-Framework-2026].

## Guidance

- If this is the first meeting → explain the limits before the client starts telling their story, in plain words, and check they understood. Because the discussion should occur at the outset unless not feasible [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If a client asks "Will you tell anyone?" mid-disclosure → answer honestly: name the specific limits that could apply, then let the client choose what to say. Because promising secrecy you cannot keep damages trust later and breaches the duty to explain limits [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If a family member calls asking how the client is doing → do not confirm details unless the client agreed or had a chance to object; in an emergency share only what is directly relevant. Because the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) limits such disclosures to information relevant to the person's involvement in care [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-510].
- If you want case advice from a colleague → remove identifying details and share only what the question needs. Because consultations must not identify the client without consent [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If a serious threat to an identifiable person emerges → consult the same day and follow `14-ethics-law.duty-to-warn-and-protect`. Because HIPAA permits good-faith disclosure to someone able to prevent a serious and imminent threat [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-512].
- If a client discloses imminent suicidal intent → follow `02-risk.crisis-response`, and share what is needed with emergency services or a person able to help (US: 911, or the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline; UK: 999). Because HIPAA permits good-faith disclosure to prevent a serious and imminent threat to the client as well as to others [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-512], and codes permit disclosure to protect the client from harm [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If a child abuse concern arises → follow `14-ethics-law.mandatory-reporting`. Because HIPAA permits disclosure to the authority authorized to receive such reports [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-512].
- If disclosure is required → share the minimum, tell the client when it is safe to do so, and record the reasoning. Because disclosure without consent should be limited to what is necessary [APA-Ethics-2017], and UK regulators expect you to justify any decision to disclose or not [HCPC-Confidentiality-Guidance].
- If you are unsure whether an exception applies → consult a supervisor, ethics line or lawyer before acting, unless danger is immediate. Because counselors consult when in doubt about an exception [ACA-Ethics-2014].

## Example phrasings

- "What you tell me stays between us, with a few exceptions I want you to know now. If I believed you or someone else were in serious danger, I would act to keep people safe. If I learned a child or a vulnerable adult was being harmed, the law requires me to report it. A court can sometimes order records. I also discuss my work with a supervisor, and your insurer receives billing details. Any questions about that?"
- To an adolescent: "Most of what you tell me stays private. If you were in danger or someone was hurting you, I would need to involve other people, and I would tell you first whenever I can."
- Avoid: "Everything you say here stays in this room."

## Evidence

These rules come from ethics codes, statutes and regulator guidance rather than trials; treat them as expert consensus and law. Exceptions and their wording vary by state and country, so check local law and your employer's policy.
