**Bottom line:** Informed consent is a conversation that starts before assessment and is revisited whenever the plan changes, not a signature on a form. Tell the client, in language they understand, the nature and likely course of therapy, fees, third-party involvement and the limits of confidentiality, and give them time to ask questions [APA-Ethics-2017]. For exposure, explain the rationale, the expected temporary rise in anxiety, the alternatives and the right to stop, then agree each step together.

## When to use

- First contact and session, before any measure or history-taking.
- Before introducing a technique the client has not agreed to, especially exposure, imagery or behavioral experiments.
- When a trainee delivers therapy, sessions will be recorded, or telehealth or digital tools are added.
- When the client is a minor, is mandated to attend, or may lack capacity for the decision.

## Steps

1. **Give the core information** in writing and aloud. Cover purposes, goals, techniques, limitations, potential risks and benefits; your qualifications and approach; fees, billing and what happens on nonpayment; the role of technology; and what happens if you become unavailable [ACA-Ethics-2014]. Add the limits of confidentiality and the foreseeable uses of the information you collect [APA-Ethics-2017].
2. **Use understandable language.** Adjust for age, culture and literacy, arrange a qualified interpreter when needed [ACA-Ethics-2014], and leave time for questions [NASW-Code-2021].
3. **Check understanding** with teach-back: "So I know I explained it well, what would you tell a friend about how this therapy works?"
4. **State the right to refuse.** Clients may refuse any service or change in approach and should hear the consequences of refusing [ACA-Ethics-2014].
5. **Consent to the plan.** After the formulation, describe the recommended treatment and the alternatives (for example medication, another therapy, or watchful waiting). If a technique is not yet established, say so and describe its risks, alternatives and the voluntary nature of participation [APA-Ethics-2017].
6. **Consent to exposure specifically.** Explain why approaching feared situations helps, that anxiety usually rises at first and then becomes more manageable, that steps are agreed together, and that the client can pause or stop. Agree the first step before doing it. This script is expert consensus resting on the general duty to explain risks, benefits and the right to refuse [ACA-Ethics-2014].
7. **Trainees.** Tell the client the therapist is in training and supervised, and name the supervisor [APA-Ethics-2017].
8. **Recording.** Get permission before recording voice or video [APA-Ethics-2017].
9. **Minors and capacity.** In the United States (US), parents usually consent for minors, but state law decides when a minor can consent alone and how much parents may access, and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule follows that state law [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-502]. Seek the minor's assent and involve them in decisions [ACA-Ethics-2014]. In England, young people aged 16 or 17 are presumed to have capacity, and a child under 16 can consent if Gillick competent [NHS-Consent-Children]. For adults in England and Wales, start from a presumption of capacity and help the person decide before concluding they cannot [MCA-2005].
10. **Revisit consent** when goals, format, setting, fees or risk change. In the United Kingdom (UK), cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) practitioners accredited by the British Association for Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapies (BABCP) must obtain informed consent except in emergencies [BABCP-Standards-2022].

## What to record

- Date, what was explained (list the documents given), questions asked and answers.
- The client's agreement, or refusal, to assessment, the treatment plan and each new technique, including the agreed exposure hierarchy and first step.
- Who gave consent for a minor, the minor's assent, and the legal basis you relied on.
- Any capacity assessment, interpreter use, and permission to record.

## Guidance

- If a client asks "Will this make me worse?" before exposure → say anxiety is likely to rise during early practice, that you will plan the pace together, and that they can pause at any time. Because clients must be told the potential risks as well as benefits [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If you hesitate to offer exposure because it feels harsh → name the hesitation in supervision and still offer the evidence-based option with full consent. Because negative therapist beliefs about exposure predict more cautious delivery and fall after training [Deacon-etal-2013].
- If a client refuses a technique → accept the refusal, explain what it may mean for progress, offer alternatives, and record the discussion. Because clients have the right to refuse modality changes and to know the consequences [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If you change role or format (for example from individual to couple or family work) → obtain fresh consent and restate the right to refuse. Because a role change needs new informed consent [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If an adolescent asks you to keep things from a parent → before they disclose, explain what state law lets you keep private and what you must share. Because HIPAA defers to state law on parental access [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-502].
- If an adult's understanding seems impaired → simplify, use written aids, and give time before judging capacity. Because all practicable steps to help must be tried first [MCA-2005].

Example phrasing: "Before we go on, I want you to know what this involves, what it may cost you in effort and discomfort, and that you can say no to any part."

## Common problems

- Treating a signed intake form as complete consent and never revisiting it.
- Starting an exposure exercise without agreement to that step.
- Not telling clients they are seeing a trainee.
- Assuming one state's rules on minors apply in another.
