**Bottom line:** The downward arrow takes one hot thought, provisionally assumes it is true, and asks what that would mean, again and again, until you reach a global statement about the self, other people or the world. It was first mentioned by Beck and colleagues in 1979 and elaborated by Burns as the vertical arrow technique [Wenzel-2012; Burns-1980]. Use it to find beliefs for the case formulation, not to change them in the same moment.

## When to use

- The same theme keeps appearing across thought records.
- The client's distress seems larger than the situational thought explains.
- You are building or checking the case formulation and need the intermediate or core belief level.
- Wait if the client is new to identifying thoughts or finds belief-level material too painful early on. Most therapists work with situational thoughts first and core beliefs later [Wenzel-2012].

## How it works

Instead of testing the thought, you temporarily accept it and ask about its meaning. Each answer is a step down from situation-specific thoughts toward conditional assumptions ("If I make a mistake, people will think I'm useless") and finally to an absolute core belief ("I'm useless") whose meaning goes no deeper [Wenzel-2012; Beck-2021]. Because beliefs and emotion are closely linked, the strongest emotion usually appears as you near the core belief [Padesky-1994].

## Steps

1. Pick one hot thought from a specific situation with a strong emotion rating.
2. Agree to set testing aside: "Let's suppose, just for now, that this is true."
3. Ask a meaning question. Choose the form that fits the problem:
   - About the self: "If that were true, what would it mean about you?"
   - About others: "What would that say about other people?"
   - About the world or future: "What would it say about how life works?" [Padesky-1994]
   - About consequences (useful in anxiety): "And if that happened, what would be so bad about it? What would happen next?" [Burns-1980]
4. Write each answer under the last, joined by a downward arrow.
5. Stop when the answer is a short, global, absolute statement ("I'm a failure", "People can't be trusted"), when the client repeats the same answer, or when emotion peaks.
6. Check the fit: "How true does that feel right now, 0 to 100?" and "How would you say this in your own words?" [Padesky-1994]
7. Summarize the chain back and link it to the formulation. Ask: "Does this show up in other situations too?"

What to record: the starting situation and thought, each step in the client's words, any if-then assumptions found along the way, the final belief with its rating, and the emotion at each step.

Example chain (social anxiety): "Others will see I'm blushing" → "They'll think something is wrong with me" → "They'll think I'm weak" → "I am weak." A published case reached the core beliefs "I am weak" and "I am less than a man" from a similar start [Wenzel-2012].

## Guidance

- If the client starts to argue with the thought → gently hold the frame: "We'll test it later. For now, if it were true, what would that mean?" Because challenging midway stops the descent before the belief is reached [Burns-1980].
- If the client answers "I don't know" → offer a sentence stem: "I am...", "People are...", "The world is..." Because beliefs are usually absolute and can often be captured in one word [Padesky-1994].
- If strong emotion or tears arrive → slow down, reflect the feeling and ask if they want to continue. Because the aim is to identify a belief, not to flood the client with it, and belief work can feel threatening early in therapy [Wenzel-2012].
- If the chain ends in a feared catastrophe rather than a belief → note the prediction for a behavioral experiment. Because consequence questions often reveal testable predictions rather than self-beliefs [Burns-1980].
- If you suggest a belief wording → ask the client to restate it in their own words and give an example. Because beliefs labeled in the client's language carry more emotion and make later change more meaningful [Padesky-1994].
- If a belief questionnaire suggests a belief → treat it as a hypothesis to check through the downward arrow and session data. Because clients may endorse many items, and the central belief may not be on the list [Wenzel-2012; Padesky-1994].
- If the chain reaches hopelessness or thoughts of death ("I'd be better off dead") → pause the technique and assess suicide risk (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`); if risk is imminent, follow `02-risk.crisis-response` (United States: call or text 988, or 911; United Kingdom: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans 116 123). Because risk takes priority over formulation work.

## Common problems

- **Leading the client.** Asking "Does that mean you're unlovable?" plants your hypothesis. Keep questions open.
- **Going too fast.** Rushing past a strong reaction can feel exposing. Pace to the client.
- **Stopping at an emotion.** "I'd feel terrible" is a feeling; ask what feeling terrible would mean.
- **Treating the result as final.** The first wording often changes over later weeks as the belief becomes clearer [Padesky-1994].

## Evidence

The downward arrow is an assessment technique supported by expert consensus and case descriptions, not by controlled trials [Wenzel-2012]. Its value lies in shaping the formulation and the choice of later belief-change methods. Related structured interviews that link images, early memories and an "encapsulated belief" have been used in research on social anxiety [Wild-etal-2008].
