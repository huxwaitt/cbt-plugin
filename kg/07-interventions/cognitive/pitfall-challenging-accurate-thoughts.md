**Bottom line:** Not every negative thought is distorted. Cognitive therapy teaches clients to reevaluate thinking when it is distorted and to solve problems when it is accurate [Padesky-1993]. Challenging a true thought invalidates the client and damages the alliance. Validate what is true, then look for the distorted conclusion that may sit on top of it, or move to problem solving and acceptance.

## What it looks like

- A client who has just lost a job says "I'm in serious financial trouble," and the therapist asks for evidence against it.
- A client describing racism at work is asked whether they might be "mind reading".
- A grieving client is asked to find a "balanced thought" about the death.
- The client goes quiet, repeats the facts more forcefully, or says "You don't get it."
- The client leaves more hopeless, feeling that even their therapist doubts their reality.

## Why it happens

- Restructuring becomes a reflex: every negative thought is treated as a target.
- Distortion labels are applied before the facts are explored.
- The therapist is uncomfortable with real adversity they cannot fix.
- The therapist does not share the client's social position and underestimates real risks the client faces.

## The fix

- If the thought matches the facts → validate first: "That's a real and serious problem." Then ask, "What can we do about it?" Because accurate thoughts call for problem solving, not disputation [Padesky-1993].
- If the facts are true but the conclusion goes further → separate the two: "You did lose the job. Does that also mean 'I'll never work again' or 'I'm useless'?" Because a distorted conclusion often rides on accurate facts, and only the conclusion needs testing.
- If evidence confirms a real shortfall the client cares about → break the area into graded steps and plan action. Because some beliefs reflect real gaps that action can change [Wenzel-2012].
- If the situation cannot be changed (loss, chronic illness) → shift to acceptance, meaning and self-compassion. Because logic alone does not help when the client needs to feel supported [Gilbert-2009].
- If the thought concerns discrimination → ask about actual experiences and the costs of vigilance before considering any bias. Because protective vigilance can be realistic (see `13-context-culture-development.identity-and-minority-stress`).
- If you have already challenged and the client is hurt → acknowledge it: "I think I jumped to questioning that when it's really happening. I'm sorry." Because naming and repairing a strain restores collaboration (see `alliance-and-stance.rupture-and-repair`).

Example phrasings:
- "From what you've told me, that worry fits the facts. Let's think about what would help most right now."
- "The loss is real. I wonder whether the thought 'It's all my fault' is adding to the pain. Shall we look at that part?"
- Avoid: "What's the evidence that you're struggling financially?"

## Sources note

The distinction between distorted and accurate thoughts is long-standing teaching in cognitive therapy [Padesky-1993]. The emphasis on validation and supportive reasoning draws on compassion-focused therapy [Gilbert-2009], and the non-disputing stance on an expert panel [Kazantzis-etal-2014]. These points rest on expert consensus rather than trials.
