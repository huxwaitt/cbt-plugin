**Bottom line:** Intermediate beliefs are the rules ("I must never let people down"), attitudes ("Making mistakes is terrible") and conditional assumptions ("If I ask for help, people will see I'm weak") that link core beliefs to everyday thoughts [Beck-2021; Wenzel-2012]. Find them, write them as if-then predictions, weigh their costs, and test them in action. Conditional beliefs are often best tested by behavioral experiments [Padesky-1994].

## When to use

- After the client can catch and evaluate situational thoughts.
- When restructuring works in the moment but the same thoughts recur, because an underlying rule keeps generating them.
- When the formulation shows compensatory strategies such as overpreparing, people-pleasing or avoidance, which often follow from a rule.
- In perfectionism, social anxiety, low self-esteem and chronic depression, where rigid rules are common.

## How it works

Rules and assumptions protect the person from a painful core belief: "If I get all As, then I'm successful" guards against "I'm a failure", while its negative twin, "If I get less than an A, I'm a failure", triggers distress whenever the standard is missed [Wenzel-2012]. Rules are rigid, often unspoken, and set standards that real life cannot always meet, and they bias attention toward evidence that fits [Wenzel-2012]. Because assumptions take an if-then form, they make predictions that can be tested by doing something differently [Padesky-1994].

## Steps

1. **Identify.** Look for if-then statements and "musts" in automatic thoughts; use the downward arrow; offer stems such as "If I..., then..." or "I should always..."; ask what the client's compensatory strategy is protecting against [Beck-2021].
2. **Write it as an assumption.** Convert rules and attitudes into testable if-then form: "I must never show weakness" becomes "If I show any weakness, people will lose respect for me" [Beck-2021].
3. **Rate it.** Belief 0 to 100, now and in the moment when it is triggered.
4. **Understand its origins.** "When did you learn this? How did it help you then?" A rule usually made sense when it formed [Wenzel-2012].
5. **Weigh advantages and disadvantages.** Draw a grid with the old rule and a possible new rule across the top and advantages and disadvantages down the side, adapting the grid Wenzel describes for core beliefs [Wenzel-2012].
6. **Test it.** Design a behavioral experiment: make a prediction from the rule, act against it in a planned way, and record what happens [Greenberger-Padesky-2016]. Example: ask a colleague for help and observe their reaction.
7. **Write a flexible replacement.** Keep what was useful and drop the absolute: "It's good to do well, and when I make mistakes I can learn and still be respected."
8. **Strengthen.** Re-rate old and new rules over weeks; repeat experiments; write coping cards for trigger situations.

What to record: the rule in the client's words, belief ratings over time, the advantages and disadvantages grid, each experiment's prediction and outcome, and the new rule.

## Guidance

- If the client defends the rule ("Without high standards I'd be lazy") → explore the advantages fully before the disadvantages. Because rules persist for real reasons, and weighing what the rule does for and against the client lets them judge its usefulness for themselves [Wenzel-2012].
- If the client fears dropping the rule completely → offer a middle position, not the opposite rule. Because a flexible rule is easier to believe and live by than a reversal (expert consensus).
- If a thought record has not shifted a rule → move to a behavioral experiment. Because experiments tend to change belief faster and more broadly than written reasoning [McManus-etal-2012a; Bennett-Levy-2003].
- If the experiment might go badly because others react unpredictably → plan for both outcomes and how the client will cope. Because others' reactions cannot be controlled, and a well-prepared experiment is informative whatever happens [Wenzel-2012].
- If the rule involves safety behaviors (checking, overpreparing) → include dropping the safety behavior in the experiment. Because keeping it prevents the client from learning that the feared outcome does not follow.

Example phrasings:
- "It sounds like there's a rule here, something like 'If I..., then...'. How would you finish that?"
- "What has this rule done for you over the years? And what has it cost you?"
- Avoid: "That rule is irrational." Rules usually made sense once; calling them irrational invites defense.

## Common problems

- **Testing the core belief instead.** "I'm a failure" is too global for one experiment; test the if-then assumption first.
- **Experiments too big.** Start small and safe; grade up.
- **New rule as rigid as the old.** "I must always be flexible" is still a must.
- **Skipping the debrief.** Ask what the result means for the rule and re-rate belief each time.

## Evidence

Modifying assumptions draws on expert consensus in the main manuals [Beck-2021; Greenberger-Padesky-2016]. Supporting experimental work is small: single-session behavioral experiments and thought records both outperformed a control condition in 91 nonclinical participants, with experiments producing faster and broader belief change [McManus-etal-2012a]. Research rarely tests whether belief-level work adds to the effect of situational thought work [Wenzel-2012].
