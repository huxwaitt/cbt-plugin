**Bottom line:** Cognitive restructuring tests one hot thought against the client's own experience, using questions about evidence, alternative explanations, realistic outcomes and usefulness. The aim is a balanced response the client believes and can use, not a more positive thought or a win in an argument [Beck-2021; Padesky-1993]. If the thought turns out to be accurate, the work shifts to problem solving or acceptance.

## When to use

- Once a specific hot thought is identified and rated (see `cognitive.identifying-automatic-thoughts`).
- In depression, anxiety, anger, guilt and shame where a situational thought drives the reaction.
- In session first, then as homework through thought records.
- Not for repetitive worry or rumination where the problem is the process of thinking; see `cognitive.worry-and-rumination-techniques`.

## How it works

Depressed and anxious states bias attention and memory toward information that fits the current belief. Good questions bring relevant information that is outside the client's current focus back into view, so the client can reach a new conclusion themselves [Padesky-1993]. Padesky describes four stages: informational questions, listening (for the client's own words, meanings and emotional reactions), summaries, and synthesizing questions that ask the client to apply what they found [Padesky-1993]. People may learn better from discoveries they reach themselves, though this is untested in therapy [Kazantzis-etal-2014].

## Steps

1. Confirm the target: one hot thought, its belief rating (0 to 100) and the emotion rating.
2. Choose questions that fit the thought [Beck-2021]:
   - Evidence: "What makes you think this is true? What makes you think it might not be completely true?"
   - Alternatives: "Is there another way to explain what happened?"
   - Decatastrophizing: "What is the worst that could happen? If it did, how would you cope? What is the best? What is most likely?"
   - Usefulness: "What is the effect of believing this? What could happen if you thought about it differently?"
   - Distancing: "If a close friend had this thought in this situation, what would you tell them?"
   - Action: "What could you do about it?"
3. Listen for unexpected answers and the client's own words, not only for the answer you expected [Padesky-1993].
4. Summarize the information gathered, in the client's language.
5. Ask a synthesizing question: "Putting this together, how do you see the thought now?" Write the client's balanced response.
6. Re-rate belief in the original thought and the emotion. No shift means something is missing.
7. Turn the result into action: a coping statement, a problem-solving step or a behavioral experiment.

What to record: the thought and ratings before and after, the balanced response in the client's words, the questions that helped most, and the next step.

## Guidance

- If the client agrees quickly ("I suppose so") but the emotion rating does not move → slow down and ask, "What part of you still believes the original thought?" Because surface agreement often means the therapist has argued rather than guided [Padesky-1993].
- If the evidence supports the thought → validate it and move to problem solving: "This is a real problem. What can we do about it?" Because the goal is to reevaluate thinking when it is distorted and to solve problems when it is accurate [Padesky-1993].
- If the worst outcome feels unbearable → spend more time on "How would you cope?" than on probability. Because decatastrophizing covers both how likely the outcome is and how the client could cope with it, and the coping half is often skipped [Beck-2021].
- If the client cannot generate alternatives → ask them to imagine a trusted friend or a person they respect looking at the situation. Because distancing gives access to reasoning the client uses for others but not for themselves [Beck-2021].
- If you notice you already know "the answer" → ask a genuinely curious question instead. Because curiosity, not persuasion, protects collaboration; in Padesky's words, if you are not truly curious to know the answer, don't ask the question [Padesky-1993; Kazantzis-etal-2014].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's look at this together as if we were investigators. What have you noticed that fits the thought? Anything that doesn't fit?"
- "We've gathered quite a bit. How does it look to you now?"
- Avoid: "So you can see that's not true, right?" It signals there is one correct answer.

## Common problems

- **Replacing negative with positive thoughts.** Balanced responses must be believable; "Everything will be fine" usually is not.
- **Working on the wrong thought.** If emotion does not shift, check whether the hot thought was chosen.
- **Intellectual but not emotional change.** Add experiential methods: experiments, imagery or compassion-focused work.
- **Using restructuring as reassurance.** Repeatedly questioning the same worry can become a safety behavior; see `cognitive.pitfall-restructuring-as-reassurance`.

## Evidence

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) packages that include restructuring are supported by many randomized trials: in a meta-analysis of 409 depression trials, CBT beat care as usual or waitlist with Hedges' g = 0.79 [Cuijpers-etal-2023a]. In the United Kingdom, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) lists individual CBT, usually 8 sessions for less severe and 16 for more severe depression, among recommended options [NICE-NG222]. Evidence for the restructuring component itself is mixed. One review found few component studies showing that cognitive interventions add to outcomes [Longmore-Worrell-2007]; a later review concluded that cognitive procedures do reduce depressive symptoms and that cognitive change contributes to improvement however it is produced [Lorenzo-Luaces-etal-2015]. In 55 adults in cognitive therapy for depression, a one standard deviation increase in a therapist's Socratic questioning (relative to that client's own early-session average) predicted a 1.51-point drop on the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) by the next session [Braun-etal-2015].
