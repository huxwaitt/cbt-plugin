**Bottom line:** Automatic thoughts are the quick words or images that pass through a client's mind in a situation and shape how they feel and act. Find them by following emotion: when mood shifts, ask what just went through the client's mind, then pick the hot thought, the one carrying most of the feeling [Beck-2021; Greenberger-Padesky-2016]. Rate belief and emotion before you evaluate anything.

## When to use

- From the first sessions onward, whenever a client describes a specific upsetting moment.
- When you see an emotion shift in session: tears, a change in voice, looking away, a sudden pause. These moments give direct access to thoughts while the emotion is active [Beck-2021].
- Before any thought record, restructuring or downward arrow, which all need one specific, emotionally charged thought.
- Not as the first move in acute crisis. Steady the session and assess risk first.

## How it works

In the cognitive model, situations do not cause emotions directly; the meaning a person gives them does. Automatic thoughts are the surface level of that meaning, with intermediate beliefs (rules and assumptions) and core beliefs below them [Beck-2021]. Many automatic thoughts are images rather than words. Imagery tends to evoke stronger emotion than verbal thought, so ask about pictures as well as words [Holmes-Mathews-2010]. The thoughts tied to the strongest emotion usually sit closest to underlying beliefs, which is why Padesky advises therapists to follow the affect [Padesky-1994].

## Steps

1. Get one specific situation: when, where, who, and what happened just before. Summaries such as "work is awful" hide the thoughts.
2. Name each emotion in one word and rate its intensity from 0 to 100 [Greenberger-Padesky-2016]. Separate emotions from thoughts: "I felt like a failure" is a thought.
3. Ask the basic question: "What was going through your mind just then?" [Beck-2021]
4. If nothing comes, try alternatives: have the client picture the moment in the present tense; role-play it; ask "Did you have a picture in your mind?"; ask "What did that say about you?"; or offer a guess or the opposite of the expected thought ("Were you thinking it would go well?") [Beck-2021].
5. List every thought and image. Turn questions into statements: "What if I fail?" becomes "I will fail" [Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
6. Find the hot thought: ask "Which of these makes you feel worst?" Rate how much the client believes it, 0 to 100 [Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
7. Summarize the link: "When your manager didn't reply, you thought 'I'm about to be fired', and anxiety hit 80."

What to record: the situation, emotions with ratings, thoughts and images in the client's own words, the hot thought marked, and its belief rating.

## Guidance

- If the client's mood shifts in session → pause and ask, "What went through your mind just now?" Because in-session shifts give access to hot thoughts while the emotion is live [Beck-2021].
- If the client answers with a feeling ("I just felt bad") → reflect it, then ask, "And when you felt bad, what were you saying to yourself?" Because emotions and thoughts must be told apart before either can be worked with [Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
- If the client says "I wasn't thinking anything" → ask them to replay the moment slowly and notice any image. Because many automatic thoughts are fleeting images rather than sentences [Holmes-Mathews-2010].
- If the thought is a question ("Why does this always happen to me?") → ask, "What answer does your mind give?" Because a question cannot be tested, while the statement behind it can [Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
- If the client cannot name emotions → ask about the body instead: "Are you aware of any tension or change in your body as we talk about this?" Because questions the client can answer build collaboration, while questions they cannot answer underline deficits [Padesky-1993].
- If several thoughts appear → ask which one is hottest and work only on that one. Because evaluating a low-emotion thought rarely shifts mood [Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
- If a thought expresses hopelessness or a wish to die ("They'd be better off without me") → stop the exercise and assess suicide risk directly (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`); if risk is imminent, follow `02-risk.crisis-response`. Because such thoughts can signal risk and take priority over skill teaching. Crisis contacts: in the United States, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) or 911; in the United Kingdom, 999, the National Health Service (NHS) line 111, or Samaritans on 116 123.

Example phrasings:
- "Let's slow that moment down. You read the text, and then what went through your mind?"
- "If that thought had a picture with it, what would we see?"
- Avoid: "That's irrational, isn't it?" Labeling a thought before it is fully heard shuts down disclosure.

## Common problems

- **Censoring.** Normalize shameful thoughts: "Most people have thoughts they'd rather not say. Anything that came up is useful."
- **Interpretations of others.** "He thinks I'm stupid" is the client's prediction about another mind, not a fact.
- **Global statements.** "I am worthless" may be a core belief; note it for later belief work.
- **Neurodivergent or low-literacy clients.** Use emotion lists or pictures; see `13-context-culture-development.autism-adaptations`.

## Evidence

Catching automatic thoughts is a component skill, not a stand-alone treatment, so no trials test it alone. It is a core step in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), whose effect for depression is well established: in a meta-analysis of 409 trials, CBT beat care as usual or waitlist with Hedges' g = 0.79 [Cuijpers-etal-2023a]. The specific questions above rest on expert consensus from the main treatment manuals [Beck-2021; Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
