**Bottom line:** In obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), health anxiety and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), testing the content of a worry can turn into a way of neutralizing it. Each "balanced answer" brings brief relief, then a new doubt appears, and the client needs another answer. When the problem is the process of worrying or doubting, target the process and the reassurance-seeking, not each new content [Normann-Morina-2018].

## What it looks like

- The client completes many thought records a day on the same fear, or rereads old ones for comfort.
- Sessions return to the same question: "But are you sure it's not cancer?" or "Could I really have hit someone?"
- The balanced thought must be perfect, or it "doesn't count".
- Anxiety drops for minutes after restructuring, then returns at the same or higher level.
- The therapist finds themselves giving more and more facts and guarantees.
- Family members are pulled into answering the same questions.

## Why it happens

- Metacognitive therapy (MCT) describes worry and rumination as part of a perseverative style, kept going by beliefs that worrying is useful or uncontrollable [Normann-Morina-2018; Wells-2009]. Evaluating each worry keeps the person engaged with it.
- Seeking certainty works like a safety behavior: short-term relief prevents learning that the doubt can be tolerated.
- Abstract, repeated analysis tends to worsen mood rather than resolve problems [Watkins-2008].
- Therapists are trained to answer thoughts, and answering feels helpful.

## The fix

- If the same content returns after being answered → stop evaluating it and name the pattern: "We've looked at this one carefully. The doubt keeps coming back because doubting is the problem, not the lack of an answer." Because further analysis feeds the loop.
- If the client asks for reassurance → reflect the urge rather than answer: "I notice the pull to get certainty. What happens if we let that question sit?" Because answering reinforces checking.
- If thought records are used many times a day → agree a limit, or switch to postponement and detached mindfulness. Because stepping back from trigger thoughts, rather than processing them, weakens the perseverative style [Normann-Morina-2018].
- If the client has OCD → use exposure and response prevention, treating reassurance-seeking (including self-reassurance and asking family) as a response to prevent. Because family involvement in reassurance should be reduced sensitively as part of the treatment plan [NICE-CG31].
- If worry is about a real, solvable problem → do one round of problem solving, then stop. Because concrete action differs from repeated analysis [Watkins-2008].

Example phrasings:
- "Is this a new piece of information, or the same question in a new form?"
- "What would it be like to leave this one unanswered for now?"
- Avoid: "I promise you, it's definitely not serious."

## Sources note

The process view of worry comes from metacognitive theory and its trials [Normann-Morina-2018; Wells-2009] and from research on constructive and unconstructive repetitive thought [Watkins-2008]. The guidance to reduce family reassurance is from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline on OCD, CG31 (2005), which was still current when checked in November 2026 although an update is in development [NICE-CG31]. The rest is expert consensus; no trial has tested when to stop restructuring and switch to process methods.
