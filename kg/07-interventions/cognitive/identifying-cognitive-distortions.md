**Bottom line:** Cognitive distortions (thinking traps) are recurring biased patterns such as all-or-nothing thinking, mind reading and catastrophizing. Naming the pattern helps a client step back from a thought and choose a testing question [Burns-1980; Beck-2021]. A label is a hypothesis, not a verdict: categories overlap, independent annotators often disagree on them, and a thought can fit a trap and still be partly true [Shreevastava-Foltz-2021].

## When to use

- After the client can reliably catch and write down automatic thoughts.
- When thought records show the same reasoning pattern again and again.
- As a quick self-help cue between sessions ("Is this fortune telling?").
- Use lightly with clients who feel criticized by the idea that their thinking is "wrong", and adapt for cultural values (see Guidance).

## How it works

Burns popularized a list of 10 distortions [Burns-1980], and later lists split or merge them; for example, mind reading and fortune telling are sometimes grouped as jumping to conclusions [Shreevastava-Foltz-2021]. Spotting a familiar pattern gives distance from the thought and points to a matching question. Patterns that recur across many records can also point to the intermediate and core beliefs underneath [Wenzel-2012].

| Pattern spotted | Testing question it suggests |
|---|---|
| All-or-nothing thinking | "Is there a middle ground? Where on a 0 to 100 scale is this?" |
| Mind reading | "What do I actually know about what they think? How could I find out?" |
| Fortune telling or catastrophizing | "What is most likely? If the worst happened, how would I cope?" |
| Should statements | "Whose rule is this? What happens if it is a preference instead of a must?" |
| Disqualifying the positive | "If a friend did this, would I count it?" |
| Emotional reasoning | "Feeling it strongly does not make it true. What does the evidence say?" |

## Steps

1. Introduce the idea with a non-personal example and a short handout of common patterns.
2. Take one of the client's own recorded thoughts and ask, "Does any pattern on this list seem to fit?" Let the client choose; offer options only if they are stuck.
3. Accept more than one label. Many thoughts fit two or three patterns.
4. Use the label as a bridge to a testing question (table above), then complete the restructuring.
5. Over weeks, tally which patterns recur and discuss what they might say about underlying rules or beliefs.

What to record: the patterns the client identifies, how often they recur, and whether labeling helped the emotion shift.

## Guidance

- If you are tempted to name the distortion for the client → ask instead, "Do you notice any pattern in how this thought is reasoning?" Because self-discovered labels are owned by the client and less likely to feel like criticism [Padesky-1993].
- If a thought fits a label but the facts support it → say so and move to problem solving. Because the skill is to reevaluate thinking when it is distorted and to solve problems when it is accurate [Padesky-1993].
- If the client says "So I'm thinking wrong again" → reframe the traps as normal mental shortcuts everyone uses under stress, not character flaws. Because logic alone rarely helps when the client experiences it as another attack; alternative thoughts need a supportive tone [Gilbert-2009].
- If a "should" reflects a cultural, religious or family value → explore what the value means to the client before treating it as a distortion. Because a value is not an error, and labeling it as one harms the alliance (see `13-context-culture-development.cultural-humility`).
- If a client from a marginalized group expects bias → ask about actual experiences before calling it mind reading or fortune telling. Because vigilance can be an accurate reading of real discrimination (see `13-context-culture-development.identity-and-minority-stress`).
- If you are designing a tool that detects distortions in text → present any label as a suggestion for the person to check, never as a finding. Because agreement on the specific distortion type is low even between human annotators [Shreevastava-Foltz-2021].

Example phrasings:
- "Some thinking habits are like well-worn paths the mind takes when stressed. Do any of these look familiar?"
- Avoid: "That's catastrophizing." Said as a verdict, it shuts down inquiry.

## Common problems

- **Labeling instead of testing.** Naming a trap can feel like the end of the job. It is only the start; always follow with a question.
- **Arguing about the right label.** It does not matter whether a thought is "labeling" or "overgeneralization". Pick one that helps and move on.
- **Overuse.** Clients who label every thought can turn the list into self-criticism. Keep the focus on a few hot thoughts.

## Evidence

Distortion lists come from clinical theory and are widely used in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) manuals [Beck-2021; Burns-1980]. No trials isolate labeling as a technique. Reliability is a real limit: in about 3,000 anonymous help-seeking posts from a public online question-and-answer dataset, two annotators agreed on the main distortion type for only 33.7 percent of about 730 double-coded posts (40 percent counting secondary labels) and for 61 percent on whether any distortion was present; machine classifiers reached a weighted F1 score no higher than 0.30 for the specific type [Shreevastava-Foltz-2021]. Treat labels as rough, overlapping guides.
