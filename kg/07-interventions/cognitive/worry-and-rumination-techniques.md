**Bottom line:** Worry (repetitive "what if" thinking about the future) and rumination (repetitive brooding about problems, causes and meanings) are processes, so arguing with each worry often adds fuel. Target the process instead: postpone worry to a set time, step back from trigger thoughts, test beliefs that worry is uncontrollable or useful, and shift rumination from abstract "why" questions to concrete "how" steps [Normann-Morina-2018; Watkins-2008].

## When to use

- Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), where worry is the core process, and depression with marked rumination.
- When thought records turn into long chains of new "what ifs".
- In chronic depression: the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends cognitive behavioral treatment that covers maintaining processes including rumination [NICE-NG222].

## How it works

Metacognitive therapy (MCT) describes a perseverative style called the cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS), kept going by positive beliefs ("Worry helps me stay prepared") and negative beliefs ("I have no control over my worry") [Normann-Morina-2018; Wells-2009]. Rumination research shows that abstract, evaluative processing ("Why am I like this?") worsens mood, while concrete, specific processing ("What happened, and what can I do next?") is more constructive [Watkins-2008]. Rumination can also become a habit cued by places, times or moods [Watkins-NolenHoeksema-2014].

## Steps

1. **Map the process.** Ask how much time the client spends worrying or ruminating, when and where it starts, and what they believe it does for them.
2. **Sort worries.** For each, ask "Is this a problem you can act on now, or a hypothetical 'what if'?" Send practical problems to problem solving.
3. **Worry postponement.** This grew out of stimulus control for worry [Borkovec-etal-1983a]. Agree on one daily worry period of about 30 minutes, at the same time and place [McGowan-Behar-2013]. When worry starts outside it, note a word or two, postpone it, and return attention to the present task. In the worry period, worry or review the notes; many items will have lost importance.
4. **Test metacognitive beliefs.** Frame postponement as an experiment: "If worry were really uncontrollable, could you postpone it?" Examine the evidence for and against "Worry keeps me safe" [Normann-Morina-2018].
5. **Detached mindfulness.** Teach the client to notice a trigger thought, step back from it, and not engage further, neither analyzing nor suppressing it [Normann-Morina-2018].
6. **Attention training.** In MCT, the attention training technique is an auditory task practicing selective, divided and switching attention [Normann-Morina-2018].
7. **Functional analysis of rumination.** With the client, compare times rumination led somewhere useful with times it did not; identify the cues, the warning signs and the payoff the client expects.
8. **Shift to concrete processing.** When brooding starts, move from "Why?" to "How?": What exactly happened? What is one small step? Practice through concreteness training exercises [Watkins-etal-2012].
9. **If-then plans.** For each common cue, plan an alternative action: "If I start replaying the meeting while driving, then I'll plan one next step."

What to record: daily minutes spent worrying or ruminating, postponement success, belief ratings for "uncontrollable" and "useful", and cues identified.

## Guidance

- If the client says "If I stop worrying, something bad will happen" → explore this as a positive belief about worry and design a small experiment of worrying less. Because positive beliefs about worry keep the process going [Normann-Morina-2018].
- If the client asks you to help them solve a hypothetical worry → ask whether answering it last time ended the worry or led to a new one. Because answering content often fuels the next "what if" (see `cognitive.pitfall-restructuring-as-reassurance`).
- If postponement fails often → treat lapses as data and shrink the task (a short delay first, lengthened over days). Because building a sense of control gradually is still evidence against uncontrollability.
- If rumination is about a real problem → shift to concrete planning rather than stopping thought. Because concrete processing can be constructive, while abstract processing is not [Watkins-2008].
- If the client reports hopelessness during rumination → ask directly about suicidal thinking (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`); if risk is imminent, follow `02-risk.crisis-response` (United States: call or text 988, or 911; United Kingdom: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans 116 123). Because depressive rumination and hopelessness can travel together.

Example phrasings:
- "Is this a worry you can do something about today, or a 'what if' for later?"
- Avoid: "Just stop thinking about it." Suppression tends to backfire (see `15-transdiagnostic-processes.thought-suppression`).

## Common problems

- Worry period turns into a second, longer worry session: keep it time-limited.
- Detached mindfulness confused with suppression: the aim is to let thoughts be, not push them away.
- Concrete processing drifting into abstract evaluation: keep questions specific and sensory.

## Evidence

In the United Kingdom, NICE recommends cognitive behavioral therapy or applied relaxation as high-intensity treatment for GAD, usually 12 to 15 weekly one-hour sessions [NICE-CG113]. Worry stimulus control beat focused worry on worry, anxiety and insomnia in 53 high worriers over two weeks [McGowan-Behar-2013]. A meta-analysis of 25 MCT studies (15 randomized) found Hedges' g = 2.06 versus waitlist and 0.69 versus cognitive behavioral or behavioral treatments at post-treatment, 0.37 at follow-up; the authors call the advantage preliminary [Normann-Morina-2018]. Rumination-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (RFCBT) improved residual depression when added to usual care in 42 patients [Watkins-etal-2011] and beat group cognitive behavioral therapy at post-treatment in 131 outpatients (Cohen's d = 0.38), with no difference at six months [Hvenegaard-etal-2020]. In 121 adults with major depression, concreteness training self-help beat usual care but not relaxation training on symptoms, though it reduced rumination more [Watkins-etal-2012]. Evidence strength: small randomized trials for MCT and RFCBT; emerging for worry postponement.
