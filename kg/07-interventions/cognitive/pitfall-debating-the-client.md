**Bottom line:** Questioning that aims to change the client's mind looks Socratic but works like a debate: a quick series of leading questions, then "So how can you still think that?" Clients often concede without believing, and change does not last [Padesky-1993]. Guided discovery uses genuine curiosity so the client reaches and owns the conclusion [Kazantzis-etal-2014].

## What it looks like

- The therapist asks closed questions in a line ("Did you help your kids this week? Was that important? Would a complete failure do that?") and ends with a triumphant summary [Padesky-1993].
- The client replies "I guess not" or "I suppose so" in a flat voice.
- Emotion ratings barely move, or the same belief returns next session.
- The client becomes quieter, defends the thought harder, or stops bringing thoughts at all.
- The therapist is talking more than the client during restructuring.

## Why it happens

- The therapist sees the flaw in the thought and wants to relieve distress quickly.
- Published examples often show a "one, two, three, aha" style where the therapist knows the answer in advance [Padesky-1993].
- Pressure of time or of a protocol.
- Confusing the goal: cognitive therapy aims to teach a process for evaluating thoughts, not to win a single point [Padesky-1993].
- Direct challenge produces reaction rather than reflection, so the harder the therapist pushes, the harder the client defends [Kazantzis-etal-2014].

## The fix

- If you notice you know where the questions are heading → switch to a question you do not know the answer to: "What makes this feel so true for you?" Because genuine curiosity is the best guard against persuasion [Kazantzis-etal-2014].
- If the client agrees too quickly → ask, "How much do you believe that now, 0 to 100?" and "What part of you still holds onto the old thought?" Because a belief rating exposes surface agreement.
- If questions are piling up → stop and summarize, then ask a synthesizing question: "What do you make of all this?" Because summaries and synthesizing questions let the client put the information together [Padesky-1993].
- If the client becomes defensive → name it and repair: "I think I was pushing my view. Let's go back to how you see it." Because naming and repairing a strain keeps the work collaborative (see `alliance-and-stance.rupture-and-repair`).
- If belief does not move after careful discovery → design an experiment rather than arguing further. Because experience often persuades where words do not.

Example phrasings:
- "Help me understand what makes this thought feel so true."
- "We've looked at quite a few things. What stands out to you?"
- Avoid: "So surely you can see that's not accurate?"

## Sources note

The contrast between changing minds and guiding discovery comes from Padesky's keynote address and an expert panel [Padesky-1993; Kazantzis-etal-2014]. Process research supports the stance: therapist Socratic questioning predicted next-session symptom improvement in cognitive therapy for depression [Braun-etal-2015], and clients' independent use of cognitive therapy skills predicted lower relapse [Strunk-etal-2007]. Whether a more open style improves outcomes over a more directive one has not been tested directly [Kazantzis-etal-2014].
