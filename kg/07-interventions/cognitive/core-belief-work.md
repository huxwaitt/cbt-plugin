**Bottom line:** Core beliefs are absolute, global beliefs about the self, others or the world ("I'm unlovable"). They are kept going because people notice and remember fitting evidence and discount the rest, so evidence alone rarely shifts them [Padesky-1994]. Effective work names a new belief in the client's words and then builds it slowly with a positive data log and continuum ratings over months, adding the historical test and experiential methods [Padesky-1994; Wenzel-2012].

## When to use

- After the client has mastered identifying and testing automatic thoughts [Padesky-1994].
- In chronic depression, long-standing anxiety, personality difficulties and relationship patterns where the same belief drives many situations.
- When situational restructuring helps but the same thoughts keep returning [Wenzel-2012].
- Not in the first sessions for most clients: belief work can feel threatening before skills and trust are built [Wenzel-2012].

## How it works

Clients with problems of shorter duration often have an alternative belief ("I'm OK") that returns once evidence is reviewed. People with lifelong problems may have no alternative belief available, so they say "I see the evidence, but I'm still bad." Treatment therefore weakens the old belief and constructs a new one at the same time [Padesky-1994].

## Steps

1. **Name both beliefs.** Identify the old belief (downward arrow) and ask, "How would you like it to be?" Write the new belief in the client's own words, as a positive absolute ("I'm likable"), not a hedge ("I'm likable sometimes") that the old belief can absorb as an exception [Padesky-1994].
2. **Continuum on the new belief.** Use a 0 to 100 scale for the new belief only (0 to 100 percent lovable) rather than a scale from unlovable to lovable. Moving from 0 to 5 percent lovable meant more to one client than moving from 100 to 80 percent unlovable [Padesky-1994].
3. **Criteria continua.** Ask what the belief means in concrete terms ("A normal person has friends, holds a job..."). Define each criterion's endpoints as perfect presence (100) and total absence (0), then have the client rate themselves on each. Most clients rate themselves mid-range on criteria while rating 0 globally, which reveals the double standard keeping the belief going [Padesky-1994].
4. **Positive data log.** Explain how beliefs filter information, using prejudice as a metaphor. Ask the client to record daily any small observation consistent with the new belief. Add a confidence rating (for example 20 percent) when they doubt an entry. Watch for loggable data in every session [Padesky-1994].
5. **Historical test.** This method, from Young, reviews a lifetime of data. For each life period, starting at ages 0 to 2 (few clients judge themselves harshly as infants), list evidence that supports and does not support the old belief, then write a summary for that period. Gather data from reading, relatives and memories [Padesky-1994].
6. **Core belief worksheet.** Weekly, rate old and new beliefs 0 to 100, record evidence for the new belief, and reframe evidence that seemed to support the old one [Padesky-1994]. Client-facing versions of these worksheets appear in Mind Over Mood [Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
7. **Other methods.** Define what the belief means with a pie chart; weigh advantages and disadvantages of old and new beliefs; act "as if" the new belief were true and observe the effects; ask trusted people for feedback [Wenzel-2012].
8. **Experiential work.** Role-play or imagery to revisit an early scene where the belief formed, with the adult self responding [Padesky-1994; Beck-2021]. See `cognitive.imagery-and-imagery-rescripting`.

What to record: both beliefs verbatim, weekly continuum ratings (most and least believed that week), log entries and categories, and historical test summaries.

## Guidance

- If the client logs nothing ("There's just nothing") → find small data in session and ask them to write it down now. Because early entries almost always come from therapist vigilance until the new belief starts to form [Padesky-1994].
- If the client discounts small positives → ask playfully whether they also ignore small negatives. Because humor can expose the unequal scoring rules that keep the belief going [Padesky-1994].
- If data are too narrow (only "helped others") → help the client add new categories of evidence. Because more categories let the client find evidence daily [Padesky-1994].
- If the client is easily overwhelmed or dissociates → favor the continuum and historical test over role-play. Because these let you pace emotion more easily [Padesky-1994].
- If evidence genuinely confirms shortfalls in some areas → move to graded tasks and problem solving in those areas. Because some beliefs reflect real gaps that action can change [Wenzel-2012].
- If distress rises between sessions after belief work → agree a written coping plan before ending each belief-focused session, and check for hopelessness or suicidal thinking (see `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`). Because naming core beliefs is painful and symptoms can rise temporarily while the belief is only partly shifted [Wenzel-2012].

## Common problems

- **Expecting fast change.** Typical course: a new belief rated 0 percent rises to 5 to 10 percent after 4 to 8 weeks of logging and to 20 to 40 percent over the next month or two; logs often need to be kept at least six months [Padesky-1994].
- **Hedged new beliefs.** These get filed as exceptions; keep the new belief clear and positive.
- **Doing it alone.** Most clients need in-session support before they can see data on their own.

## Evidence

These methods rest on clinical case series and expert consensus [Padesky-1994; Beck-2021]. Research has rarely tested whether core belief work adds to outcomes beyond work with situational thoughts and behavior [Wenzel-2012]. Evidence strength: expert consensus.
