**Bottom line:** Many distressing cognitions are images, and imagery evokes stronger emotion than verbal thought, so ask about images directly [Holmes-Mathews-2010]. Imagery rescripting (ImRs) has the client re-enter a distressing image or memory and change what happens in it so that its meaning changes. A meta-analysis of 19 trials found large effects, mostly from pre-post comparisons [Morina-etal-2017]. It needs specific training, supervision, careful preparation and attention to safety.

## When to use

- Recurrent intrusive images in social anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression and body image concerns.
- When verbal restructuring changes belief "in the head" but not "in the gut".
- When a present image links back to an early memory with the same felt meaning.
- Nightmares: imagery rehearsal therapy (IRT), a related method, rewrites the nightmare script and rehearses it while awake [Krakow-etal-2001].

## How it works

Images act on emotion systems much as perception does and can call up emotional memories [Holmes-Mathews-2010]. Early unpleasant memories may create negative self-images that are reactivated in later situations and never updated by later, more benign experience. Rescripting introduces new information into the memory itself during a planned reliving, often with a compassionate perspective [Wild-etal-2008]. Imagery methods are now part of mainstream cognitive therapy manuals, including restructuring the meaning of early memories [Beck-2021].

## Steps

The timings below come from a pilot study of 11 adults with social phobia, delivered by clinical psychologists trained in rescripting; use it only with such training or supervision [Wild-etal-2008].

Assessment (about 30 minutes):
1. Ask whether the client gets spontaneous images when distressed. With eyes closed, have them describe the image, the worst part, and what it means about them.
2. Rate distress and vividness (0 to 100) and how often it occurred in the past week.
3. Ask, "When did you first feel the way this image makes you feel?" and explore that memory.
4. Summarize the meaning in one or two sentences, the encapsulated belief (for example "I'm an outsider and always will be").

Preparation: discuss the memory's meaning first. Consider other explanations, recall disconfirming experiences, and separate what happened then from adult life now. In the pilot this took about 45 minutes.

Rescripting in three stages, adapted from Arntz and Weertman, about 30 to 45 minutes:
1. The client relives the memory at the age it happened, in the present tense.
2. The client relives it as their current adult self, watching the younger self and stepping in however they wish, bringing the new perspective.
3. The client relives it from the younger self's viewpoint with the adult self present and intervening. Ask the younger self, "What else do you need?" and build that in. Many ask for comfort and compassion.

Close by re-rating the image, memory and belief, and plan a recording or written summary to review between sessions.

What to record: the image, memory and encapsulated belief; ratings before and after; who intervened and how; the client's reactions; any dissociation or risk.

## Guidance

- If the client says "I just see a blur" → reassure them that images are often fleeting and partial, and ask about any sense, sound or body feeling. Because vividness is not required, and other senses can carry the meaning [Holmes-Mathews-2010].
- If the adult self cannot think how to intervene → let the therapist or another helper enter the image first, then hand over. Because the aim is a new meaning, and support can be modeled before the client takes over (expert consensus; versions of rescripting differ in who intervenes [Holmes-etal-2007]).
- If the client dissociates or becomes overwhelmed → stop, ground them in the room, and switch to methods where emotion is easier to pace, such as the continuum or historical test. Because high emotion in early-memory work can overwhelm clients prone to dissociation [Padesky-1994].
- If the memory involves current danger, such as ongoing abuse → address safety and safeguarding first (see `02-risk.safeguarding`). Because rescripting is for past events, not a substitute for protection.
- If the client worries that rescripting changes "what really happened" → explain that the facts stay known; what changes is the meaning and the emotional charge. Because accurate expectations protect consent.

Example phrasings:
- "When you feel this way in a meeting, is there a picture in your mind? What does it look like from where you are?"
- "Now, as the adult you are today, step into that room. What do you want to do or say?"
- Avoid: "Just imagine it never happened." Rescripting changes meaning, not facts.

## Common problems

- **Skipping preparation.** Rescripting without first discussing the meaning can leave the new perspective thin.
- **Therapist rescripts too much.** Let the client choose what happens as soon as they can.
- **Ending too fast.** Leave time to settle and re-rate.
- **Complex trauma.** Plan pacing and stabilization; see `ptsd.complex-ptsd`.

## Evidence

A meta-analysis of 19 trials (7 randomized) with 363 adults found large pre to post effects (Hedges' g = 1.22; 1.79 at follow-up) and g = 0.90 versus passive control, with an average of 4.5 sessions; most data were pre-post and randomized trials were few [Morina-etal-2017]. In 155 adults with PTSD from childhood trauma, up to 12 sessions of 90 minutes of ImRs or eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) produced similarly large gains (Cohen's d = 1.72 and 1.73), with 7.7 percent dropout [BoterhovenDeHaan-etal-2020]. In 168 women with chronic nightmares after sexual assault, three sessions of IRT reduced nightmare frequency compared with waitlist [Krakow-etal-2001]. Evidence strength: supported by randomized trials for PTSD and nightmares; emerging for other conditions.
