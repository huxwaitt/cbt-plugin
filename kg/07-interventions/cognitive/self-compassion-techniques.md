**Bottom line:** Some clients learn to generate balanced thoughts yet still feel no better, saying in effect "I understand the logic but it doesn't help" [Gilbert-2009]. Compassion-focused methods, drawn mainly from compassion-focused therapy (CFT), train a warm, supportive inner stance so that alternative thoughts are felt as kind and helpful, not just correct. Meta-analytic evidence is promising but based mostly on small trials [Kirby-etal-2017].

## When to use

- High shame and self-criticism in depression, anxiety, eating problems, trauma and personality difficulties [Gilbert-2009].
- A head-heart gap: restructuring is accurate but does not change emotion.
- Self-attack after lapses, which blocks homework and change.
- Alongside imagery rescripting and core belief work, where a compassionate adult perspective is often what is added [Wild-etal-2008].

## How it works

CFT describes three emotion regulation systems: threat and protection; drive and resource-seeking; and contentment, soothing and safeness [Gilbert-2009]. In people with high shame and self-criticism the threat system dominates and the soothing system is hard to access. Kristin Neff describes self-compassion as three elements: self-kindness rather than self-judgment, common humanity rather than isolation, and mindfulness rather than over-identification with painful thoughts [Neff-Site].

## Steps

1. **Explore the self-critic.** Ask what the critic says, in what tone, and what it fears would happen if it stopped. Self-critical thinking often has a protective function, and clients may fear giving it up [Gilbert-2009].
2. **Psychoeducation.** Explain the three systems in plain words and that self-criticism activates threat, which makes change harder, not easier.
3. **Practice compassionate attention.** Help the client recall times they were kind to others or others were kind to them, and notice strengths and efforts [Gilbert-2009].
4. **Compassionate imagery.** Guide the client to create an ideal compassionate other: appearance, voice, facial expression. Images are usually fleeting and unclear, and non-human images (an animal, a tree) are fine [Gilbert-2009].
5. **The compassionate self.** Like method acting, the client imagines being a deeply compassionate person, with that posture, voice tone and way of thinking, and practices regularly [Gilbert-2009].
6. **Compassionate reasoning.** Rework thought-record alternatives in a warm voice: "What would a caring friend who knows you well say?" The aim is for the alternative to feel supportive, not only logical [Gilbert-2009; Neff-Site].
7. **Compassionate behavior.** Plan courageous steps, including exposure, carried out with an encouraging inner tone; praise effort rather than results [Gilbert-2009].

What to record: the critic's typical phrases and function, practice frequency, how warm the alternative felt (0 to 100), and any fear or grief reactions.

## Guidance

- If the client says "Being kind to myself is letting myself off the hook" → explore what helps a struggling child or friend improve: harsh attack or warm encouragement. Because self-compassion supports change out of care rather than out of feeling worthless [Neff-Site].
- If warmth triggers sadness or grief → slow down, normalize and validate, and stay with the client. Because for people from harsh backgrounds, early experiences of kindness can activate unresolved attachment pain [Gilbert-2009].
- If compassionate imagery feels threatening or stressful → reduce intensity, shorten exercises and work on the fear itself. Because fears of compassion are linked to self-criticism, insecure attachment and distress [Gilbert-etal-2011].
- If the client cannot form an image → suggest a non-human image or focus on voice tone instead. Because images are often vague, and the felt quality matters more than clarity [Gilbert-2009].
- If self-attack includes thoughts of self-harm or suicide → assess risk directly (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`, `02-risk.self-harm`) before continuing; if risk is imminent, follow `02-risk.crisis-response` (United States: call or text 988, or 911; United Kingdom: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans 116 123). Because severe self-criticism can coexist with suicidal thinking.

Example phrasings:
- "That critic sounds harsh. What do you think it's trying to protect you from?"
- "Read the alternative thought again, but this time in the voice of someone who really cares about you."
- Avoid: "You just need to love yourself more." It adds a new standard to fail.

## Common problems

- **Compassion mistaken for positive thinking.** It acknowledges pain and does not deny problems.
- **Therapist pushes warmth too fast.** Expect fear and grief; pace to tolerance.
- **Practice only in session.** Brief daily practice builds the skill.

## Evidence

A meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials with 1,285 participants found moderate effects of compassion-based interventions on self-compassion (d = 0.70), depression (d = 0.64), anxiety (d = 0.49) and psychological distress (d = 0.47), and noted that most studies were small [Kirby-etal-2017]. An early systematic review of 14 CFT studies, 3 of them randomized, found promising results especially for people high in self-criticism, but called for larger high-quality trials [Leaviss-Uttley-2015]. Evidence strength: emerging, supported by small randomized trials.
