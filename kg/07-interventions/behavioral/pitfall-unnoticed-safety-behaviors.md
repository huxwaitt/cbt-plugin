**Bottom line:** A safety behavior that the client credits with preventing disaster stops the disaster belief from being tested [Salkovskis-1991]. When obvious avoidance is gone but subtle or mental safety behaviors remain, exposures look complete but teach little. Ask about them directly before and after every trial.

## What it looks like

- "The presentation went fine, but I'd memorized every word."
- The client completes the supermarket exposure but holds the cart tightly, stays near the exit, and checks their phone for the time.
- An exposure in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) goes smoothly, but the client silently says a neutralizing phrase or reviews the event later.
- The client reports practicing "with my breathing" every time.
- Anxiety falls unusually fast, or belief ratings stay high after many repetitions.
- A family member, or the therapist, is answering reassurance questions between sessions.

## Why it happens

Many safety behaviors are invisible, automatic or mental, and clients may not see them as linked to fear. Some look like normal coping, such as relaxing, carrying water or rehearsing. Therapists may not ask, or may give reassurance themselves, which is a common error in exposure work [Gillihan-etal-2012]. Distraction and poorly handled mental compulsions are recognized pitfalls in exposure and response prevention [Gillihan-etal-2012].

## The fix

1. **Ask the attribution question** after every trial: "What do you think would have happened if you hadn't [done X]?" If the answer is a catastrophe, X is working as a safety behavior [Salkovskis-1991].
2. **Ask about the inside as well as the outside:** "What were you doing in your head to keep things under control?"
3. **Watch during in-session exposures** for gripping, gaze avoidance, scanning exits, holding objects, rapid speech or counting.
4. **Ask close others,** with consent, what they do to help, such as answering questions or doing tasks for the client.
5. **Classify function.** Preventive behaviors, which stop full contact with the threat, are the priority. Restorative ones, used after full contact to recover, appear not to interfere with exposure success [Goetz-etal-2016].
6. **Run a with-and-without comparison** as a behavioral experiment. Dropping safety behaviors during exposure produced larger falls in catastrophic belief in a panic experiment [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
7. **Write the dropped behaviors into each hierarchy item** so they are part of the task.
8. **Stop reassuring.** Replace it with an agreed supportive response.

Example phrasing: "You did the whole thing, which is great. Help me understand what was keeping you safe in there, even small things you might not think count."

Avoid: "Well done, see, nothing happened!" This praises completion but misses whether the client believes safety came from the behavior.

## Sources note

The concept and its maintenance role come from the cognitive account of safety-seeking behavior [Salkovskis-1991] and an experiment in panic disorder with agoraphobia [Salkovskis-etal-1999]. Therapist errors are described in expert clinical guidance [Gillihan-etal-2012]. The preventive versus restorative distinction is from a systematic review [Goetz-etal-2016]. Experimental findings on safety behaviors during exposure are mixed, and some researchers support limited, planned use; see the critical review [Blakey-Abramowitz-2016] and `behavioral.safety-behavior-reduction`.
