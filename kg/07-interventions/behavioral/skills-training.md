**Bottom line:** Skills training teaches specific behaviors the client lacks or does not use, through a fixed loop: explain, model, rehearse, give feedback, and practice in real life. Within cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) it is used for assertiveness, social skills and organization and planning. Social skills training improves functioning in schizophrenia, and CBT-based organization and planning training improves symptoms in adults with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in randomized trials [Kurtz-Mueser-2008] [Safren-etal-2010] [Solanto-etal-2010].

## When to use

- **Assertiveness:** the client cannot say no, ask for what they need, or raise a problem without passivity or aggression. Assertiveness training has a long evidence base but is now neglected as a stand-alone method, partly because larger CBT packages absorbed it [Speed-etal-2018].
- **Social skills:** conversation, making friends, job interviews or handling conflict, especially in psychosis, autism or long-term social withdrawal.
- **Organization and planning:** missed deadlines, lost items, procrastination and poor time management, especially in adult ADHD.
- **Within behavioral activation (BA):** skills training is a recognized BA component when a skill gap blocks activity [Kanter-etal-2010].

First decide whether the problem is a skill gap or a skill the client has but does not use because of anxiety, low mood or beliefs. In social anxiety, skills are often present but inhibited; treat the anxiety first (see `social-anxiety.treatment`).

## How it works

Clients learn by seeing a skill, trying it in a safe setting, getting specific feedback and practicing until it becomes routine. Real-world practice brings natural rewards, such as being heard, which keep the new behavior going.

## Steps

1. **Pick one skill** linked to a goal: "Ask my supervisor for a clear deadline."
2. **Break it down.** For an assertive request: describe the situation in neutral terms, say how it affects you, state a specific request, and say what will help both people.
3. **Model.** Show the skill in a short role-play, or watch an example together.
4. **Rehearse.** The client tries it in role-play, starting easy.
5. **Feedback.** Name one thing that worked, then one specific change. Repeat the role-play.
6. **Plan real-world practice:** when, with whom, and what counts as success (doing the skill, not getting a "yes").
7. **Review** the attempt next session and build the next step.

**Organization and planning** (adult ADHD protocols) teach one central calendar and one task list, daily review, breaking tasks into steps, prioritizing, setting up the environment to reduce distraction, and noticing thoughts that fuel procrastination [Safren-etal-2010] [Solanto-etal-2010].

## Guidance

- If the client says "Being assertive feels rude" → explore the values and cultural expectations involved, and tailor the style. Because norms about directness vary across cultures and settings (see `13-context-culture-development.cultural-humility`).
- If the role-play goes well but real life does not happen → shrink the step and plan the exact time and person. Because skills transfer through real practice, not rehearsal alone.
- If the client measures success by the other person's response → redefine success as performing the skill. Because outcomes partly depend on others and should not decide whether the client practices.
- If an assertive step could expose the client to violence or retaliation (for example, an abusive partner) → do not assign it; assess safety first and follow safeguarding procedures (see `02-risk.safeguarding`). Because skills practice must not raise risk.
- If a client with ADHD drops the planner after a week → simplify to one tool, set a fixed daily review time and link it to an existing habit. Because the system only works if it is used daily.

Example phrasing: "Let's try it once with me as your landlord. Afterward I'll tell you one thing that really worked."

## Common problems

Teaching many skills at once overloads the client. Feedback that is vague ("good job") does not shape behavior. Therapists may assume a skill deficit when the real block is fear; check before training. Skills taught only in session fade without planned practice.

## Evidence

- In 22 randomized trials (1,521 clients with schizophrenia), social skills training had a large effect on content mastery (d = 1.20), moderate effects on performance-based skills and community functioning (both d = 0.52) and negative symptoms (d = 0.40), and small effects on relapse (d = 0.23) [Kurtz-Mueser-2008]. A Cochrane review (13 trials, 975 participants) found benefits against standard care but rated the evidence very low quality [Almerie-etal-2015].
- In 86 medication-treated adults with ADHD, 12 sessions of CBT beat relaxation with educational support; clinician-rated response was 53% versus 23%, with gains kept at 12 months [Safren-etal-2010].
- In 88 adults with ADHD, a 12-week meta-cognitive therapy group targeting time management, organization and planning beat supportive group therapy (odds ratio 5.41) [Solanto-etal-2010].
