**Bottom line:** Many therapists hold negative beliefs about exposure, such as that it is harsh or risky, and those beliefs predict more cautious delivery [Deacon-etal-2013]. Cautious delivery shows up as hierarchies that never reach the core fear, exposures that allow distraction or reassurance, and treatment that turns into talk. Expert supervisors list not encouraging clients to approach their most distressing situations as a common therapist pitfall that reduces the effectiveness of exposure [Gillihan-etal-2012].

## What it looks like

- After many sessions the client is still practicing items rated 30 to 40 out of 100.
- Exposures end early when the client looks distressed, or the therapist offers a way out before the client asks.
- The therapist distracts the client with conversation, or reassures ("Nothing bad will happen, I promise").
- Homework is "try if you feel up to it."
- Sessions are spent discussing anxiety rather than doing exposure.
- The therapist avoids items that involve taboo content, such as harm or sexual obsessions, because they feel awkward.

## Why it happens

Therapists may believe exposure is cruel, will damage the alliance, or will cause dropout. A 21-item scale of therapist beliefs about exposure showed that negative beliefs were linked to more cautious delivery to a hypothetical client with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and that these beliefs fell markedly after a workshop on exposure [Deacon-etal-2013]. Therapists also feel the client's distress and want to relieve it. Lack of supervision and practice with exposure adds to the problem. Clinician delivery errors are a recognized barrier to the effectiveness of exposure and response prevention [Hezel-Simpson-2019].

## The fix

1. **Check your own beliefs.** Ask: "Do I think this client is too fragile for this? What is my evidence?" Bring it to supervision (see `14-ethics-law.competence-and-supervision`).
2. **Plan to reach the top.** Agree in advance by which session the top hierarchy items will be attempted.
3. **Model and accompany.** Do the first exposures in session, with you modeling if useful. Willingness grows when the therapist shows it can be done.
4. **Keep attention on the trigger.** Avoid filler conversation during trials [Gillihan-etal-2012].
5. **Replace reassurance with support for tolerance.** "I can't promise that, and I know it's hard. Can you stay with the not knowing a little longer?" [Gillihan-etal-2012]
6. **Use clear homework:** what, when, how long, and what will be dropped.
7. **Consider intensity when suitable.** In a case series, one session averaging 2.1 hours left 90% of 20 patients with specific phobia much improved or recovered at about 4 years [Ost-1989].
8. **Repair, don't retreat.** If the client is upset with you, acknowledge it, revisit the rationale and agree the next step together, rather than dropping exposure (see `alliance-and-stance.rupture-and-repair`).

Avoid: "Let's leave that one for now, it seems a bit much."

Instead: "That one is high on the list, and it's where the fear lives. Shall we plan a first version of it for next week?"

Collaboration still matters: pushing harder without consent is not the fix. The aim is an agreed, steady pace that actually reaches the core fear.

## Sources note

The link between therapist beliefs and cautious delivery comes from a scale development study with three samples of clinicians [Deacon-etal-2013]. The list of therapist errors comes from expert clinical guidance on exposure and response prevention for OCD [Gillihan-etal-2012]. Dropout of about 20% to 30% in exposure and response prevention is reported in [Hezel-Simpson-2019]. Evidence that cautious delivery causes worse outcomes in real clients comes mostly from expert consensus and indirect data.
