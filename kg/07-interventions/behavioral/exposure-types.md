**Bottom line:** Match the type of exposure to what the client actually fears. In vivo exposure confronts real situations and objects, imaginal exposure confronts memories, images and feared consequences, and interoceptive exposure deliberately brings on feared body sensations [Kaczkurkin-Foa-2015]. Most plans combine types, and combining in vivo with imaginal exposure gives better results in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) [Kaczkurkin-Foa-2015].

## When to use

| Feared stimulus | Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Places, objects, people, activities | In vivo | Riding an elevator; touching a public faucet without washing |
| Trauma memories | Imaginal | Recounting the memory aloud in the present tense |
| Feared consequences that cannot be staged | Imaginal | A script of the feared outcome in OCD or generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) |
| Body sensations | Interoceptive | Spinning to bring on dizziness in panic disorder |

Interoceptive exposure began in panic treatment and is being extended to other conditions, including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), social anxiety, specific phobias and physical disorders, though clinicians still underuse it [Boettcher-etal-2016].

## How it works

Each type removes avoidance of a different part of the fear: the situation, the internal image or memory, or the sensations read as danger. All work through the same learning principles (see `behavioral.exposure-principles`).

## Steps

**In vivo**
1. Pick an item from the hierarchy and name the feared outcome.
2. Do it in session first when possible, with the therapist modeling if useful, then assign repeats as homework.
3. Drop safety behaviors and vary settings over time.

**Imaginal**
1. Write or speak the scenario in the first person and present tense, with sensory detail.
2. For trauma work, prolonged exposure (PE) repeatedly revisits and recounts the memory, then processes thoughts and feelings about it. PE typically runs 8 to 15 weekly sessions of 90 minutes, and one trial found 60-minute sessions no worse [VA-NCPTSD-PE].
3. For OCD or GAD, write a script of the feared consequence and repeat it without neutralizing or reassurance [Kaczkurkin-Foa-2015].

**Interoceptive**
1. Test a set of exercises in session. One protocol used nine: hyperventilation, head shaking, head between the legs, step-ups, breath holding, body tension, spinning, breathing through a straw, and chest breathing [Lee-etal-2006].
2. After each exercise, rate discomfort, similarity to the client's attacks, and fear from 0 to 100. Keep the exercises that resemble the client's symptoms [Lee-etal-2006].
3. Practice the selected exercises repeatedly, at full intensity, without stopping early or using safety behaviors, then combine them with feared situations.

## Guidance

- If the client has heart or lung disease, a seizure disorder, is pregnant, or has another relevant medical condition → check with their medical provider before interoceptive or very physical exercises (see `12-medical-pharmacology.when-to-refer-medically`). Because induced sensations should be safe to provoke at full strength (expert consensus).
- If the client faces ongoing danger (such as current abuse) or reports suicidal intent → pause trauma-focused exposure, assess risk (see `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`) and act on it; if danger is imminent, use crisis contacts (United States (US): call or text 988, or 911; United Kingdom (UK): 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans 116 123). Because exposure targets overestimated threat, not real current danger, and safety comes first (expert consensus).
- If no exercise reproduces the client's main symptom → add situational or combined tasks. Because tested exercises reproduced some symptom clusters but none reproduced cardiorespiratory fears [Lee-etal-2006].
- If the client narrates the trauma in the past tense or skips the worst moment → gently ask for present tense and slow down at the hardest point. Because avoidance inside the narrative blocks the corrective learning.
- If an in vivo item cannot be arranged → use imaginal or virtual versions as a bridge, then plan a real-world test. Because virtual reality exposure was linked to lower efficacy in panic disorder trials and is still preliminary in OCD [Pompoli-etal-2018] [Hezel-Simpson-2019].

## Common problems

Stopping an interoceptive exercise at the first sign of sensation teaches that sensations must be escaped. Imaginal scripts that hint at the feared outcome but never state it do not test it. In vivo practice done only with a companion can turn the companion into a safety signal. Benzodiazepines were treated as interfering with exposure in at least one trial protocol [Lee-etal-2006]; discuss medication with the prescriber rather than changing it yourself.

## Evidence

- Specific phobia: exposure-based treatments had large effects in a meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials. In vivo exposure beat other modes after treatment but not at follow-up [Wolitzky-Taylor-etal-2008].
- Panic disorder: a component network meta-analysis (72 studies, 4,064 participants) linked interoceptive exposure and face-to-face delivery to better efficacy and acceptability [Pompoli-etal-2018].
- PTSD: across 13 randomized trials (675 participants), PE had a large effect against controls (Hedges' g = 1.08) and did not differ from other active trauma treatments [Powers-etal-2010].
- OCD: outcomes were best when exposure was therapist-guided, response prevention was complete, and in vivo and imaginal exposure were combined [Kaczkurkin-Foa-2015].
