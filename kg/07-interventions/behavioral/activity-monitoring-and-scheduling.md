**Bottom line:** An activity diary shows what the client actually does and how each activity links to mood, mastery and pleasure. Read it together to find patterns, then turn those patterns into a short, specific, graded schedule. Monitoring and scheduling appear in every behavioral activation (BA) protocol, and activity scheduling alone has a large effect on depression in randomized trials [Kanter-etal-2010] [Cuijpers-etal-2007].

## When to use

- At the start of BA or cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for depression, to get a baseline before planning change.
- When the client says "I do nothing all day" or "nothing helps," since the diary usually shows more variation than the client expects [Beck-2021].
- Whenever mood seems disconnected from what the client does, or to check whether a new schedule is working.

## Steps

1. **Explain the purpose.** "For one week, let's collect information on what you do and how you feel. We're not judging; we're looking for patterns."
2. **Choose the format.** Hourly blocks on a paper grid, a phone note, or an app. If hourly recording is too much, use morning, afternoon and evening blocks. For clients with literacy difficulties, use a simplified form; the revised brief BA manual includes one [Lejuez-etal-2011].
3. **Agree ratings.** Rate mood 0 to 10, and rate mastery (sense of achievement) and pleasure 0 to 10 for each activity [Beck-2021]. Practice one entry in session using the last hour.
4. **Plan recall.** Fill entries soon after each block, not at night from memory. Agree a time and a reminder.
5. **Review together.** Ask the client to lead: "What do you notice?" Look for the lowest and highest mood times, activities linked to better mood, long inactive stretches, avoided tasks, time spent in bed or ruminating, and missing areas such as social contact or exercise [Martell-etal-2022].
6. **Select targets.** Pick two or three activities: some that already lift mood, some that give mastery, and some that serve a stated value.
7. **Schedule specifically.** Write what, when, where and with whom into the week. Break big tasks into a first step that takes minutes [Martell-etal-2022].
8. **Plan for barriers.** "If it is raining, then I'll walk in the mall instead."
9. **Keep monitoring.** The same diary now records planned versus done, plus ratings, and feeds the next session.

## What to record

- The diary itself, or a photo of it, kept with the session notes.
- Patterns identified by the client, in their words.
- The scheduled activities, with day and time, and the value each serves.
- Completion rate and mood, mastery and pleasure ratings for planned activities across weeks.
- Barriers met and the plan agreed for each.

## Guidance

- If the client returns a blank or partial diary → review what got in the way and fill in yesterday together in session. Because recovering one day in session keeps the task alive and shows how to do it [Beck-2021].
- If the client rates everything 0 → ask for finer distinctions ("Was any hour slightly less bad?") and note the activity. Because small differences point to activities worth building on [Beck-2021].
- If the client minimizes achievements ("anyone could do that") → rate mastery against how hard the task is for them now, while depressed. Because depressed clients discount effort and need credit for small steps [Beck-2021].
- If the schedule is ambitious → cut it to two or three activities and grade up. Because early success builds momentum and failure feeds self-criticism [Martell-etal-2022].
- If an activity never happens → break it into a smaller first step or change the time. Because task size and timing are the most common barriers [Martell-etal-2022].

## Common problems

Diaries filled from memory at the end of the week are less useful; recall drifts toward the current mood. Some clients find hourly recording burdensome or shaming; use coarser blocks and praise any data. Over-scheduling a whole day leaves no room for rest and can feel like a test. Clients who work shifts or care for others need a schedule that fits the real week. Do not let the diary replace a functional analysis: knowing what someone did is not the same as knowing what the behavior does for them (see `behavioral.pitfall-activity-without-function`). In a meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials (780 participants), activity scheduling had a pooled effect of 0.87 against control and did not differ from cognitive therapy (0.02 across 10 comparisons), so this simple procedure deserves careful delivery [Cuijpers-etal-2007].
