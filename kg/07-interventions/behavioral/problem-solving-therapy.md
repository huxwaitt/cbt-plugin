**Bottom line:** Problem-solving therapy (PST) teaches clients a constructive attitude toward problems plus a repeatable sequence: define the problem, generate options, decide, act, and review. It is supported by many randomized trials for depression, with effects similar to other psychotherapies, and works best when it includes the problem-orientation training and all four core skills [Cuijpers-etal-2018] [Bell-DZurilla-2009]. It is brief and can be delivered by trained nurses and primary care clinicians [Mynors-Wallis-etal-2000].

## When to use

- Depression or stress linked to real, current problems: money, housing, work, caregiving, relationships, health management.
- Clients who avoid problems, act impulsively, or go round in circles without deciding.
- Primary care and integrated care, where short sessions are the norm. One primary care model uses 6 to 10 sessions, with a first visit of about one hour and later visits of about 30 minutes [AIMS-PST].
- Older adults with depression and executive difficulties: a modified 12-week PST beat supportive therapy at week 12, with response of 56.7% versus 34.0% [Arean-etal-2010].
- As written guided self-help: United Kingdom (UK) guidance on depression from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) lists problem-solving among guided self-help materials [NICE-NG222].

## How it works

Problem solving has two parts. Problem orientation is the client's attitude: whether problems are seen as normal and solvable, or as threats to avoid. Problem-solving skills are the steps for working a problem through. Component analyses found larger effects when treatment included training in a positive orientation, all four major skills, and the full package [Bell-DZurilla-2009]. Problem-orientation training and homework also moderated effects in a second meta-analysis [Malouff-etal-2007].

## Steps

1. **Orientation.** Normalize problems and link symptoms to them: "Low mood and unsolved problems feed each other. Working on the problems is one way in."
2. **Problem list.** List current problems and pick one that matters and is workable.
3. **Define it.** Gather facts, separate facts from assumptions, and state the problem as a gap between what is and what the client wants. Set a realistic goal.
4. **Generate options.** Brainstorm without judging. Aim for quantity, including odd ideas.
5. **Decide.** For each option, consider likely results, pros and cons for self and others, and feasibility. Choose one or a combination.
6. **Act.** Plan concrete steps: who, what, when, and what could get in the way.
7. **Review.** Next session, check results against the goal. Praise effort. If it did not work, return to an earlier step.
8. **Generalize.** Have the client lead the steps on a new problem so the skill becomes theirs.

## Guidance

- If the client says "There's nothing I can do" → ask "What would be one small part of this that is within your reach this week?" Because a negative orientation blocks the skills, and orientation training improves outcomes [Bell-DZurilla-2009].
- If the client jumps straight to a solution → slow down and define the problem first. Because a clear definition makes options better and goals checkable.
- If brainstorming dries up → offer "What would a friend suggest?" or add one silly option. Because suspending judgment widens the range.
- If the chosen solution fails → review without blame and pick the next option. Because review and repetition are part of the method, not a sign of failure.
- If the "problem" is a hypothetical worry ("What if I get cancer someday?") → separate solvable current problems from uncontrollable what-ifs and route the latter to worry techniques. Because PST needs a problem that can be acted on now.
- If the problem involves suicidal thoughts, abuse or another crisis → step out of PST and follow risk and safeguarding procedures (see `02-risk.crisis-response` and `02-risk.safeguarding`); if danger is imminent, use crisis contacts (United States (US): call or text 988, or 911; UK: 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans 116 123). Because safety takes priority over skills practice.

## Common problems

Clients pick global problems ("my life") that cannot be worked; narrow to one part. Therapists solve the problem for the client, which helps once but teaches nothing; ask, do not tell. Real-world barriers such as poverty can make every option look poor; acknowledge the constraint and target the parts that can move.

## Evidence

- A meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials (3,530 patients) found a large pooled effect against control (g = 0.79) but high heterogeneity. The 9 low-risk-of-bias trials showed g = 0.34, comparable to other psychotherapies [Cuijpers-etal-2018].
- Across 31 studies of mental and physical health problems (2,895 participants), PST beat no treatment (d = 1.37) and treatment as usual or attention placebo (d = 0.54), but not other established treatments (d = 0.22) [Malouff-etal-2007].
- Across 21 samples, PST for depression was as effective as other psychosocial treatments and medication [Bell-DZurilla-2009].
- In primary care, PST delivered by a general practitioner or a practice nurse worked equally well, and adding antidepressants did not improve on either treatment alone [Mynors-Wallis-etal-2000].
