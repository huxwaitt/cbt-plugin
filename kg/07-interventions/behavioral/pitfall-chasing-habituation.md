**Bottom line:** Making "anxiety comes down" the test of a good exposure teaches clients that anxiety is dangerous and must be reduced before they are safe. In-session fear reduction does not reliably predict outcome [Craske-etal-2008]. Set each trial up to test a feared outcome and to build tolerance of distress, and judge success by what was learned [Craske-etal-2014] [Hezel-Simpson-2019].

## What it looks like

- The therapist says "We'll stay until your Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS) rating halves" and ends trials on that rule.
- A trial where fear stayed high is recorded as a failure, even though the feared outcome never happened.
- The client says "I'll do it once I'm less anxious" or "It doesn't count because I was still scared."
- Clients slip calming strategies in to bring anxiety down faster, which turns them into safety behaviors.
- Exposures are always done in the same room, at the same time, in the same order, and fear returns in other places.

## Why it happens

Early exposure theory and many manuals used fear decline within and between sessions as a sign of processing, and many clinicians were trained that way [Benito-Walther-2015]. Watching a client in distress is uncomfortable, and a falling SUDS number gives both people a clear signal to stop. Clients also want relief, and calm feels like proof of progress. A review of extinction and exposure research found that neither how much fear falls nor how low it ends predicts therapeutic outcome [Craske-etal-2008].

The field is not unanimous. A habituation process model still treats fear decline as a useful intermediate step [Benito-Walther-2015]. Both views agree that clients should not reduce fear with anxiety-reducing behaviors during exposure.

## The fix

1. **Reframe the goal at the start.** "The aim isn't to feel calm today. It's to find out whether what you fear happens, and that you can carry the anxiety while you find out."
2. **Name and rate a prediction** before every trial (specific outcome, likelihood from 0 to 100) [Craske-etal-2014].
3. **End on learning, not on calm.** Stop when the prediction has been tested, then ask "What did you expect? What happened? What did you learn?" Re-rate the likelihood.
4. **Record both numbers** (peak SUDS and prediction likelihood), but use the prediction change to judge progress.
5. **Vary the practice** across settings, times, stimuli and order so learning transfers [Craske-etal-2014] [Craske-etal-2022].
6. **Praise staying with high fear:** "You stayed even though it was at 80. That's exactly the skill."
7. **Check for hidden calming moves** that crept in to bring numbers down.

If a trial ends with high fear because of time, close it with the learning review rather than a relaxation exercise.

Avoid: "Let's do some breathing to bring you back down before you leave."

Instead: "You're still quite anxious, and that's okay. What did you learn today that you didn't know this morning?"

## Sources note

The finding that fear reduction does not predict outcome comes from a review of laboratory extinction and clinical exposure studies [Craske-etal-2008]. The clinical strategies are drawn from inhibitory learning papers [Craske-etal-2014] [Craske-etal-2022] and their application to exposure and response prevention [Hezel-Simpson-2019]. A competing habituation process model is described in [Benito-Walther-2015]. Evidence that specific strategies such as variability improve long-term outcomes in clinical trials is still emerging.
