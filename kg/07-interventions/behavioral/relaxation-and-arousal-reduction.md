**Bottom line:** Applied relaxation is an evidence-based treatment for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), with outcomes similar to cognitive therapy in randomized trials [Ost-Breitholtz-2000] [Arntz-2003] [Siev-Chambless-2007]. For panic disorder, relaxation adds little or may reduce effectiveness, and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) with interoceptive exposure does better [Siev-Chambless-2007] [Pompoli-etal-2018]. Any calming skill can turn into a safety behavior if the client uses it to prevent a feared catastrophe, so check its function before teaching it.

## When to use

- **GAD:** applied relaxation as a full treatment or a component. United Kingdom guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) offers a choice of CBT or applied relaxation as the high-intensity psychological treatment for GAD, usually 12 to 15 weekly one-hour sessions [NICE-CG113].
- **General tension, stress, pain or sleep difficulties:** as a coping skill.
- **Panic disorder and agoraphobia:** prefer interoceptive and situational exposure. Use breathing work cautiously and only with a clear rationale (see Guidance).
- **Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD):** progressive muscle relaxation was inferior to exposure, so do not use it as the main treatment [Kaczkurkin-Foa-2015].

## How it works

Applied relaxation teaches clients to notice the earliest signs of anxiety and to relax quickly in response, so relaxation becomes a coping response used in real situations [HayesSkelton-etal-2013a] [Ost-1987]. Breathing training aims to correct over-breathing and low carbon dioxide, which can produce panic-like sensations; capnometry-assisted respiratory training normalized carbon dioxide and reduced panic severity about as much as cognitive training in one trial [Meuret-etal-2010].

## Steps

Applied relaxation (after Öst), usually over about 12 weekly sessions in trials [Ost-Breitholtz-2000] [Arntz-2003]:

1. **Self-monitoring of early signs** of anxiety: body, thoughts, situations [HayesSkelton-etal-2013a].
2. **Progressive muscle relaxation:** tense and release muscle groups, with daily practice.
3. **Release-only relaxation:** relax the same groups without tensing first.
4. **Cue-controlled relaxation:** pair slow breathing with a cue word such as "relax."
5. **Differential relaxation:** relax unneeded muscles during everyday activity.
6. **Rapid relaxation:** relax in seconds, many times a day, linked to everyday cues.
7. **Application training:** use the skill at the first sign of anxiety in real situations, including deliberately chosen anxiety-provoking ones [Ost-1987].
8. **Maintenance:** brief daily scanning and rapid relaxation.

Slow breathing: practice daily when calm, then use briefly in daily life. Teach it as a general calming skill, not as protection against fainting or losing control.

## Guidance

- If the client says "I only got through the meeting because I did my breathing" → ask "What did you think would have happened if you hadn't?" Because a skill credited with preventing catastrophe is acting as a safety behavior and can block disconfirmation [Blakey-Abramowitz-2016].
- If the client with panic disorder asks for breathing exercises to stop attacks → explain that the most effective panic treatments bring on sensations deliberately, and offer breathing only as a general skill. Because interoceptive exposure is linked to better outcomes and relaxation to lower efficacy in panic treatment [Pompoli-etal-2018].
- If a client is overwhelmed early in exposure work → a calming skill used early and then faded can be acceptable. Because judicious, temporary use of safety behaviors may help some clients approach feared situations [Rachman-etal-2008].
- If relaxation practice itself makes the client anxious → shorten the session, keep eyes open, or switch method. Because some anxious clients find bodily focus alarming at first.
- If the client has GAD and prefers a body-based approach → offer full applied relaxation with application training. Because it matched cognitive therapy in GAD trials [Siev-Chambless-2007].

## Common problems

Clients practice only when calm and never apply the skill in real situations, so it never generalizes. Relaxation becomes a ritual ("I must do my breathing or I'll panic"). Over-breathing during "deep breathing" can bring on symptoms; emphasize slow, gentle breaths. The evidence on breathing training for panic is mixed and not strong enough to settle whether it helps [Meuret-etal-2003].

## Evidence

- GAD: in 36 outpatients given 12 weekly sessions, applied relaxation and cognitive therapy both produced large gains; clinically significant improvement was 53% versus 62% after treatment and 67% versus 56% at one year [Ost-Breitholtz-2000]. In 45 patients, recovery on trait anxiety at 6 months was 53.3% for applied relaxation and 55% for cognitive therapy [Arntz-2003].
- Meta-analyses found relaxation and cognitive therapy equivalent for GAD, but cognitive therapy with interoceptive exposure better than relaxation for panic disorder without agoraphobia [Siev-Chambless-2007].
- In a component network meta-analysis of panic CBT (72 studies), muscle relaxation was linked to lower efficacy, and breathing retraining improved acceptability with small effects on efficacy [Pompoli-etal-2018].
