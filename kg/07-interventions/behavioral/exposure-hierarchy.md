**Bottom line:** An exposure hierarchy is a list of concrete feared situations, each rated for expected distress on the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS, 0 to 100) and for the likelihood of the feared outcome. It lets client and therapist agree a starting point and a direction. Use it as a map, not a staircase: it must reach the most feared items, and varying the order and conditions helps learning last [Gillihan-etal-2012] [Craske-etal-2014].

## When to use

- After assessment and formulation, once the client understands the rationale for exposure and has agreed to try it.
- For specific phobia, panic and agoraphobia, social anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), health anxiety and the in vivo part of trauma-focused work.
- Whenever exposure stalls, to check whether the list has gaps or has stopped short of the core fear.

## Steps

1. **Collect items.** Draw on the formulation, diaries and assessment. List avoided situations, triggers, and the safety behaviors used to cope. In OCD, also catalog obsessions, compulsions and feared outcomes [Hezel-Simpson-2019].
2. **Make each item concrete.** Specify place, duration, distance, company and which safety behaviors are dropped. "Ride the bus two stops, alone, without headphones" beats "use public transport."
3. **Teach SUDS.** Anchor the scale with the client's own examples: 0 for fully calm, about 50 for clearly anxious but able to function, 100 for the worst distress they can imagine. SUDS has support as a quick global rating of distress [Tanner-2012]. Names vary (distress, discomfort, disturbance), but the 0 to 100 format is the same.
4. **Rate each item.** Record expected SUDS, the specific feared outcome, and its likelihood from 0 to 100.
5. **Fill gaps.** Vary one parameter at a time (duration, proximity, crowding, time of day, alone or accompanied) to create items across the whole range.
6. **Order and pick a start.** Sort from low to high. Start with an item the client rates as clearly challenging but agrees to do. For interoceptive work, one protocol kept only exercises rated at least 30 out of 100 for similarity to the client's panic, then ranked them by fear, lowest first [Lee-etal-2006].
7. **Progress.** Repeat an item until the feared outcome has been tested and the client has learned something new, then move on. Do not wait for SUDS to fall to a fixed level (see `behavioral.exposure-principles`).
8. **Vary.** Once early items are done, mix the order, contexts and stimuli rather than always climbing one step at a time [Craske-etal-2014] [Craske-etal-2022].
9. **Reach the top.** Plan to do the highest items. Not pushing clients toward their most distressing situations is a common therapist error [Gillihan-etal-2012].

## What to record

A table with one row per item: description, expected SUDS, feared outcome, likelihood before, safety behaviors to drop, date done, peak SUDS, what happened, what was learned, likelihood after. Keep it in the notes and give the client a copy. Re-rate the whole list every few sessions; falling expected SUDS across items is a useful idiographic progress measure (see `08-measurement.idiographic-measures`).

## Guidance

- If the client rates every item 90 to 100 → break items into smaller variants and ask "Which of these two would be slightly easier?" Because comparisons are easier than absolute ratings and reveal a usable gradient.
- If the client wants to start at the bottom and stay there → agree a pace that reaches the top items by a set session. Because hierarchies that never reach the core fear leave it untested [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If the client is keen to go fast → consider intensive work. Because a single session averaging 2.1 hours led to 90% of 20 patients with specific phobia being much improved or recovered at about 4 years, although more sessions predicted somewhat better outcomes in a later meta-analysis [Ost-1989] [Wolitzky-Taylor-etal-2008].
- If an item cannot be arranged in real life → use imaginal or virtual versions and plan a real-world test later. Because imaginal work suits what cannot be staged, and a later real-world test checks that the learning carries over (see `behavioral.exposure-types`).
- If a rating sits far above the others for no clear reason → ask what the client predicts will happen. Because an outlier usually marks a different feared outcome that needs its own items.

## Common problems

Items written as categories rather than tasks cannot be scheduled. Hierarchies that leave out subtle safety behaviors make exposures look complete when they were not. Treating SUDS as a stop rule ("we stop at 20") reinstates the habituation goal. Hierarchies also go stale: as fears shift, new items appear and old ratings drop, so revise the list as you go.
