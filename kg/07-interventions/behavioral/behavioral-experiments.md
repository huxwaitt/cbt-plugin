**Bottom line:** A behavioral experiment is a planned activity that tests a specific belief or prediction through direct experience, done in or between sessions. It turns a belief into a testable prediction, gathers real-world data, and reviews what the result means. Experiments are a core cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) method, and limited evidence suggests they shift beliefs faster and more broadly than verbal methods or exposure alone [Bennett-Levy-etal-2004] [McMillan-Lee-2010] [McManus-etal-2012a].

## When to use

- A belief is rated as true "in my gut" even after the client agrees with a balanced thought.
- A prediction drives avoidance or safety behaviors: "If I stop rehearsing what to say, I'll freeze."
- The client is unsure what will happen and needs data rather than argument.
- To build and strengthen a new, more helpful belief.

## How it works

Experiments produce experiential evidence, which tends to change emotional belief more than reasoning alone. Dropping safety behaviors is itself an experiment: in panic disorder with agoraphobia, patients who dropped safety behaviors during a 15-minute exposure showed larger falls in catastrophic belief and anxiety than those who kept them [Salkovskis-etal-1999].

**Types** [Bennett-Levy-etal-2004]:
- **Active:** the client does something differently and observes the result.
- **Observational:** the client watches the therapist or others act, or gathers data, such as a survey of what other people think.
- **Hypothesis-testing:** test the old belief (A), compare old and new beliefs (A versus B), or gather evidence for a new belief (B).
- **Discovery:** open-ended, for when the client cannot say what will happen.

## Steps

1. **Name the target belief** and rate belief from 0% to 100% as a baseline.
2. **Turn it into a prediction:** what exactly will happen, how anyone would know, and how likely it feels. Name the alternative belief and rate it too.
3. **Design the test:** what, where, when, with whom, and which safety behaviors are dropped. Make it a "no-lose" design, in which any result teaches something [Bennett-Levy-etal-2004].
4. **Anticipate problems:** what might stop it, and what the client will do then.
5. **Run it,** in session first when possible (for example, a role-play or a survey drafted together).
6. **Record the outcome:** what happened, emotions, and any surprises.
7. **Debrief:** compare outcome with prediction, ask what it means for the belief, and re-rate both beliefs.
8. **Plan the next experiment** to extend or check the learning.

**Template row:** Belief (rating) | Prediction | Experiment | Safety behaviors dropped | Possible problems and plans | Outcome | What I learned | Belief re-rating.

## Guidance

- If the prediction is vague ("it'll go badly") → ask "What would we see or hear if it went badly?" Because only an observable prediction can be disconfirmed [Bennett-Levy-etal-2004].
- If the experiment "worked" but belief barely moved → ask what explained the outcome ("I was lucky," "I was careful"). Because attributing safety to a safety behavior keeps the belief intact [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- If the result seems to confirm the fear → explore it with curiosity: "What happened exactly? What does it say, and what doesn't it say?" Because no-lose designs treat every outcome as data [Bennett-Levy-etal-2004].
- If the client agrees but then does not run it → reduce the step, or run it together in session. Because experiments the client does not believe they can do become another avoidance.
- If the test could carry real risk (driving, medical decisions, legal matters) → redesign it to be safe or use observational methods. Because the purpose is learning, not risk-taking.

Example phrasing: "We've talked about whether people will notice your hands shaking. Shall we find out? What if we design a small test this week?"

Avoid: "Just go and prove the thought wrong." This sets up a pass-or-fail test rather than a shared inquiry.

## Common problems

Experiments drift into unplanned exposure when no belief is named. Therapists sometimes design an experiment the client does not own; collaborative design matters (see `alliance-and-stance.collaborative-empiricism`). Debriefs are skipped when time runs short, losing most of the learning. Some clients generalize slowly; plan several experiments across contexts.

## Evidence

A systematic review of 14 studies (644 participants) across obsessive-compulsive disorder, panic, social anxiety and specific phobia found some evidence that behavioral experiments outperform exposure alone, with methodological limits [McMillan-Lee-2010]. In 91 non-clinical participants, one session of experiments changed beliefs sooner and more broadly than thought records [McManus-etal-2012a]. Because experiments often involve facing feared stimuli, their cognitive and exposure effects are hard to separate [Kaczkurkin-Foa-2015]. Evidence strength: supported by small trials and expert consensus.
