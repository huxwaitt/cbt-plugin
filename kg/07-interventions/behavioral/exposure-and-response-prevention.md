**Bottom line:** Exposure and response prevention (ERP) asks the client to face obsessional triggers on purpose and then not perform the rituals, mental neutralizing or reassurance seeking that usually follow. It is the first-line psychological treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) and is supported by many randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [Hezel-Simpson-2019] [Ost-etal-2015]. Its most common failures come from the therapist: stopping short of the core fear, allowing distraction, and giving reassurance [Gillihan-etal-2012].

## When to use

Use ERP when obsessions trigger compulsions or other neutralizing behaviors, including washing, checking, ordering, mental reviewing, praying to undo, avoidance, and asking others for reassurance. Severity guides setting: ERP is offered as outpatient, partial hospital or residential care [Hezel-Simpson-2019]. In the United Kingdom (UK), National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance steps care by functional impairment: low-intensity cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) including ERP (up to 10 therapist hours) for mild impairment, a choice of more intensive CBT including ERP (more than 10 therapist hours) or a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) for moderate impairment, and both combined for severe impairment [NICE-CG31]. NICE has this guideline under update.

## How it works

Rituals bring short-term relief, so they are reinforced, and they stop the client from learning that the feared outcome does not happen or that distress can be tolerated. ERP blocks this loop. The aim is tolerance of distress and uncertainty, not making anxiety vanish [Hezel-Simpson-2019]. Inhibitory learning strategies, such as maximizing expectancy violation and practicing in many contexts, apply directly [Craske-etal-2014] [Hezel-Simpson-2019].

## Steps

1. **Assess and explain.** Catalog obsessions, compulsions (including mental ones), avoidance and feared outcomes. Explain the rationale with the client's own cycle [Hezel-Simpson-2019].
2. **Build the hierarchy.** Rate items on the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS, 0 to 100) and name the feared outcome for each (see `behavioral.exposure-hierarchy`).
3. **Agree response prevention rules.** Write down which rituals stop, from when, and what to do instead ("notice the urge, let it be there, carry on with the task").
4. **In vivo exposure in session.** Do the first exposures together. Keep attention on the trigger. Ask the client to stay in contact without neutralizing.
5. **Imaginal exposure** for feared consequences that cannot be staged, such as harming someone or being responsible for a fire [Hezel-Simpson-2019].
6. **Process afterward.** "What did you expect? What happened? What did you learn about the fear and about your ability to handle it?"
7. **Homework.** Daily self-directed exposures plus ritual prevention in everyday life. In one study, low completion of between-session exposures predicted worse outcome [Hezel-Simpson-2019].
8. **Climb and vary.** Move up the hierarchy and vary contexts. Reach the top items.
9. **Relapse prevention.** End with a plan for lapses and ongoing "lifestyle exposure" (see `09-ending.relapse-prevention-blueprint`).

## Guidance

- If the client asks "Do you think I locked it? Is that thought normal?" → decline warmly and name the pattern: "I notice the urge to check with me. What would it be like to sit with not knowing?" Because therapist reassurance works as a ritual and undermines ERP [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If the client chats, counts or looks away during exposure → bring attention back to the trigger. Because distraction is a common error that weakens exposure [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If exposures go well but the core fear (for example, "I am a dangerous person") is never touched → design items that test it directly, often with imaginal scripts. Because failing to address the core fear is a recognized pitfall [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If the client reports no rituals but distress drops quickly → ask about mental reviewing, praying, counting or "canceling" thoughts. Because mental compulsions are easy to miss and are handled poorly in many treatments [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If family members answer reassurance questions or do rituals for the client → involve them, with consent, in a shared plan to step back supportively (see `ocd.family-accommodation`). Because struggles with close others are a known barrier [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If the client discloses unwanted thoughts of causing harm → assess risk with your usual structured questions, without treating the content as evidence of intent (see `ocd.pitfall-misreading-intrusions-as-risk` and `02-risk.harm-to-others`), before any harm-themed exposure. Because harm scripts suit only unwanted obsessional thoughts that clash with the client's values (expert consensus).
- If ritual prevention is only partial → agree a staged plan that reaches full prevention. Because outcomes are best when response prevention is complete [Kaczkurkin-Foa-2015].

## Common problems

About 20% to 30% of clients drop out early [Hezel-Simpson-2019]. Reduce this with a strong rationale, a collaborative pace, and early wins. Clients may substitute a new ritual for a blocked one, such as hand wipes instead of washing. Some delay rituals rather than drop them, which keeps the "danger, then neutralize" link alive. Comorbid depression can sap effort; consider sequencing or combining treatment.

## Evidence

- A meta-analysis of 37 RCTs (1993 to 2014) found very large effects for CBT for OCD against waitlist (1.31) and placebo (1.33), no meaningful difference between ERP and cognitive therapy (0.07), and an advantage over antidepressant medication alone (0.55) [Ost-etal-2015].
- In an RCT of 122 adults, intensive ERP (4 weeks, then 8 weekly maintenance sessions) gave response rates of 62% (all treated) and 86% (completers), versus 42% and 48% for clomipramine and 8% and 10% for placebo; adding clomipramine to ERP did not beat ERP alone [Foa-etal-2005].
- Only about half of ERP patients reach full remission, so follow-up and booster planning matter [Hezel-Simpson-2019].
