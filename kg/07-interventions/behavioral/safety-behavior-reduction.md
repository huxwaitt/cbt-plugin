**Bottom line:** Safety behaviors are actions a client takes to prevent a feared catastrophe, such as gripping a cart to avoid fainting or rehearsing sentences to avoid sounding stupid. Because the client credits them with preventing disaster, they block the discovery that the disaster was unlikely [Salkovskis-1991]. Dropping them during exposure produced larger belief change in an experiment [Salkovskis-etal-1999], but meta-analytic evidence is mixed and some researchers argue for temporary, judicious use [Meulders-etal-2016] [Rachman-etal-2008].

## When to use

- Exposure has happened many times without lasting change.
- The client says "It was fine, but only because I..."
- Formulation of panic, social anxiety, health anxiety or generalized anxiety shows behaviors that keep threat beliefs alive.

## How it works

Threat beliefs and safety behaviors are linked. A safety behavior is used because a threat is perceived. Because it is seen as preventive, it makes spontaneous disconfirmation unlikely [Salkovskis-1991]. In panic disorder with agoraphobia, patients who dropped safety behaviors during a 15-minute exposure showed larger falls in catastrophic belief and anxiety on retest than patients who kept them [Salkovskis-etal-1999]. From the inhibitory learning view, safety signals reduce the mismatch between expectation and outcome that drives new learning [Craske-etal-2014] [Blakey-Abramowitz-2016].

## Steps

1. **Ask about the worst moment.** "When you were in the meeting and felt most anxious, what did you do to stop things getting worse?" Then: "What did you fear would happen if you had not?"
2. **Look for subtle and mental forms.** Avoiding eye contact, holding a drink to hide shaking, sitting near exits, carrying medication "just in case," checking pulse, reassurance seeking, mental rehearsal, distraction, and relaxation used to prevent collapse [Gillihan-etal-2012].
3. **Link each behavior to its feared outcome** in the formulation.
4. **Classify function.** Preventive behaviors stop full contact with the threat; restorative behaviors help the client recover after full contact. A systematic review found preventive behaviors may weaken exposure, while restorative ones did not interfere with meaningful indicators of success [Goetz-etal-2016].
5. **Test the cost.** Run a short in-session experiment: the same task with, then without, the behavior. Compare anxiety, the belief, and how the client came across [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
6. **Plan the drop.** Remove preventive behaviors first. Either drop them all at once or fade them on a schedule agreed in advance.
7. **Add them to the hierarchy** as explicit conditions ("without the water bottle").
8. **Review attributions** after each trial: "What does it tell you that you stayed without the bottle and did not faint?"

## Guidance

- If the client cannot imagine doing a task without the behavior → agree a planned, time-limited use with a date to drop it. Because judicious early use may help some clients start exposure [Rachman-etal-2008].
- If anxiety rises when a behavior is dropped → name that as expected and part of the test. Because the purpose is to learn the catastrophe does not happen, not to feel calm [Craske-etal-2014].
- If a new behavior appears when an old one is dropped → treat it the same way. Because substitution keeps the same belief protected.
- If the therapist or family provides reassurance → agree a supportive alternative response with everyone involved. Because reassurance is a safety behavior and a common therapist error in exposure [Gillihan-etal-2012].
- If a behavior is also genuinely protective (an epinephrine auto-injector for a diagnosed allergy, a medically prescribed inhaler) → do not drop it; target only the excess, such as repeated checking. Because the goal is accurate threat appraisal, not unsafe practice.

Example phrasing: "What would it mean if you went to the store without the phone in your hand and nothing bad happened?"

Avoid: "You don't need that, just leave it at home." This removes the behavior without testing the belief behind it.

## Common problems

Clients drop visible behaviors but keep mental ones, so results look complete when they are not. Therapists treat every coping action as a safety behavior; check whether it prevents disconfirmation before targeting it. Fading too slowly can keep the behavior as a hidden safety signal.

## Evidence

The idea is supported by cognitive models and experiments [Salkovskis-1991] [Salkovskis-etal-1999]. A meta-analysis of 20 studies compared each condition with baseline exposure: removing safety behaviors gave a small, non-significant benefit on self-reported fear (standardized mean difference 0.31), and adding them a small, non-significant cost (-0.13). The authors found no compelling evidence either way [Meulders-etal-2016]. A critical review from the inhibitory learning perspective found mixed results and offered clinical recommendations [Blakey-Abramowitz-2016]. Evidence strength: supported by experimental studies; the role of fading versus immediate dropping remains debated.
