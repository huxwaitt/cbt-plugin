**Bottom line:** Treat hopelessness as both a symptom to understand and a risk marker: whenever a client expresses it, ask directly about suicidal thoughts, then respond with validation and a small testable step rather than argument [Beck-etal-1985]. Treat harsh self-criticism as a pattern with a function, not a fact to debate; reflect it, get curious about it, and build a kinder inner voice [Gilbert-2009]. Reassurance alone rarely lands.

## The principle

Hopelessness is the belief that the future will not improve. In 207 patients hospitalized for suicidal ideation and followed for 5 to 10 years, 14 died by suicide, and among baseline measures only hopelessness (the scale and a pessimism item) predicted it; a Beck Hopelessness Scale score of 10 or more identified 91 percent of those who died [Beck-etal-1985]. A later meta-analysis pooling depression and hopelessness measures found the link real but weak (weighted mean odds ratio 1.33 for suicide death) [Ribeiro-etal-2018], so hopelessness cannot predict who will die. It is still a clear cue: "Nothing will ever change" means ask about suicide, not only restructure the thought.

Shame and self-criticism are transdiagnostic problems that can make it hard for people to feel relieved, reassured or safe [Gilbert-2009]. Clients often say "I know it logically, but I don't feel it": the alternative thought is understood but lacks warmth. Pre-treatment self-criticism predicts poorer psychotherapy outcome (r = -.20 across 49 studies and 3,277 patients) [Low-etal-2020], so it deserves direct attention.

## Guidance

Hopelessness
- If the client says "Nothing will help" or "What's the point?" → reflect it, then ask plainly: "Sometimes when people feel this hopeless they think about ending their life. Has that been on your mind?". Because hopelessness is a recognized suicide risk marker [Beck-etal-1985], and asking directly has not been found to increase suicidal thinking [Dazzi-etal-2014].
- If the answer is yes, or you are unsure → move to `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment` now and, where indicated, a safety plan; give crisis contacts: in the United States call, text or chat 988, available 24/7 [Lifeline-988], or 911 for immediate danger; in the UK 999 for immediate danger, National Health Service (NHS) 111 (mental health option) or Samaritans on 116 123 [NHS-Urgent-Help; Samaritans-Site]. Because the presence of suicidal wishes is one of the factors the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale manual lists for setting session priorities [Young-Beck-1980].
- If risk is screened and managed → validate the hopelessness as understandable given how long they have struggled, without agreeing the future is fixed. Because arguing for hope triggers counterargument and lowers partnership [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If the client predicts treatment will fail → treat it as a prediction to test with a small, time-limited step. Because the cognitive method treats thoughts as hypotheses to test with data [Young-Beck-1980].
- If you feel pulled into the client's despair → keep a steady, relaxed confidence. Because the therapist's confidence can partly counter initial hopelessness [Young-Beck-1980].

Self-criticism and shame
- If the client calls themselves names ("I'm pathetic") → reflect the pain and the harshness, not just the content ("You're talking to yourself really harshly right now"). Because compassion-focused therapy targets the warmth of a person's relationship with themselves, not only the content of their thoughts [Gilbert-2009].
- If you are tempted to reassure ("You're not pathetic") → instead ask what the critic is trying to achieve or protect. Because reassurance targets the content, while what is missing is a felt sense of safeness and soothing [Gilbert-2009].
- If the client can see the double standard → ask what they would say to a friend in the same situation, and how they would say it. Because the friend perspective is a standard Socratic question that brings in information outside the client's focus [Padesky-1993].
- If alternative thoughts are believed but not felt → work on the warmth and tone of the alternative, not only its logic (see `cognitive.self-compassion-techniques`). Because shame-prone clients struggle to feel reassured even when they accept the argument [Gilbert-2009].
- If shame makes disclosure hard → slow down, normalize, and thank the client for telling you. Because shame makes it hard to feel safe [Gilbert-2009], and disclosure needs a relationship that feels safe.

## Example phrasings

- "That sounds like a heavy, flat kind of hopeless. When it gets this heavy, do thoughts of not wanting to be alive come in?"
- "Given everything you've tried, it makes sense part of you expects this to fail too. Would you be willing to test that with one small step this week?"
- "If your best friend made the same mistake, what would you say to her? How would your voice sound?"
- "It seems like the critical voice thinks it's keeping you in line. What would it be afraid of if it stopped?"
- Avoid: "You have so much to live for." (dismisses the pain and can end the conversation about risk).
- Avoid: "That's just a cognitive distortion." (invalidating label).

Record: hopelessness statements in the client's words, the suicide questions asked and answers, actions taken, and the self-critical phrases and their triggers.

## Evidence

Hopelessness as a long-term suicide marker rests on a prospective cohort [Beck-etal-1985]; pooled longitudinal data show a consistent but weak association [Ribeiro-etal-2018]. Compassion-based interventions improved self-compassion (d = 0.70) and depression (d = 0.64) in a meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials, though most were small [Kirby-etal-2017]. Evidence strength: consistent but modest for hopelessness as a risk marker, which should prompt assessment rather than predict; randomized-trial support, mostly small samples, for compassion-based work; expert consensus for the in-session wording.
