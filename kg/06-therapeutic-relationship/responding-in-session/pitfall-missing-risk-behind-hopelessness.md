**Bottom line:** Hopelessness is a thought to work with and also a well-established marker of suicide risk [Beck-etal-1985], though a weak predictor on its own [Ribeiro-etal-2018]. Before you restructure it, ask directly and calmly about suicidal thoughts, act on the answer, and record it. Asking does not have to derail the session; skipping it can be the costliest error in this domain.

## What it looks like

- Client: "Honestly, I don't see the point anymore." Therapist: "Let's look at the evidence for and against that thought."
- The therapist reassures ("Things will get better, I promise") and moves on.
- The agenda continues because risk was "covered at intake".
- Notes record "client hopeless" with no record of suicide questions or answers.

## Why it happens

- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) habits: hopelessness looks like a classic automatic thought, so the restructuring reflex fires.
- Worry that asking about suicide will upset the client or "put the idea in their head", although studies have not found that asking increases suicidal thinking [Dazzi-etal-2014].
- Time pressure and a full agenda.
- Risk being treated as a one-time intake task rather than something monitored throughout treatment.

## The fix

- If the client expresses hopelessness, being a burden, or wanting to escape → ask directly about thoughts of suicide in plain words. Because hopelessness predicted eventual suicide in a long-term follow-up of patients with suicidal ideation, with high hopelessness scores identifying 91 percent of those who died [Beck-etal-1985].
- If the answer is yes or unclear → pause the agenda and follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment` and, where indicated, `02-risk.safety-planning`. Because the presence of suicidal wishes is a listed factor in setting session priorities, and an unanswered risk question outweighs the planned agenda [Young-Beck-1980].
- If there is immediate danger → do not leave the client alone, follow `02-risk.crisis-response` and your service's emergency procedure, and call 911 (United States) or 999 (UK). Because emergency services are the route when life is at risk [NHS-Urgent-Help].
- If the client is safe for now but needs crisis support → give contacts: the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline by call, text or chat (United States), or National Health Service (NHS) 111 mental health option or Samaritans on 116 123 (UK). Because these services answer 24/7 [Lifeline-988; Samaritans-Site; NHS-Urgent-Help].
- If risk is low and managed → then work with the hopeless thought, starting with validation and a small testable step. Because hopelessness is also a treatment target, and testing predictions is the core cognitive method [Young-Beck-1980].
- If hopelessness recurs in later sessions → ask again each time (see `02-risk.ongoing-risk-monitoring`). Because priorities, including the presence of suicidal wishes, are set afresh in every session, not settled once at intake [Young-Beck-1980].

Helpful: "When you say there's no point, I want to check something important. Have you been having thoughts of ending your life?" Avoid: "Let's put that thought on a thought record."

Record: the client's exact words, the questions asked and answers, actions taken, contacts given, and the plan for follow-up.

## Sources note

The risk link comes from a prospective cohort of 207 inpatients followed for 5 to 10 years [Beck-etal-1985]; later work finds it drops out of multivariate models of repeat suicide attempts once prior attempts and suicidal ideation are included [OConnor-etal-2015], and pooled longitudinal data show only a weak association [Ribeiro-etal-2018], so it is a marker to prompt assessment, not a prediction tool. The finding that asking does not raise suicidal thinking comes from a review of studies [Dazzi-etal-2014]. The priority rule is from the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) manual [Young-Beck-1980]. Crisis contacts were checked on the providers' sites.
