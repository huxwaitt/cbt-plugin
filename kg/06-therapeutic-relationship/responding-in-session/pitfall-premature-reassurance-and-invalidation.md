**Bottom line:** Reassurance ("I'm sure it'll be fine"), vague praise and quick techniques feel kind but often tell the client their reaction is wrong or too much. Validate first, check it landed, and only then move to change [Linehan-1997]. Save affirmation for specific, genuine strengths.

## What it looks like

- Client: "I made a mess of the presentation." Therapist: "I'm sure it wasn't as bad as you think." Client: "No, it really was."
- Moving to a thought record within a minute of the client starting to cry.
- Generic praise ("You're doing great!") that the motivational interviewing (MI) coding standard does not count as a real affirmation [Moyers-etal-2015].
- Telling a self-critical client "You're not a failure", followed by "I know, but I still feel it".
- Assuming every negative prediction is distorted when it may be partly accurate.

## Why it happens

- The therapist is uncomfortable with the client's pain and wants to relieve it, or their own anxiety.
- Session pressure to "get to the technique".
- Confusing validation with agreement, and fearing that validating a feeling will reinforce a distorted thought [Linehan-1997].
- For shame-prone clients, reassurance addresses the logic but not the felt lack of safety, so it bounces off [Gilbert-2009].

## The fix

- If the client has just disclosed distress → reflect and validate before anything else, and watch whether they soften. Because therapist empathy relates to outcome [Elliott-etal-2018].
- If you want to reassure → replace it with a normalizing or reflective statement ("Most people would be rattled after that"). Because normalizing is one of the validation levels and does not deny the experience [Hall-2012].
- If you want to praise → make it specific to an effort or strength the client showed. Because only specific, genuine affirmations count and are credible [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If you are unsure whether a negative prediction is distorted → stay neutral and test it. Because the client is sometimes correct, and the therapist should not assume distortion [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the client says "you don't get it" → treat it as a rupture: acknowledge, ask what you missed, and reflect the correction (see `alliance-and-stance.rupture-and-repair`). Because accepting correction is itself validating [Hall-2012].

Helpful: "That sounds humiliating, standing there while the slides froze. What was the worst part?" Avoid: "Don't worry, nobody will remember it."

## Sources note

The validation levels come from Linehan's chapter and a plain-language summary that labels them differently [Linehan-1997; Hall-2012]. The affirmation standard is from the Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) 4.2.1 manual, which states it is stricter than earlier MI definitions [Moyers-etal-2015]. Evidence that reassurance itself harms therapy outcome is indirect; this pitfall rests mainly on expert consensus.
