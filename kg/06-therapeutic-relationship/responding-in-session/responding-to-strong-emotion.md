**Bottom line:** Strong emotion in session is both useful information and a signal to change pace. If the client can still think, slow down, validate and ask what went through their mind just before the feeling surged [Young-Beck-1980]. If they cannot (flooded, enraged or detached), regulate first with short sentences, grounding or de-escalation, and return to the work only when they are back in the room.

## The principle

A mood shift in session is one of the best moments to find a "hot" automatic thought: point out the change and ask what the client was thinking just before it [Young-Beck-1980]. But learning needs enough arousal to engage and not so much that thinking shuts down. So the first decision is whether the client can still reflect.

Quick check: can the client answer a simple question, track what you say, and describe what they feel? If yes, explore. If no, regulate.

## Guidance

Tears and sadness
- If the client starts crying → pause, let silence sit, offer tissues without rushing, then reflect softly. Because therapists use silence to let clients express feelings and to convey empathy [Hill-etal-2003].
- If the client apologizes for crying → normalize it in one sentence and invite them to stay with it. Because normalizing a reaction as one most people would have is a recognized level of validation [Hall-2012].
- If the tears follow a specific moment → once calmer, ask "What was going through your mind just then?". Because in-session mood shifts are a direct route to key cognitions [Young-Beck-1980].

Panic or high anxiety
- If breathing speeds up and the client says "I can't do this" → shorten your sentences, lower your pace and voice, and guide attention to something concrete (feet on the floor, objects in the room). Because an agitated or highly aroused person may be impaired in processing verbal information, so short sentences and simple words help [Richmond-etal-2012].
- If the client has a panic model in treatment → after the peak, ask what they predicted would happen and what actually happened, and link it to their model (see `panic-and-phobias.cbt-model`). Because treating a prediction as a hypothesis and comparing it with the outcome is the core cognitive method [Young-Beck-1980].

Anger
- If the client becomes angry, including at you → stay calm, keep a respectful distance, keep your hands visible and open, speak concisely, and identify what they want. Because these are core domains of verbal de-escalation [Richmond-etal-2012].
- If you cannot agree with what they say → agree with the truth, in principle, or with the odds. Because finding honest agreement lowers escalation [Richmond-etal-2012].
- If behavior becomes threatening → state limits matter-of-factly, offer choices, and end the session or get help if safety requires. Because limit setting must be clear, reasonable and respectful, and seeking help is sometimes the best intervention [Richmond-etal-2012].
- If the anger is about something you did → treat it as a rupture and acknowledge your part. Because this starts the repair (see `alliance-and-stance.rupture-and-repair`).

Dissociation
- If the client goes blank, responds slowly, or says they feel far away or unreal → stop the content, use their name, and ground them: orient to time and place, name things they can see and hear, press feet into the floor, hold a cold or textured object. Because grounding is commonly cited as the main way to reduce dissociative episodes in session [Hammond-Brown-2025].
- If dissociation recurs → build a shared understanding of triggers and early signs, and agree a grounding plan in advance. Because a working conceptualization of dissociative episodes plus coping skills is the cognitive approach to them [Kennerley-1996].

For all strong emotion
- If you feel alarmed or defensive → notice it and steady yourself before speaking. Because a clinician with too much emotion cannot de-escalate effectively [Richmond-etal-2012].
- If distress includes hopelessness or any hint of self-harm → ask directly about suicidal thoughts, follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`, and give crisis contacts. Because the client needs a route to help between sessions: in the United States the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline takes calls, texts and chats 24/7 [Lifeline-988], and 911 is for immediate danger; in the UK, 999 is for immediate danger, and National Health Service (NHS) 111 (mental health option) or Samaritans on 116 123 give urgent support [NHS-Urgent-Help; Samaritans-Site]. If the client cannot keep themselves safe, do not end the session with them alone; follow `02-risk.crisis-response`.
- If the session is nearly over → leave time to settle and summarize; do not end at the peak. Because the aim of grounding is to restore physiological equilibrium [Hammond-Brown-2025], and the client should leave regulated.

## Example phrasings

- "Take your time. There's no rush here."
- "Something shifted just now. What went through your mind?"
- "Can you feel your feet on the floor? Tell me three things you can see in this room."
- "You're really angry, and you have good reason to be upset about the wait. I want to understand. Can we sit down and go through it?"
- Avoid: "Calm down." (commands rarely calm and can feel humiliating).
- Avoid: "Let's do a thought record on that." while the client is sobbing.

Record: the emotion and trigger, what was done (validation, grounding, de-escalation), whether the client returned to baseline, and any risk questions asked with answers.

## Evidence

The de-escalation domains are a consensus statement; the authors note that scientific studies of specific verbal techniques are few [Richmond-etal-2012]. Grounding has no operational definition and has not been tested in efficacy studies [Hammond-Brown-2025]. Evidence on silence comes from a therapist survey [Hill-etal-2003]. Evidence strength: expert consensus throughout.
