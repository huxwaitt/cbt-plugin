**Bottom line:** A reflection is a statement, not a question, that returns what the client said, either as said (simple) or with added meaning (complex). Aim for at least 40 percent complex reflections, which the motivational interviewing (MI) coding standard treats as fair, and 50 percent as good [Moyers-etal-2015]. Go one step deeper than the client's words, not three, and let the client correct you.

## The principle

Reflective listening makes the therapist's understanding visible so the client can confirm or fix it. The Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) rates a therapist who only rephrases explicit content as limited, and one who grasps the client's "internal reality" and conveys it in words and tone as excellent [Young-Beck-1980]. The Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) code makes the same split: simple reflections add little meaning; complex reflections add meaning, emphasis or link several statements [Moyers-etal-2015].

Types, from shallow to deep:

| Type | What it adds | Example (client: "I just sat in the car outside work for an hour.") |
|---|---|---|
| Repeat or rephrase (simple) | Nothing new | "You sat in the car for an hour." |
| Reflection of feeling | Names an emotion not yet named | "Going in felt like too much." |
| Reflection of meaning | Names what it means to them | "It felt like proof you can't cope anymore." |
| Continuing the paragraph | Guesses the next unspoken sentence | "And part of you wondered what they'd think when you finally walked in." |
| Double-sided | Holds both sides of ambivalence | "You want to keep this job, and walking in right now feels unbearable." |
| Amplified | Slightly overstates a position so the client can push back | "So there's no way you could go back in." |
| Summary | Links several statements | See `responding-in-session.core-response-skills` |

In MI practice, a double-sided reflection usually ends on the side that favors change, so the conversation continues from there [Moyers-etal-2015]. Amplified reflections are a tool for sustain talk and need a neutral, curious tone [Miller-Rollnick-2023]. The same words said with a sarcastic or disapproving tone are coded as a confrontation, not a reflection [Moyers-etal-2015].

## Guidance

- If you are unsure how deep to go → reflect one level beyond the client's words (feeling or meaning), not a full interpretation. Because the CTRS warns against projecting the therapist's own or theoretical attitudes onto the client [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the client uses an unusual word, image or metaphor ("I'm a ghost at home") → reflect that word back rather than translating it. Because reflecting idiosyncratic words and metaphors often intensifies affect and opens routes to core themes [Padesky-1993].
- If your reflection is wrong → accept the correction and reflect the corrected version. Because the aim is shared understanding, and checking is part of accurate reflection [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If you notice you keep ending reflections with a rising tone ("You felt sad?") → drop your voice at the end. Because the MITI codes a reflection worded as a question as a question, and a question asks the client to confirm rather than showing you understood [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If the client is ambivalent → use a double-sided reflection and end on their own reasons for change. Because this moves the conversation away from sustain talk without arguing [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If the client is highly distressed → use shorter, simpler reflections of feeling before any complex ones. Because a highly aroused person may struggle to process complex verbal information, so short, simple statements work better [Richmond-etal-2012] (see `responding-in-session.responding-to-strong-emotion`).
- If you notice yourself reflecting only the negative content → also reflect strengths and values the client mentions in passing. Because selective reflection shapes what the client says next [Moyers-etal-2015].

## Example phrasings

- "It sounds like the hardest part wasn't the argument, it was what it seemed to say about you."
- "You're exhausted, and you're still trying to hold everything together."
- "On one hand drinking is the only thing that switches your mind off, and on the other you hate how you feel the next morning."
- Avoid: "So what you're saying is that you're catastrophizing." (labels the client in jargon instead of reflecting).
- Avoid: "I hear you." on its own (claims understanding without showing it).

Record: a short quote of the reflection that most clearly landed, since it often captures the key meaning for the formulation.

## Evidence

Empathy, which reflective listening expresses, correlates with outcome at r = .28 across 82 samples [Elliott-etal-2018]. Client, observer and therapist perceptions of empathy predicted outcome better than empathic accuracy measures [Elliott-etal-2018]. The MITI 40 and 50 percent complex-reflection thresholds are expert opinion [Moyers-etal-2015], and in the first reliability study coders agreed less well on percent complex reflections than on most other codes [Moyers-etal-2016]. Evidence strength: meta-analytic correlational support for empathy; expert consensus for reflection types.
