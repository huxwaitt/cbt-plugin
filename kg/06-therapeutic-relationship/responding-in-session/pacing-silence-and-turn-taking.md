**Bottom line:** Pace the session to the client's capacity to absorb new information: not so slow that points are belabored, not so fast that topics change before the client integrates them [Young-Beck-1980]. Keep your turns short and mostly reflective, use silence on purpose rather than by default, and interrupt politely when discussion drifts or stops being productive.

## The principle

The Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) item on pacing describes two opposite errors: belaboring a point the client has already grasped or gathering far more data than needed, and switching topics too fast or intervening before enough is known [Young-Beck-1980]. The ideal is to limit peripheral discussion tactfully and pace the session as fast as is right for this client [Young-Beck-1980]. Collaboration means deciding when to talk and when to listen, when to suggest and when to wait for the client's idea [Young-Beck-1980].

Talk ratio. There is no validated target for the share of session time a cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) therapist should speak. A usable proxy is the turn level: in the motivational interviewing (MI) coding standard a "volley" is one uninterrupted stretch of therapist speech, and reflections should outnumber questions [Moyers-etal-2015]. Long therapist volleys of explanation are usually a sign of lecturing.

Silence. Therapists report using silence to encourage reflection, give the client responsibility, help them express feelings, avoid interrupting the flow and convey empathy, and report avoiding it with very disturbed clients [Hill-etal-2003]. In an automated analysis of 84 recorded MI sessions, with silences from 0.2 to 24 seconds, client emotional expression stayed largely the same as silences got longer [Soma-etal-2023]. So silence is a tool, not a technique that works by itself.

## Guidance

- If you have just asked a question → wait at least a few seconds before rephrasing or adding another. Because giving time to process and respond before adding more is a core principle of clear communication under stress [Richmond-etal-2012], and rephrasing stacks a second question on the first.
- If the client pauses while thinking or feeling → stay quiet and keep attentive. Because therapists use silence to support reflection and the expression of feelings [Hill-etal-2003].
- If the client is very distressed, confused or dissociating → do not use long silences; use short, steady statements. Because therapists avoid silence with very disturbed clients [Hill-etal-2003].
- If a silence becomes awkward or the client looks lost → reflect or ask a small question ("What's coming up as you sit with that?"). Because silence does not reliably deepen emotional expression on its own [Soma-etal-2023].
- If the client talks at length on a peripheral topic → interrupt politely with a summary and a link back to the agenda. Because the CTRS asks the therapist to politely interrupt peripheral discussions and return to the agenda item [Young-Beck-1980].
- If discussion of the central issue is going in circles → gently stop and try another angle. Because unproductive discussion should be interrupted and approached from another perspective [Young-Beck-1980].
- If you plan to interrupt during sessions → ask permission once, early ("Sometimes I may cut in to keep us on track. Is that okay?"). Because a collaborative rationale makes structure feel shared rather than imposed [Young-Beck-1980].
- If you are covering more than one or two problems → narrow the focus. Because it is usually impossible to cover more than one or two target problems in a session [Young-Beck-1980].
- If several minutes of guided discovery have passed → summarize before going on. Because summaries every few minutes let the client absorb new information [Padesky-1993].

## Example phrasings

- Polite interruption: "Can I stop you there for a moment? I want to make sure we get to the panic at work, which you said mattered most today."
- Slowing down: "We've covered a lot. Let's pause and see what stands out for you so far."
- Speeding up: "It sounds like this part is clear. Shall we move on to trying it?"
- Silence breaker: "Take your time." (then wait).
- Avoid: talking over a client who is mid-sentence to finish their point for them.
- Avoid: "Mm-hmm, mm-hmm" for ten minutes while the agenda disappears.

Record: whether agenda items were covered, items carried over, and any moment when pacing changed and why (for example, slowed because of distress).

## Evidence

The CTRS pacing standards are expert criteria. The CTRS itself was rated reliably across 1,298 community CBT sessions, but its factor structure differed between adult and child sessions, so pacing should not be read as a stand-alone skill score [Affrunti-Creed-2019]. Evidence on silence is limited to therapist surveys [Hill-etal-2003] and one automated speech analysis in MI sessions [Soma-etal-2023]. No study sets an optimal talk ratio for CBT, which is an evidence gap. Evidence strength: expert consensus.
