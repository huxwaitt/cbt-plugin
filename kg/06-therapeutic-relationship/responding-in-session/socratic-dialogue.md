**Bottom line:** Socratic questioning in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) aims to guide discovery, not to change the client's mind toward an answer the therapist already holds. Run it in four stages: informational questions, listening, summarizing, and a synthesizing question that applies what was found to the original belief [Padesky-1993]. If it feels like cross-examination, slow down, reflect and summarize.

## The principle

Padesky defines a good Socratic question as one the client has the knowledge to answer, that draws attention to relevant information outside their current focus, and that moves from concrete to abstract so the client can re-evaluate a conclusion or build a new idea [Padesky-1993]. She contrasts "changing minds" (a run of leading questions ending in "So is it really true?") with "guiding discovery", where the therapist is genuinely curious and the client owns the conclusion [Padesky-1993]. The Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) scores a therapist low who relies on debate, persuasion or lecturing, or seems to be "cross-examining" the client, and high when the client draws their own conclusions with a balance of questions and other interventions [Young-Beck-1980].

The four stages [Padesky-1993]:

| Stage | Therapist task | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Informational questions | Make the concern concrete; gather specifics the client knows | "When you say 'I'm no good', what happened that made that feel true this week?" |
| 2. Listening | Listen for unexpected answers, idiosyncratic words, images and emotion | Reflect the word "fraud" when the client uses it |
| 3. Summarizing | Summarize every few minutes; have the client write key points | "So far we have three things: ..." |
| 4. Synthesizing question | Apply the new information to the original belief | "How does all this fit with your idea that you're no good?" |

Useful question families. Most map onto the CTRS manual's list of functions of questioning (meaning, criteria for a label, consequences, alternatives, discounted evidence) [Young-Beck-1980]; reattribution is standard CBT practice:

- Meaning: "What did it mean to you that he didn't reply?"
- Evidence: "What tells you that's true? Is there anything that doesn't fit?"
- Defining terms: "What would a 'failure' actually do? Does that describe you?"
- Alternatives: "What else could explain it?"
- Consequences and utility: "What does holding this rule cost you? What does it give you?"
- Reattribution: "What other factors played a part, besides you?"
- Past experience and distancing: "Have you been in a situation like this before? What would you tell a friend?" [Padesky-1993]

## Guidance

- If you already know the "right answer" you want → ask instead what the client sees, and be ready to be surprised. Because if the therapist is never surprised, they are probably not asking interesting questions or not listening [Padesky-1993].
- If the client cannot answer a question ("I don't know what I feel") → switch to a question they can answer ("Do you notice anything in your body as we talk about this?"). Because questions the client cannot answer weaken collaboration [Padesky-1993].
- If you have asked several questions without a summary → summarize now. Because failing to summarize is one of the most common mistakes in Socratic questioning [Padesky-1993].
- If the dialogue is ending → ask the synthesizing question rather than stating the conclusion yourself. Because therapists often stop short of this final stage, and it is the client's last chance to discover something unexpected [Padesky-1993].
- If the client becomes defensive or says "I know, I know" → stop questioning, reflect the reaction and ask for feedback. Because pressure tactics make the client feel attacked, and the key test is collaborating rather than arguing [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the client lacks information they need (how anxiety affects the body) → give it briefly and then ask what they make of it. Because effective CBT therapists do not rely only on questioning; sometimes information or explanation fits better [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the belief survives the discussion → turn it into a prediction to test outside the session. Because Socratic questioning can help the client design their own assignments, such as a behavioral experiment to test a new idea [Padesky-1993].

## Example phrasings

- Helpful: "I'm curious what you notice when you look at it that way."
- Helpful: "Let me check I've got all of this. ... Looking at these together, what do you make of the idea that you always mess up?"
- Avoid: "Would a complete failure have done that? So is it really accurate to call yourself a failure?" (leading, one-two-three-aha questioning).
- Avoid: "Surely you're exaggerating." (the CTRS manual's example of high-pressure persuasion).

Record: the original belief rating, the synthesizing question, the client's conclusion in their words, and any new belief rating.

## Evidence

In 55 adults receiving cognitive therapy for depression, a one standard deviation within-patient increase in therapist Socratic questioning predicted a 1.51 point lower Beck Depression Inventory, Second Edition (BDI-II) score the next session, controlling for the therapeutic alliance [Braun-etal-2015]. In 123 clients, cognitive change mediated the link between Socratic questioning and symptom change in early sessions [Vittorio-etal-2022]. Evidence strength: within-patient process studies; no randomized trial isolates Socratic questioning, and Padesky's stages are expert guidance.
