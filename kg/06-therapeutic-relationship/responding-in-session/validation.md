**Bottom line:** Validation tells the client that their emotion, thought or action makes sense in some way, without agreeing that the conclusion is true. Marsha Linehan describes six levels, from attentive listening to radical genuineness [Linehan-1997]. Validate first, briefly and specifically, then invite change; change requests that skip validation tend to be heard as "you are wrong to feel this".

## The principle

Validation comes from dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), where it balances change strategies [Linehan-1997]. It is not praise, reassurance or agreement. The rule is "validate the valid": find the part that is understandable (the feeling, the wish, the logic given past learning) and say so, while leaving the accuracy of the belief open for later testing.

Linehan's levels, in increasing order. Labels vary across sources; the left column follows the original chapter, the right a widely used plain-language version [Linehan-1997; Hall-2012].

| Level | What the therapist does | Plain-language label | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Listens and observes with full, nonjudgmental attention | Being present | Stops writing, faces the client, waits |
| 2 | Reflects accurately what was said | Accurate reflection | "You were up all night going over it." |
| 3 | Names what has not been said (feelings, thoughts), open to correction | Mindreading | "I wonder if part of you felt humiliated." |
| 4 | Validates in terms of past learning or biology | History and biology | "After years of being criticized at home, expecting criticism makes sense." |
| 5 | Validates as reasonable or normal now | Normalizing | "Most people would feel shaken after a call like that." |
| 6 | Responds as a genuine equal, not fragile or pathologized | Radical genuineness | Treats the client as capable, speaks plainly and warmly |

Hall advises using the highest level possible [Hall-2012]; the levels are not a script to climb in order. Level 4 explains a reaction by history even when it does not fit the present; level 5 says it fits the present. Choose carefully, because saying "that makes sense given your past" can imply "but not now".

## Guidance

- If the client is crying or angry and you plan a technique → first offer one or two validating sentences and check they landed. Because emotional reactions that are heard first make collaboration on change possible, and high empathy relates to better outcome [Elliott-etal-2018].
- If the client states a distorted conclusion ("I'm a terrible mother") → validate the feeling and the caring behind it, not the verdict. Because validating the valid means not endorsing what is inaccurate [Linehan-1997].
- If you cannot honestly agree with the content → agree with the truth, in principle, or with the odds, or agree to disagree. Because these forms of agreement reduce escalation without dishonesty [Richmond-etal-2012].
- If you guess an unspoken feeling and the client corrects you → thank them and use their word. Because accepting correction is itself validating [Hall-2012].
- If the client's prediction may be accurate → say so and test it rather than assuming distortion. Because some negative predictions are partly correct, and the therapist should stay neutral about them [Young-Beck-1980].
- If you notice you use level 4 explanations often in a session → also look for level 5 or 6 responses. Because in one coding study more use of level 4 was associated with increased negative affect, while level 6 was associated with less negative and more positive affect [Carson-Wong-etal-2018].

## Example phrasings

- "Of course you're furious. You asked for help three times and nobody called back."
- "It makes sense that you'd want to cancel. You've been bracing for that meeting all week."
- "Feeling like a failure after that is understandable, and I'd like us to look at whether 'failure' is the whole picture."
- Avoid: "I'm sure it wasn't that bad." (minimizes).
- Avoid: "You're right, you did ruin everything." (validates the invalid).
- Avoid: "I totally understand." alone (claims rather than shows understanding).

Record: what was validated and the client's response (calmer, more open, or still unheard), since an unheard client signals a possible rupture.

## Evidence

In 121 DBT sessions from 35 clients, overall frequency of validation was not related to within-session emotion change, but higher-level validation was associated with more positive and less negative affect, and lower levels with less positive affect [Carson-Wong-etal-2018]. Broader empathy research links therapist understanding with outcome (r = .28) [Elliott-etal-2018]. Evidence strength: emerging and correlational; the levels themselves are expert-derived, and labels differ across sources.
