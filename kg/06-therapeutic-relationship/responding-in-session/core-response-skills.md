**Bottom line:** Almost every therapist turn can be built from four moves, known as OARS: open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries, plus information given with permission. Reflect more than you ask: the motivational interviewing (MI) coding standard treats one reflection per question as fair and two per question as good [Moyers-etal-2015]. Pick the move by what the client's last turn needs, not by habit.

## The principle

OARS comes from motivational interviewing (MI) and is the shared listening toolkit of most talking therapies, including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [Miller-Rollnick-2023]. The four moves do different jobs:

| Move | What it is | Main job |
|---|---|---|
| Open question | Invites a longer answer in the client's words ("What happened next?") | Gather the client's view and evoke their own reasons |
| Affirmation | A genuine, specific statement about a strength, effort, intention or value | Build hope and partnership |
| Reflection | A statement that returns something the client just said, sometimes with added meaning | Show understanding and steer gently |
| Summary | Several reflections gathered together | Check understanding, link themes, move on |

The Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) code defines a reflection as a statement that captures and returns something the client said; a simple reflection adds little meaning, a complex one adds meaning or emphasis [Moyers-etal-2015]. An affirmation must be specific and genuine; "You did great!" and "Way to go!" do not count, and repeating affirmations past two or three times makes them lose credibility [Moyers-etal-2015]. In the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS), good feedback includes regular capsule summaries and asking the client to correct them, because depressed clients often signal understanding out of compliance [Young-Beck-1980].

## Guidance

- If the client has just said something emotionally loaded → reflect it before asking anything new. Because accurate reflection is the core of rated empathy, and empathy ratings rise with accurate complex reflections [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If you notice you have asked three questions in a row → stop and reflect or summarize. Because a reflection to question ratio below 1:1 falls under the MITI "fair" threshold [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If you need facts for the formulation → ask one open question at a time and wait. Because good questions are ones the client has the knowledge to answer and that bring relevant information into focus [Padesky-1993].
- If you need a yes or no fact (dates, medication, risk) → a closed question is the right tool; follow it with a reflection. Because many fact-finding questions in one interview can lower the rated partnership [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If the client describes effort, persistence or a value → affirm it specifically and tie it to what they did. Because only specific, genuine affirmations are credible [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If several minutes of content have built up → summarize and ask "What did I miss?". Because therapists commonly under-summarize, and summaries let both people see whether they understand things the same way [Padesky-1993].
- If you want to give information or advice → ask permission, keep it short, then ask what the client makes of it. Because unpermitted persuasion is coded as MI non-adherent [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If the session is moving to a new topic → use a transitional summary that names what is being left and where you are going. Because summaries help clients see new information as a whole [Padesky-1993].

## Example phrasings

- Open question: "What was going through your mind when she said that?" Avoid: "Did that upset you, and was it like last time, or different?" (stacked closed questions).
- Reflection: "So the email landed as proof you'd let the team down." Avoid: "Why did you think that?" (sounds like a challenge).
- Affirmation: "You came in today even though getting out of bed felt impossible this week." Avoid: "You're amazing!" (vague praise).
- Summary: "Let me pull this together. The week started okay, the email hit hard, you stayed home and the thought 'I'm useless' got louder. Did I get that right?"
- Permission: "I have some information about how panic works. Would it help if I shared it?" Avoid: launching into a five-minute explanation.

Record in notes: the main reflections that landed (client said "yes, exactly") and any summary the client corrected, since corrections are formulation data.

## Evidence

Therapist empathy predicts outcome with a mean weighted r of .28 across 82 samples and 6,138 clients [Elliott-etal-2018]. In a meta-analysis of the MI causal model (12 primary studies), MI-consistent therapist skills were associated with more client change talk (r = .26), and client sustain talk predicted worse outcome (r = -.24) [Magill-etal-2014]. The MITI ratio and complex-reflection thresholds are expert opinion that the authors say lack normative validity data [Moyers-etal-2015]. Evidence strength: correlational process research plus expert consensus; no trial isolates OARS in CBT.
