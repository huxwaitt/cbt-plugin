**Bottom line:** Use the client's words, plain language and short sentences, and offer ideas as hypotheses the client can correct. Tone and body language decide how words land: the same reflection said with disapproval counts as a confrontation [Moyers-etal-2015]. Aim for warmth, genuineness and relaxed confidence, never condescension [Young-Beck-1980].

## The principle

The Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) asks for techniques presented in language the client can easily understand [Young-Beck-1980], for warmth and concern conveyed through content and through tone of voice and eye contact, and for questioning that never seems critical, disapproving or ridiculing [Young-Beck-1980]. It warns against "clairvoyance": volunteering a thought the client has not said, which undermines collaboration and leads to blind alleys if wrong [Young-Beck-1980]. When the therapist does suggest a pattern, it is offered as a plausible idea and the client is asked whether it "rings true" [Young-Beck-1980].

Four language rules:

1. Plain words. Say "thoughts that pop into your head" before "automatic thoughts", and "thinking traps" before "cognitive distortions", unless the client prefers technical terms.
2. Tentative framing. "I wonder if...", "It sounds like... is that right?", "One possibility is..."
3. The client's words. Keep their phrases, images and metaphors; they carry meaning and emotion [Padesky-1993].
4. Respectful address. Ask how the client prefers to be addressed; it shows they have some control from the start [Richmond-etal-2012].

## Guidance

- If you are about to use a technical term → say it in plain words first and add the term only if useful. Because CTRS standards require explanations the client can easily understand [Young-Beck-1980].
- If you have a hypothesis about a hidden thought or belief → offer it tentatively and ask whether it fits. Because the therapist should be open to the assumption not fitting and work with the client to find a better statement [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the client uses a vivid word ("I'm drowning") → use that word in your reflection. Because idiosyncratic words and metaphors are routes to emotion and core themes [Padesky-1993].
- If you notice your reflection has an edge ("So you didn't do it, again?") → rephrase neutrally. Because a reflection delivered with confrontational tone is coded as confronting [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If you are talking with someone who is distressed or agitated → use short sentences and simple words, and give time to respond. Because an agitated person may be impaired in processing verbal information, and long or complex speech adds confusion [Richmond-etal-2012].
- If you are in person → keep an open posture, hands visible, calm face, and avoid staring; make sure your body language matches your words. Because incongruent body language reads as insincere [Richmond-etal-2012].
- If the client looks lost after an explanation → ask them to put it in their own words rather than asking "Does that make sense?". Because teach-back checks understanding and was effective across most studies reviewed [Talevski-etal-2020].
- If you are tempted to sound like the expert to reassure → keep a relaxed confidence without lecturing. Because a professional, confident manner can partly counter early hopelessness [Young-Beck-1980].

Words to watch:

| Instead of | Try | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Your irrational thoughts" | "The thought that showed up" | Can sound critical or disapproving of the client's view [Young-Beck-1980] |
| "You're catastrophizing" | "Your mind jumped to the worst case" | Plain, describes rather than diagnoses |
| "Noncompliant with homework" | "The practice didn't happen this week" | Curious rather than blaming |
| "Why did you do that?" | "What led up to that?" | "Why" can sound accusatory |
| "You should" | "Would it help to...?" | Leaves the choice with the client |

## Example phrasings

- "I might be off here, so tell me if this doesn't fit. I wonder if 'useless' is the word that keeps coming back."
- "You called it 'the fog'. When the fog comes down, what happens next?"
- Avoid: "Clearly your core belief is that you're unlovable." (clairvoyance stated as fact).
- Avoid: "That's not a very helpful way to think, is it?" (sounds disapproving).

Tool designers: text has no tone of voice, so tentative phrasing, explicit checks ("Did I get that right?") and avoiding sarcasm or exclamation-heavy praise carry more weight in written replies; see `14-ethics-law.digital-and-ai-tools` for governance.

Record: preferred name and pronouns, the client's key words for their problem, and terms they dislike.

## Evidence

These are expert standards from the CTRS manual, the motivational interviewing coding manual and an emergency psychiatry consensus statement [Young-Beck-1980; Moyers-etal-2015; Richmond-etal-2012]. Teach-back has the strongest outcome evidence of the methods here, mostly from medical settings [Talevski-etal-2020]. Evidence strength: expert consensus; little direct trial evidence on wording in cognitive behavioral therapy.
