**Bottom line:** Too many questions, stacked questions and leading questions turn Socratic questioning into cross-examination. The fix is to reflect at least as often as you ask, ask one open question at a time, summarize every few minutes, and give up the "right answer" you were steering toward [Moyers-etal-2015; Padesky-1993].

## What it looks like

- Runs of three or more questions with no reflection in between.
- Two or three questions in one turn ("How did you feel, and what did you think, and did you tell anyone?").
- A chain of closed, leading questions ending in "So is it really true that...?" and a client who says "I suppose not" without conviction. Padesky calls this "one-two-three-aha" questioning to change minds [Padesky-1993].
- The client's answers get shorter; they look at the clock, agree quickly, or say "I know, I know".
- On the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS), the therapist seems to be "cross-examining", putting the client on the defensive [Young-Beck-1980].
- In a coded session, reflections are fewer than questions, below the motivational interviewing (MI) "fair" threshold of 1:1 [Moyers-etal-2015].

## Why it happens

- Generic lists of "good Socratic questions" are used as a script; Padesky found such lists useful for beginners but not enough on their own [Padesky-1993].
- The therapist already knows where the conversation should end and is building a case, so the client's individual answers stop mattering [Padesky-1993].
- Assessment habits: history-taking needs many questions, and the habit carries into therapy.
- Anxiety about silence, or about doing cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) "properly", leads to filling every gap with another question.
- Asking information-only questions gives the lowest rating on the MI empathy scale, because it shows little attention to the client's perspective [Moyers-etal-2015].

## The fix

- If you notice two questions in a row → make the next turn a reflection. Because the Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) "good" standard is two reflections per question [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If you catch yourself asking a compound question → keep only the first part and wait. Because one clear question lets the client answer fully [Padesky-1993].
- If you know the answer you want → switch to genuine curiosity, or give the information directly with permission. Because guided discovery requires being open to unexpected answers [Padesky-1993].
- If several minutes of questioning have passed → summarize and ask the client to correct it. Because not summarizing enough is one of the most common Socratic errors [Padesky-1993].
- If the client becomes defensive → stop, reflect what you see and ask for feedback. Because the test of good guided discovery is collaborating, not arguing [Young-Beck-1980].

Helpful: "You've told me a lot. Let me check I've got it before I ask anything else." Avoid: "And then what? And how did that feel? And was that the same as before?"

## Sources note

The descriptions come from the CTRS rating manual, Padesky's keynote on Socratic questioning, and the MITI 4.2.1 coding manual [Young-Beck-1980; Padesky-1993; Moyers-etal-2015]. The MITI ratio threshold is expert opinion. No study has tested question stacking directly; the evidence is from observer standards and expert consensus.
