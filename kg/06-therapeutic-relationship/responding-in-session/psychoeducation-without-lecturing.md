**Bottom line:** Teach in short exchanges, not speeches: ask what the client already knows, give one small piece tailored to them, then ask what they make of it (elicit-provide-elicit) [Moyers-etal-2015]. Build models from the client's own recent example, check understanding with teach-back, and write the key point down. Information supports cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) but is not the treatment by itself.

## The principle

The Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) expects therapists to explain the rationale for most interventions, in language the client can easily understand, and to check that the client actually understands, because depressed clients often signal agreement out of compliance [Young-Beck-1980]. Motivational interviewing (MI) adds a format for doing this without taking over: elicit, provide, elicit [Moyers-etal-2015; Miller-Rollnick-2023]. Two failure modes are common: the "information dump" after a perfunctory "Can I tell you something?", and asking permission so often that it sounds hollow; both lower rated partnership [Moyers-etal-2015].

Use questions when the client has the knowledge (Socratic questioning); give information when they do not, since asking questions a client cannot answer weakens collaboration [Padesky-1993].

## Guidance

- If you are about to explain something → first ask what the client already knows or thinks ("What have you heard about how anxiety works?"). Because eliciting first lets you skip what they know and correct what they do not [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If the client agrees to hear information → give one or two sentences, then pause. Because dumping several minutes of information after one permission lowers partnership [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If you are teaching a model (vicious cycle, panic cycle) → draw it with the client's own example from this week, filling the boxes with their words. Because techniques should be presented articulately in language the client understands, tailored to the individual [Young-Beck-1980].
- If you have finished a piece of explanation → ask the client to explain it back in their own words ("So I know I explained it clearly, how would you describe this to a friend?"). Because teach-back was effective in 19 of 20 studies in a systematic review [Talevski-etal-2020].
- If the client says "Yes, that makes sense" quickly → ask how it applies to a recent situation of theirs. Because quick agreement may be compliance rather than understanding [Young-Beck-1980].
- If you want to recommend something → state it as information and leave the decision with them ("Many people find..., you know your situation best"). Because advice without autonomy support is coded as persuasion [Moyers-etal-2015].
- If the explanation is longer than a minute or two → break it up with a question or a drawing. Because the CTRS describes a beginning, middle and end with checks, not monologue [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the client reads or listens to materials between sessions → review what they took from it next session. Because passive psychoeducation alone has only a small effect [Donker-etal-2009].

## Example phrasings

- Elicit: "What's your understanding of why avoiding things might keep the fear going?"
- Provide: "One thing we know is that avoiding brings quick relief, and the relief teaches the brain the situation really was dangerous."
- Elicit: "How does that fit with what happens for you?"
- Rationale: "The reason I'm suggesting we track your mood is so we can see the pattern together, rather than me guessing."
- Teach-back: "I want to check I explained it well. Could you walk me through the cycle using Tuesday?"
- Avoid: "Let me explain the cognitive model. There are three levels of cognition: automatic thoughts, intermediate beliefs and core beliefs..." (jargon lecture).
- Avoid: "Does that make sense?" as the only check (invites a yes).

Record: what was explained, the client's teach-back in their words, any misunderstanding corrected, and written materials given.

## Evidence

A meta-analysis of four trials of passive psychoeducation found a small reduction in depression and distress (d = 0.20, number needed to treat 9) [Donker-etal-2009]. Teach-back improved outcomes from knowledge recall to readmissions in 19 of 20 studies, mostly in medical settings [Talevski-etal-2020]. Elicit-provide-elicit is defined in the MI coding manual; its specific effect in CBT has not been tested separately [Moyers-etal-2015]. Evidence strength: small meta-analytic effect for information alone; expert consensus for format.
