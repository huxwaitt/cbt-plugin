**Bottom line:** The therapeutic alliance is the collaborative part of the therapy relationship: agreement on the goals of therapy, agreement on the tasks used to reach them, and a bond of trust that holds the work together [Bordin-1979]. Across 295 studies and more than 30,000 patients, a stronger alliance predicts better outcome (r = .28), and the same link appears in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [Fluckiger-etal-2018; Cameron-etal-2018]. Build it deliberately and check it every session; do not assume it.

## What it is

Bordin's model defines the alliance by three parts that apply to any therapy [Bordin-1979].

| Part | Meaning | CBT example |
|---|---|---|
| Goals | Client and therapist agree on what therapy is for | "Get back to work without daily panic" |
| Tasks | They agree on the activities that will get there | Thought records, exposure, homework |
| Bond | Trust and confidence that let the client do hard tasks | Client believes the therapist respects them and knows the work |

The parts depend on each other. A client who mistrusts the therapist is more likely to dispute a task, and a therapist who will not negotiate tasks or goals can leave the client feeling misunderstood or disrespected [Safran-etal-2011]. "Working alliance" and "helping alliance" are aliases [Fluckiger-etal-2018]. In CBT the alliance overlaps with collaborative empiricism, whose core is "sharing the work" of testing beliefs together [Tee-Kazantzis-2011].

## Why it matters in CBT

- Size of the link: face-to-face therapy r = .278 (about d = .58); internet-based therapy r = .275 (23 studies). It held across rater perspective, alliance and outcome measures, treatment approaches, patient characteristics and countries [Fluckiger-etal-2018].
- CBT for depression: r = .26; therapist ratings of the alliance predicted outcome less well than client or observer ratings [Cameron-etal-2018].
- Children and adolescents: r = .19 across 28 prospective studies [Karver-etal-2018].
- Dropout: clients with weaker alliances are more likely to drop out (d = .55, 11 studies) [Sharf-etal-2010].
- Goal consensus (r = .24) and collaboration (r = .29) each predict outcome [Tryon-etal-2018].
- The American Psychological Association (APA) task force rated the alliance "demonstrably effective" [Norcross-Karpiak-2025].

## Key details

- **The therapist's share matters most.** In 331 patients seen by 80 therapists, differences between therapists in alliance predicted outcome; differences between one therapist's patients did not. Therapists who form strong alliances across many clients get better results [Baldwin-etal-2007].
- **The arrow points both ways.** Pooled session-by-session data (17 studies, 5,350 participants) showed alliance and symptoms influencing each other over sessions 1 to 7, often an upward spiral [Fluckiger-etal-2020]. In CBT for depression, alliance ratings rose in sessions right after sudden symptom gains [Tang-DeRubeis-1999]. Early progress and the alliance feed each other; neither can be skipped.
- **Correlation is not proof of cause.** The evidence is mostly correlational, and the relationship works together with treatment methods and client factors, not instead of them [Norcross-Karpiak-2025]. In CBT the techniques are the tasks the alliance makes possible.
- **Clients hold back.** Clients often have negative feelings about therapy that they are reluctant to raise [Safran-etal-2011], so a smooth-seeming session is not evidence of a strong alliance.

## In the session

- If you are starting treatment → agree two or three goals in the client's words and say how each CBT task serves them ("We'll use thought records because the 3 a.m. worry spiral is what you most want to change"). Because goal consensus and collaboration predict outcome [Tryon-etal-2018].
- If the client agrees to tasks but does not do them → ask how the task fits before adding structure or persuasion. Because disagreement about tasks is a form of alliance strain and can reflect sensitivity to feeling controlled [Safran-etal-2011].
- If you believe the alliance is fine but have not asked → ask at the end of each session what helped and what did not, or use a brief client-rated alliance scale. Because therapist ratings are the weakest predictor and clients rarely volunteer complaints [Cameron-etal-2018; Safran-etal-2011].
- If early progress appears → name it and credit the client's effort. Because early gains and alliance strengthen each other [Fluckiger-etal-2020].
- If the client goes quiet, defers, or criticizes the therapy → switch to `alliance-and-stance.rupture-and-repair`. Because unrepaired strains predict dropout [Sharf-etal-2010].

Record the agreed goals, the client's own alliance rating each session, and any strain and how you addressed it.
