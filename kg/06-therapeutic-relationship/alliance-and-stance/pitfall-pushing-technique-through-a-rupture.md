**Bottom line:** When a client resists or withdraws during a technique, doing more of the technique usually makes the rupture worse. In cognitive therapy for depression, therapists who responded to alliance strains by increasing their focus on distorted cognitions appeared to deepen the strains, and that focus correlated with worse outcome [Castonguay-etal-1996]. Pause the technique, attend to the relationship, then return to the work in a form the client can accept.

## What it looks like

- The client says "I know it's irrational, but it doesn't help," and the therapist answers with another round of evidence-gathering questions.
- The client gives shorter and shorter answers while the therapist fills in the thought record for them.
- The client says "These worksheets aren't for me," and the therapist explains again why worksheets work.
- Homework stops coming back, sessions are canceled, and the therapist labels the client "resistant" or "not ready."

These are withdrawal and confrontation markers being met with more of the same [Safran-etal-2011].

## Why it happens

- **Technique feels safe.** Under interpersonal pressure, protocol is a comfortable place to retreat. Castonguay and colleagues found therapists increased adherence to cognitive rationales and techniques specifically to correct alliance problems [Castonguay-etal-1996].
- **Misreading collaborative empiricism as persuasion.** The aim is shared testing of beliefs, not winning an argument [Tee-Kazantzis-2011].
- **Rigidity and over-structuring** are recognized therapist contributions to weaker alliances [Ackerman-Hilsenroth-2001].
- **Strains go unspoken.** Clients often hide negative feelings about therapy for fear of the therapist's reaction [Safran-etal-2011], so the therapist does not realize the technique is the problem.

## The fix

- If the client pushes back or goes flat during a technique → stop, name what you notice, and ask. "I notice I've been pushing this thought record and you've gone quiet. What's it been like on your end?" Because inviting the client's view and responding non-defensively is the core of repair [Safran-etal-2011].
- If the client says the technique does not fit → change the task, not the goal. Offer a behavioral experiment instead of a written record, or validate first and test later. Because changing tasks or goals in a way meaningful to the client is an established repair, including shifting from challenging to validating [Safran-etal-2011].
- If you notice yourself wanting to win the point → treat the urge as a rupture marker in yourself. Because ratings of the therapist's contribution to ruptures predicted dropout (d = .61) [Eubanks-etal-2019].
- If the strain repeats across sessions → take a recording to supervision and plan a repair conversation. Because repair is associated with better outcomes (r = .29) [Eubanks-etal-2018].

Example repair: "You're right that I've been trying to talk you out of the thought. That's not how this is supposed to work. Can we step back and look at what the thought is protecting you from?"

## Sources note

The key evidence is one process study of 30 clients in cognitive therapy for depression [Castonguay-etal-1996], consistent with the broader rupture literature [Safran-etal-2011; Eubanks-etal-2018]. It is correlational; the recommendation to pause technique during strain rests on these findings plus expert consensus.
