**Bottom line:** Clients who expect therapy to help tend to do better (r = .18 across 81 samples and 12,722 patients), and so, more weakly, do clients who find the treatment credible early on (r = .12) [Constantino-etal-2018a; Constantino-etal-2018b]. Build hope honestly: give an explanation of the problem that the client accepts as fitting their experience, set realistic expectations, and make early progress visible. Hopelessness about the future is also a risk signal and must be assessed, not just reassured.

## The principle

Two related client beliefs matter, and each has its own meta-analysis [Constantino-etal-2018a; Constantino-etal-2018b].
- **Outcome expectation:** the client's belief about how much therapy will help them.
- **Treatment credibility:** the client's belief that this treatment is logical, suits them and is likely to work.

The contextual model explains why. Therapy offers a cogent explanation of the client's distress, and when the client accepts that explanation it creates the expectation that they can cope or solve their problems. This pathway needs an initial bond and a strong alliance to work [Wampold-2015]. Hope therefore comes from an understood, personalized rationale, not from promises. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks services to offer care "in an atmosphere of hope and optimism" [NICE-CG136].

Hope also grows from evidence of change. Early alliance and symptom improvement reinforce each other session by session [Fluckiger-etal-2020], and in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for depression, alliance improved in sessions right after sudden gains [Tang-DeRubeis-1999].

## Guidance

- If you are presenting the CBT model → build it from the client's own recent example and ask whether it fits; revise until they find it plausible. Because a rationale produces expectations only when the client accepts it [Wampold-2015], and early credibility predicts outcome [Constantino-etal-2018b].
- If the client is skeptical because past therapy failed → ask what was tried and what helped or did not, then say specifically how this plan differs. Because NICE advises reviewing positive and negative experiences of previous treatment when planning care [NICE-NG222].
- If the client asks "Will this work?" or "How long will it take?" → give an honest, evidence-based answer and promise to check progress together within a few weeks. Because congruent answers keep trust [Kolden-etal-2018], and NICE advises reviewing how well treatment is working 2 to 4 weeks after starting [NICE-NG222].
- If early gains appear → point them out with the numbers and credit the client's actions. Because early change and alliance build an upward spiral [Fluckiger-etal-2020].
- If progress stalls → say so openly and problem-solve the plan together instead of reassuring. Because feedback on poor progress helps clients predicted to do poorly [Lambert-etal-2018].
- If you want to measure expectancy → give a brief credibility and expectancy questionnaire after presenting the rationale. Because the Credibility/Expectancy Questionnaire separates the two factors reliably [Devilly-Borkovec-2000].
- If the client says things will never get better, life is pointless, or they would be better off gone → ask directly about suicidal thoughts and follow your risk procedure (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`). Give crisis contacts: in the US call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911; in the UK call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111, or Samaritans on 116 123. If risk is imminent, do not leave the person alone and arrange emergency help. Because hopelessness is an established, though on its own modest, predictor of suicidal thoughts and behavior, so it cannot be used to rule risk in or out [Ribeiro-etal-2018], and NICE advises always asking people with depression directly about suicidal ideation and intent [NICE-NG222].

## Example phrasings

- "From what you've described, the panic keeps going because you leave before the feeling peaks, so your body never learns it passes. Does that fit?"
- "I can't promise, but this approach helps many people with this problem, and we'll check every few weeks whether it's helping you."
- "Two weeks ago you rated the dread at 80. Today it's 55. What did you do differently?"
- "You've tried a lot already. What made the last therapy not work for you?"
- Avoid: "This will definitely fix it."
- Avoid: "Just try to think positively."
- Avoid: "Don't say that, of course things will get better." (dismisses hopelessness instead of assessing it)

## Evidence

- Outcome expectation: 81 samples, 12,722 patients, r = .18 (about d = .36), high heterogeneity, no evidence of publication bias [Constantino-etal-2018a].
- Early credibility: 24 samples, 1,504 patients, r = .12 (about d = .24) [Constantino-etal-2018b].
- In the questionnaire validation study, expectancy predicted some outcomes while credibility did not [Devilly-Borkovec-2000].
- Evidence is correlational; clinician strategies to raise expectations are supported mainly by indirect evidence and expert consensus.
