**Bottom line:** Measure outcome every session and the alliance at the end of every session, look at the scores with the client, and change course when progress stalls or the alliance dips. Feedback improves outcomes modestly (d = 0.15 across 58 studies) and slightly reduces dropout [DeJong-etal-2021]; its main value is catching clients who are not on track, whom therapists rarely spot by judgment alone [Hannan-etal-2005; Lambert-etal-2018].

## When to use

- With every client, from the first session. National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance for depression advises reviewing whether treatment is working 2 to 4 weeks after starting and considering routine outcome monitoring (ROM) with validated sessional measures such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [NICE-NG222]. In the UK, National Health Service (NHS) Talking Therapies (formerly Improving Access to Psychological Therapies) builds session-by-session measurement into outcome-informed supervision [Ostergard-etal-2020].
- Especially when the client is not improving, when you feel sure the client is fine without data, or when dropout risk is high.
- Feedback-informed treatment is the practice of collecting this client feedback systematically and using it in the session [Duncan-Reese-2015].

## Steps

1. **Explain it once.** "Each week I'll ask two very short questionnaires. They help me see early whether this is working, so we can change course instead of wasting your time."
2. **Outcome at the start.** Give a symptom measure for the main problem and, if you like, a general one. The Outcome Rating Scale (ORS) has four 10 cm visual analog lines (individual, interpersonal, social, overall well-being) summed to 0 to 40, and takes 1 to 3 minutes [Ostergard-etal-2020].
3. **Score and discuss in the first minutes.** Compare with last week and with the expected course. The ORS manual sets an adult clinical cutoff of 25 and a reliable change index of 5 points, and defines "not on track" as less than a 5-point gain by session 3 [Ostergard-etal-2020].
4. **Alliance at the end.** The Session Rating Scale (SRS) uses four 10 cm lines (relationship, goals, method, overall), also 0 to 40 [Ostergard-etal-2020]. Any other brief client-rated alliance scale works (`08-measurement.process-measures`).
5. **Respond to any dip or less-than-top rating** the same day: thank the client and ask what to change.
6. **If not on track or deteriorating:** review the alliance, motivation, social support and stressful life events (the areas the Outcome Questionnaire system checks for off-track clients), plus the goals, then change approach, intensity or provider, and take it to supervision [Ostergard-etal-2020].
7. **Review your caseload data** periodically for your own development (`alliance-and-stance.facilitative-interpersonal-skills`).

## What to record

- Each session's outcome and alliance scores, ideally as a graph.
- Not-on-track or deterioration flags, what you discussed, and any plan change.
- Alliance concerns raised and how they were addressed.
- If mood items worsen, the risk check you completed.

## Guidance

- If a score worsens reliably → raise it in that session and check risk. Because in one study, 48 therapists predicted only 1 of 40 clients who deteriorated, while a questionnaire algorithm identified most of them [Ostergard-etal-2020; Hannan-etal-2005].
- If you feel confident a client is improving → check the data anyway. Because 25% of clinicians rated their skill at the 90th percentile, none rated themselves below average, and clinicians underestimate deterioration [Walfish-etal-2012].
- If alliance scores are always perfect → invite small criticisms explicitly. Because clients are often reluctant to voice negative feelings about therapy [Safran-etal-2011].
- If the client declines forms → ask the same questions verbally on a 0 to 10 scale. Because assuming you know the client's view, instead of asking, is ineffective [Norcross-Karpiak-2025].
- If the client is severely unwell or treated in a psychiatric setting → keep monitoring, but frame poor-progress feedback carefully and expect smaller effects. Because effects of one feedback system were near zero in psychiatric settings, and negative feedback may demoralize severe patients [Ostergard-etal-2020].

## Common problems

- **Collecting without discussing.** Scores that never enter the conversation cannot change anything (`alliance-and-stance.pitfall-collecting-feedback-without-using-it`).
- **Mixed evidence.** A Cochrane review (12 studies, 3,696 participants) found no clear effect on symptoms (standardized mean difference -0.07), with low-quality evidence [Kendrick-etal-2016]. A larger later meta-analysis found a small benefit, similar for not-on-track clients (d = 0.17) [DeJong-etal-2021]; another found larger gains for clients predicted to do poorly [Lambert-etal-2018]. For the Partners for Change Outcome Management System (PCOMS), benefits appeared mainly in counseling settings, in studies with researcher allegiance that used the ORS as the only outcome [Ostergard-etal-2020]. A second PCOMS meta-analysis that set aside ORS-only outcomes found no effect on independent well-being measures (g = 0.03, 6 trials) [Pejtersen-etal-2020]. The American Psychological Association (APA) task force still rates collecting and delivering client feedback "demonstrably effective" [Norcross-Karpiak-2025].
- **ORS change thresholds disagree.** A recalculation that accounted for test-retest reliability gave a reliable change index of 10.7 points rather than 5, so treat a 5-point change as tentative [Ostergard-etal-2020].
- **Licensing.** A paper-and-pencil license is free for individual practitioners, groups and agencies pay a fee, and the scales may not be used electronically except through authorized vendors [ORS-SRS-Site].
