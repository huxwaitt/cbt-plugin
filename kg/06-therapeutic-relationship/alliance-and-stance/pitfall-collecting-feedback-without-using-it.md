**Bottom line:** Measurement helps only when it changes the conversation and, when needed, the plan. Feedback offers clinicians a chance to strengthen the alliance, shift focus, revisit goals or alter interventions [Ostergard-etal-2020]; forms that are filed and forgotten add burden without benefit. Look at the score with the client in the first minutes, ask about any dip, and act when progress stalls.

## What it looks like

- The client completes forms in the waiting room; the therapist glances at them after the session, or never.
- An outcome graph has been flat or worsening for six sessions and the treatment plan is unchanged.
- Alliance ratings are always perfect and nobody has asked what could be better.
- Measures are completed because the service requires them, and clients describe them as "paperwork."

## Why it happens

- **Measures are introduced as an administrative task,** not as a tool for the client's benefit.
- **Discussing a low alliance score feels awkward,** and therapists avoid inviting criticism. Yet clients often hold back negative feelings for fear of the therapist's reaction [Safran-etal-2011].
- **No decision rule exists** for what counts as "off track" or what to do next. Feedback effects are concentrated in clients predicted to do poorly [Lambert-etal-2018], so without a rule the most important signals are missed.
- **Mixed evidence breeds cynicism.** A Cochrane review found insufficient evidence that routine monitoring improves outcomes, though all included trials were at high risk of bias [Kendrick-etal-2016]; a later, larger meta-analysis found a small benefit (d = 0.15) and slightly less dropout [DeJong-etal-2021].

## The fix

- If the client has just completed a measure → look at it together within the first few minutes and compare it with last time. Because feedback is meant to inform the therapist-client discussion of progress [Ostergard-etal-2020].
- If the alliance score drops or is anything less than the top → thank the client and ask what to change, then change something visible next session. Because responding openly to negative feedback is part of repair [Safran-etal-2011].
- If a client is not on track → run a structured review of alliance, goals, motivation, social support and life events, and agree a change. Because feedback reduced deterioration and nearly doubled reliable improvement in clients predicted to do poorly [Lambert-etal-2018].
- If clients see measures as paperwork → explain once how you use them, and show them their own graph. Because the effective element is collecting and delivering feedback, not merely collecting it [Norcross-Karpiak-2025].

## Sources note

The core trials and meta-analyses disagree on effect size, and the strongest effects come from systems with explicit not-on-track decision rules and support tools [Lambert-etal-2018; Ostergard-etal-2020]. The behaviors described here are expert consensus drawn from that literature rather than separately tested.
