**Bottom line:** A rupture is a tension or breakdown in collaboration, ranging from a barely noticed strain to an open impasse [Safran-etal-2011]. Watch for withdrawal (moving away) and confrontation (moving against), pause the technique, invite the client's view, own your part without defensiveness, then repair by adjusting the task or goal or by exploring what happened. Repair episodes are associated with better outcome (r = .29 across 11 studies and 1,314 patients) [Eubanks-etal-2018]; "addressing ruptures works; ignoring them does not" [Norcross-Karpiak-2025].

## When to use

Ruptures are common. In a study of three brief therapies, including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), for personality disorders, 37% of patients and 56% of therapists reported a rupture in the first six sessions, and observers detect ruptures in more sessions than patients report [Safran-etal-2011]. Each maps onto Bordin's alliance parts: disagreement about goals, disagreement about tasks, or strain in the bond [Bordin-1979; Safran-etal-2011]. Markers, grouped as in a recent version of the Rupture Resolution Rating System (3RS), are shown below [Cirasola-etal-2024].

| Type | Marker | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Withdrawal | Shuts down | Silence, minimal answers, denial, sudden self-criticism |
| Withdrawal | Avoids | Abstract talk, long side stories, topic shifts |
| Withdrawal | Masks experience | Deferential or appeasing; affect that does not match the words |
| Confrontation | Complains | Criticizes the therapist, tasks, session arrangements or progress |
| Confrontation | Pushes back | Rejects ideas, defends self, hostility |
| Confrontation | Controls | Tries to run the session or presses for a quick fix |

## Steps

1. **Notice** the marker and your own reaction (an urge to push, appease or rush).
2. **Pause the technique.** Do not explain the rationale harder; a heavier technique focus during strain appeared to worsen it [Castonguay-etal-1996].
3. **Invite.** Describe what you saw, tentatively: "When I suggested the thought record you went quiet. What happened for you just then?"
4. **Validate and own your part.** Respond openly, accept responsibility for your contribution instead of blaming the client, and thank them for raising it [Safran-etal-2011].
5. **Choose a repair** from two families [Safran-etal-2011; Cirasola-etal-2024].
   - Immediate: clarify a misunderstanding, restate the rationale, change the task or goal, or return to the work.
   - Exploratory: explore the client's experience of the moment, or link it carefully to a pattern in their life.
6. **Check** the repair: "How is it to talk about this?" Agree the next step together.
7. **Follow up** next session and watch the client's alliance rating.

## What to record

- The marker (withdrawal or confrontation) and the likely dimension (goal, task, bond).
- What you said, the client's response, and whether the strain resolved, partly resolved or persisted.
- Any change to tasks, goals or pacing, and any supervision sought.

## Guidance

- If the client suddenly agrees with everything or gives minimal answers → name the shift gently and ask about it. Because clients often hold negative feelings they fear raising, so the therapist must take the initiative [Safran-etal-2011].
- If the client says "This is pointless" → do not defend the treatment; acknowledge the frustration and ask what has felt pointless. Because confrontation markers predicted dropout (d = .74) [Eubanks-etal-2019].
- If the strain is about a task the client dislikes → offer an alternative route to the same goal. Because changing tasks or goals is a core repair [Safran-etal-2011].
- If you contributed (missed something, ran late, pushed too hard) → say so plainly. Because ratings of the therapist's contribution to ruptures predicted dropout (d = .61) [Eubanks-etal-2019].
- If the same strain recurs and fits the formulation (for example, feeling controlled) → explore the link once, carefully, rather than interpreting repeatedly. Because frequent relational interpretations can have negative effects; quality matters more than quantity [Safran-etal-2011].
- If the client discloses suicidal thoughts during a rupture → set the repair aside, ask directly about suicidal ideation and intent, and follow your risk procedure (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`, `02-risk.crisis-response`). If risk is imminent, do not leave the client alone and get emergency help: in the US call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911; in the UK call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans on 116 123. Because National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance advises always asking people with depression directly about suicidal ideation and intent [NICE-NG222].

## Common problems

- **Missing withdrawal.** Deference looks like cooperation. Ask about it.
- **Over-processing.** Not every strain needs exploration; a simple task change often repairs it [Safran-etal-2011].
- **Your own withdrawal markers.** Therapists also show them, such as laughing when uncomfortable or deflecting personal questions; in one single-case study these were often followed by more client withdrawal [Cirasola-etal-2024].
- **Training limits.** Rupture resolution training did not significantly improve outcomes overall (r = .11, 6 studies), though effects were stronger when training aligned with CBT and treatment was brief [Eubanks-etal-2018]. The American Psychological Association task force rates repairing ruptures "probably effective" [Norcross-Karpiak-2025].
- **Resolution matters.** Observer ratings of how well ruptures were resolved also predicted dropout (d = .67) [Eubanks-etal-2019].
