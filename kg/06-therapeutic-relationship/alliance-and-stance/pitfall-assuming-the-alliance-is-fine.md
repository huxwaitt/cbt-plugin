**Bottom line:** Therapists are poor judges of their own alliances and outcomes. Clinicians overestimate improvement and underestimate deterioration [Walfish-etal-2012], rarely predict who will deteriorate [Hannan-etal-2005], and their alliance ratings predict outcome less well than clients' ratings [Cameron-etal-2018]. Ask the client, every session, in a form that makes criticism easy.

## What it looks like

- Notes say "good rapport" every week, but no client-rated alliance or progress measure exists.
- The client is pleasant, agrees with every suggestion, and reports homework as "fine" without detail.
- The therapist is surprised when the client cancels twice and stops coming.
- Supervision discussion focuses on technique, never on how the client experiences the therapist.

## Why it happens

- **Self-assessment bias.** In a survey of mental health professionals, 25% rated their skill at the 90th percentile and none rated themselves below average [Walfish-etal-2012].
- **Deterioration is invisible from the chair.** In one study, 48 therapists predicted only 1 of 40 clients who deteriorated by the end of therapy [Ostergard-etal-2020], while formal monitoring identified most of them [Hannan-etal-2005].
- **Clients do not volunteer complaints.** Many have negative feelings about therapy they are reluctant to raise; observers detect rupture markers in far more sessions than patients report (77% versus 42% of sessions in one small study) [Safran-etal-2011].
- **Deference looks like a good alliance.** Withdrawal ruptures include appeasing and over-agreeing [Safran-etal-2011].

## The fix

- If you have no client-rated data on the relationship → add a brief alliance scale at the end of each session and discuss the result. Because assuming you know the client's view is one of the behaviors that does not work [Norcross-Karpiak-2025].
- If ratings are always at the top → say you expect and welcome criticism: "A perfect score is nice, but I learn most from anything less than perfect. What's one thing that could be better?" Because clients hold back negative feelings for fear of the therapist's reaction [Safran-etal-2011].
- If a client is agreeable but progress is flat → treat compliance as a possible withdrawal marker and ask about it. Because weaker alliances predict dropout (d = .55) [Sharf-etal-2010].
- If you feel confident about a case → check the outcome trajectory before deciding nothing needs to change. Because therapist judgment misses most deteriorating clients [Hannan-etal-2005].

## Sources note

Self-assessment and prediction findings come from a survey [Walfish-etal-2012] and from outcome-monitoring research with student clients [Hannan-etal-2005], with the 1 in 40 figure reported in a later meta-analysis [Ostergard-etal-2020]. The rater-perspective finding is meta-analytic and specific to cognitive behavioral therapy for depression [Cameron-etal-2018]. Generalization to all settings is reasonable but not proven.
