**Bottom line:** Collaborative empiricism means therapist and client work as a team, treating the client's beliefs as hypotheses to be tested against evidence from the client's own life rather than errors to be argued away. Its core is "sharing the work" [Tee-Kazantzis-2011]. Collaboration (r = .29) and goal consensus (r = .24) both predict outcome [Tryon-etal-2018], so make goals, agendas, homework and experiments joint decisions, and treat your own ideas as hypotheses too.

## The principle

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) treats the relationship as an active, collaborative partnership that the therapist builds on purpose [Beck-2021]. Collaborative empiricism has two halves:

- **Collaborative:** the client is an equal partner who shares decisions and effort. Tee and Kazantzis ground this in self-determination theory: people engage more when the work supports their own autonomy [Tee-Kazantzis-2011].
- **Empirical:** both parties look at what actually happens. A thought is a guess about reality, checked by observation and experiment, not by the therapist's authority.

The construct is central in CBT theory, but Tee and Kazantzis found no operational definitions, measures or direct studies of it, and argued that general alliance measures do not capture it [Tee-Kazantzis-2011]. Direct evidence remains thin. The supporting outcome evidence comes from the broader collaboration and goal consensus research [Tryon-etal-2018].

The stance fails in two directions. The therapist can become a debater who tries to win the argument about a thought; when therapists responded to alliance strains by doubling down on cognitive rationale, the strains appeared to get worse [Castonguay-etal-1996]. Or the client can hand all decisions to the therapist; in one case example, a client's "you're in charge here" turned out to reflect a lifelong pattern of submitting to others [Safran-etal-2011].

## Guidance

- If you are setting goals → write them in the client's words and agree how you will both know they are met. Because goal consensus predicts outcome [Tryon-etal-2018].
- If you have a hypothesis about a thought or pattern → offer it as a guess and ask the client to check it against their experience. Because collaborative empiricism integrates the empirical method with shared work rather than persuasion [Tee-Kazantzis-2011].
- If you are planning homework or an experiment → design it together, ask the client to predict obstacles, and agree what result would count as evidence either way. Because patient-therapist collaboration predicts outcome (r = .29) [Tryon-etal-2018].
- If the client says "You're the expert, just tell me what to do" → offer expertise on methods while keeping the client as the expert on their own experience, and give real choices. Because deference can hide a withdrawal pattern [Safran-etal-2011], and autonomy support drives engagement [Tee-Kazantzis-2011].
- If the client resists testing a belief or seems to feel argued with → stop the technique, ask what is happening, and validate the concern before going on. Because intensifying cognitive rationale during alliance strain appeared to worsen the strain and interfere with change [Castonguay-etal-1996].
- If an experiment disconfirms your own prediction → say so plainly and update the formulation together. Because modeling the empirical stance is what makes the work shared rather than a contest [Tee-Kazantzis-2011].
- If the session is ending → ask what was useful, what was not, and what to change next time. Because research supports seeking client input and giving regular feedback throughout therapy [Tryon-etal-2018].

## Example phrasings

- "Here's a hunch, and you tell me if it fits: when your boss frowns, your mind jumps to 'I'm about to be fired.' Does that match what happens?"
- "You're 90% sure they'll laugh if you speak up. How could we find out what actually happens?"
- "There are two ways we could start: the sleep plan or the worry time. Which feels more useful this week?"
- "My prediction was wrong. You didn't need the safety behavior at all. What do we make of that?"
- "What did I get wrong today, or what would you change?"
- Avoid: "That thought is irrational."
- Avoid: "Your homework this week is..." with no discussion.
- Avoid: "Trust me, this works." (asks for faith instead of offering a test)

## Evidence

- Goal consensus: 54 studies, 7,278 patients, r = .24 (about d = .49). Patient and therapist collaboration: 53 studies, 5,286 patients, r = .29 (about d = .61) [Tryon-etal-2018].
- The American Psychological Association task force rated collaboration and goal consensus "demonstrably effective" [Norcross-Karpiak-2025].
- When Tee and Kazantzis reviewed it, collaborative empiricism itself had no validated measures or direct outcome studies; its value in CBT rests on theory, expert consensus and the collaboration evidence above [Tee-Kazantzis-2011].
- Caution evidence: in cognitive therapy for depression, a technique focus used to fix alliance problems correlated negatively with outcome [Castonguay-etal-1996].
