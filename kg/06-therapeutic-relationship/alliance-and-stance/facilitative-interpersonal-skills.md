**Bottom line:** Therapists differ in effectiveness: on average about 5% of the variation in client outcome lies between therapists, with a range of 0.2% to 29% across studies [Johns-etal-2019]. Experience, profession and adherence to a cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) protocol explain little of this. What distinguishes more effective therapists is skill under interpersonal pressure (facilitative interpersonal skills), professional self-doubt held alongside self-acceptance, and time spent deliberately practicing difficult moments [Anderson-etal-2009; Nissen-Lie-etal-2017; Chow-etal-2015].

## What it is

**Therapist effects** are differences in average outcome between therapists who treat comparable clients. Expressed as an effect size, they are about d = 0.35 in clinical trials and d = 0.55 in naturalistic settings [Wampold-2015].

**Facilitative interpersonal skills (FIS)** are skills that help people in emotional distress change. They are measured with a performance task: the therapist responds aloud to video clips of difficult clients (for example an angry or passive-aggressive client), and trained raters score eight domains, listed below [DeJong-etal-2024].

1. Verbal fluency
2. Hope and positive expectations
3. Persuasiveness
4. Emotional expression
5. Warmth, acceptance and understanding
6. Empathy
7. Alliance bond capacity
8. Alliance rupture repair responsiveness

## Why it matters in CBT

- In 25 therapists treating 1,141 clients, FIS was the only skill measure that accounted for differences in therapist outcomes; a self-report social skills inventory did not [Anderson-etal-2009].
- In a randomized trial, clients of high-FIS therapists improved more and rated the alliance higher from the first session; the therapists' training status made a negligible difference [Anderson-etal-2016].
- Therapist variability in the alliance, not patient variability, predicted outcome [Baldwin-etal-2007].
- Adherence (r = .02) and competence (r = .07) ratings did not significantly predict outcome across 36 studies [Webb-etal-2010]. Delivering a CBT protocol correctly is necessary but not sufficient.
- Experience alone does not help: across 6,591 patients and 170 therapists, outcomes declined very slightly as experience grew, though early termination fell [Goldberg-etal-2016].

## Key details

- **Skill under pressure is the point.** In a pilot study of 46 trainees, scores were higher when responding to benign clips than to challenging ones [DeJong-etal-2024]. Test and train on hard moments, not easy ones.
- **Professional self-doubt.** In 70 therapists and 255 patients, therapists who reported more doubt about their professional effectiveness produced more change in clients' interpersonal distress when they also related to themselves kindly as people. Constructive coping (reflecting, consulting, problem-solving with the patient) helped; avoiding problems, withdrawing or acting out frustration was linked to less change [Nissen-Lie-etal-2017].
- **Overconfidence is the norm.** A quarter of clinicians rated themselves at the 90th percentile and none below average [Walfish-etal-2012].
- **Deliberate practice.** Among 69 therapists and 4,580 clients, therapist effects explained 5.1% of outcome variance. In a subsample of 17 therapists, experience, qualifications and caseload did not predict outcome, but time spent specifically improving skills did, and the most effective therapists put more effort into reviewing recordings alone [Chow-etal-2015]. In a randomized trial with 88 therapists, a deliberate practice workshop beat a didactic one on observer-rated skill, with the advantage kept at 4 months [Westra-etal-2021].
- **Format matters.** Therapists showed lower observer-rated skills in teletherapy scenarios than in matching in-person scenarios [Lin-etal-2025].

## In the session

- If a client turns angry or critical toward you → stay engaged, warm and verbally fluent, acknowledge their point, and invite more. Because responding well to interpersonally challenging moments is what FIS captures and what predicts outcome [Anderson-etal-2009].
- If you notice yourself avoiding a client or topic → name it in supervision and plan a direct response. Because avoidant coping was linked to less patient change [Nissen-Lie-etal-2017].
- If you want to improve → record sessions (with consent), pick one difficult moment, write or rehearse better responses, get feedback, and repeat. Because targeted practice time, not experience, predicted outcomes [Chow-etal-2015]; the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) also advises supervision that can include review of recordings [NICE-NG222].
- If you feel sure you are above average → compare your caseload outcome data with benchmarks. Because self-assessment is biased upward [Walfish-etal-2012].
- If you are evaluating an artificial intelligence (AI) assisted or scripted tool → test it on challenging client simulations, not only benign ones. Because skill drops in challenging situations [DeJong-etal-2024].
