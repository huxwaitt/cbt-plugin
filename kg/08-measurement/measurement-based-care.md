**Bottom line:** Measurement-based care (MBC) means giving a brief validated measure before every or nearly every session, reviewing the score and its trend with the client, and changing the plan together when progress stalls [Lewis-etal-2019]. Progress feedback gives a small average benefit and fewer dropouts [DeJong-etal-2021], and for clients predicted to do poorly it reduced deterioration and nearly doubled reliable change [Lambert-etal-2018]. One-time screening, infrequent measurement, and feedback outside the clinical encounter do not help [Fortney-etal-2017].

## When to use

- Every episode of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), from the assessment session to the final session and any follow-up or booster.
- Most of all when a client is not improving as expected. Clinicians rarely predict who will fail to benefit, while formal monitoring flagged 85% of later deteriorators by the third session in one study [Hannan-etal-2005]. Therapists reviewing notes had considerable difficulty recognizing deterioration [Hatfield-etal-2010].
- In services that report outcomes. In England, National Health Service (NHS) Talking Therapies (formerly IAPT) monitors symptoms session by session and obtains pre and post scores for 98% of patients [Clark-etal-2018]. US services have no single national system, so the clinic sets its own routine.

## Steps

1. At assessment, choose one primary symptom measure that fits the main problem, plus a functioning measure if useful (see `08-measurement.instrument-selection`). Record the baseline.
2. Explain the purpose in plain words: "I'd like you to fill in this short questionnaire before each session. It takes about two minutes and helps us both see whether what we're doing is working."
3. Give it before the session starts, in the waiting room, by secure app, or at the start of a video call. Use the same measure and time frame every time.
4. Score it before agenda setting. Look at the total, any risk item, and the trend since baseline.
5. Share it during the mood check: "Your score went from 18 to 14 this week. Does that fit how the week felt?" Show a simple graph.
6. Act on it. Decide whether the change exceeds measurement error (see `08-measurement.reliable-and-clinically-significant-change`). If the line is flat or rising after several sessions, revise the plan with the client [Lewis-etal-2019].
7. At the end, compare the final score with baseline and record the outcome category: reliably improved, recovered, no reliable change, or reliably deteriorated.

The four core components are a routinely given measure, clinician review, client review, and a collaborative decision about the treatment plan [Lewis-etal-2019].

## What to record

- Measure name and version, date, total and subscale scores, and the response to any risk item.
- One line on what you and the client made of the score.
- Any plan change the score prompted, or why you made none.
- Missing data and the reason (declined, forgot, technology failure).

## Guidance

- If a score rises by more than the measure's reliable change amount → name it calmly and explore it before the planned agenda: "Your score is quite a bit higher this week. Can we spend a few minutes on what has been happening?" Because catching deterioration early is where feedback helps most [Lambert-etal-2018].
- If a client is still flat or worse after several sessions → review the formulation, therapeutic alliance, homework and treatment dose with the client and in supervision, and record the decision. Because feedback paired with structured problem solving prevented more treatment failures than feedback alone [Shimokawa-etal-2010].
- If any self-harm or suicide item is endorsed above zero → ask about it in that session before other agenda items and follow `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`; if risk is imminent, follow `02-risk.crisis-response` (United States: 911, or call or text 988; United Kingdom: 999, NHS 111, Samaritans 116 123). Because a positive answer on item 9 of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) predicts suicide attempts over the following months, not only days [Simon-etal-2013].
- If the client says the measure misses their experience → ask what is missing and add a personal rating (see `08-measurement.idiographic-measures`) rather than dropping the standard measure. Because disagreement between score and experience is clinical information.
- If a client asks why you measure every week → "Therapists are not very good at noticing when things are slipping. This gives us both an early warning." Because a shared purpose improves completion and honest answering.

## Common problems

- **Collecting without discussing.** Scores fed back to clinicians outside the clinical encounter did not improve outcomes [Fortney-etal-2017].
- **Expecting large effects.** The pooled effect of progress feedback on symptoms is small (d = 0.15), with a small reduction in dropout [DeJong-etal-2021]. A Cochrane review found no clear overall benefit, but graded that evidence low quality because most trials had high risk of bias [Kendrick-etal-2016]. In one large CBT trial, feedback gave similar outcomes in about two fewer sessions [Janse-etal-2017].
- **Switching measures mid-treatment.** The trend line is lost. Add a measure if needed; do not swap.
- **Low uptake.** Typically fewer than 20% of behavioral health practitioners use MBC [Lewis-etal-2019]. Build it into the session routine and the record template rather than relying on memory.
