**Bottom line:** Collecting scores is not measurement-based care. The benefit comes from reviewing the score with the client in the session and changing the plan when progress stalls. Scores filed unseen, fed back outside the encounter, or gathered only occasionally do not improve outcomes [Fortney-etal-2017].

## What it looks like

- A stack of completed questionnaires in the chart, none mentioned in session notes.
- A client's score has not moved in eight sessions and the plan is unchanged.
- The client asks "Do you even look at these?"
- Scores are sent to a dashboard that the therapist checks monthly, after sessions.
- Measures are given at intake and discharge only, so a mid-treatment slide is invisible.

## Why it happens

- Measurement is seen as an administrative task for the service, not a clinical tool.
- Clinicians believe measures are no better than clinical judgment, a common barrier to measurement-based care (MBC) [Lewis-etal-2019], yet therapists reviewing notes had considerable difficulty recognizing client deterioration [Hatfield-etal-2010], and clinicians rarely predicted which clients would fail to benefit [Hannan-etal-2005].
- Session time feels short, and discussing a score seems to compete with the planned technique.
- No decision rules exist, so a flat line never triggers anything.

## The fix

- If scores arrive before the session → look at the total, the risk item and the trend before you greet the client. Because MBC expects review before or during the encounter [Lewis-etal-2019].
- If you start the session → show the score in the mood check and ask for the client's view: "Your score is about the same as the last three weeks. What do you make of that?" Because client review of the data is a core component of MBC [Lewis-etal-2019].
- If the trend is flat or worse after several sessions → put it on the agenda and review the formulation, alliance, homework and dose, then agree one change. Because feedback combined with structured problem-solving tools prevented more treatment failures than feedback alone [Shimokawa-etal-2010].
- If you supervise → ask to see the graph for every case reviewed, not only the notes. Because therapists reading notes had considerable difficulty recognizing deterioration [Hatfield-etal-2010].
- If a service designs the workflow → schedule measures before every session, show results to the clinician in the session, and record one line of response. Because one-time screening, infrequent assessment and feedback outside the encounter were the ineffective approaches [Fortney-etal-2017].
- Record in each note: the score, the client's comment, and the decision ("continue", "adjust", or "review in supervision").
- Avoid: "Just pop that form in the box on your way out."

## Sources note

Reviews agree that frequent feedback discussed in the session is the active ingredient [Fortney-etal-2017] [Lewis-etal-2019]. The average effect of feedback is small (d = 0.15) [DeJong-etal-2021], so the case for measuring rests mainly on catching clients who are going off track.
