**Bottom line:** A functional analysis (often taught as ABC: antecedents, behavior, consequences) identifies the important, changeable, causal relationships that control a specific problem behavior for this person: what reliably comes before it, and what follows it in the short and long term. It is the formulation strategy with the most empirical support, partly because it is the most studied, and it tells you which trigger or reinforcer to change [Persons-Hong-2016]. Keep it specific to one behavior, check both antecedents and consequences, and test it with monitoring.

## When to use

- The target is an observable behavior with a clear pattern (substance use, bingeing, self-injury, aggression, avoidance, compulsive checking, procrastination).
- No disorder-specific model fits, or the behavior crosses diagnoses. Kuyken and colleagues list functional analysis as a generic option at the explanatory level [Kuyken-etal-2008].
- You need to individualize a standard protocol. Ghaderi used functional analysis to add or remove elements of standard cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for bulimia nervosa [Ghaderi-2006].

For self-harm, complete a risk assessment first (`02-risk.self-harm`) and keep the analysis on triggers, functions and alternatives, never on method detail. If there is current suicidal intent or a recent serious act, stop the analysis and follow `02-risk.crisis-response`. In a crisis, US clients can call or text 988 or call 911; UK clients can call 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123.

## Steps

Virués-Ortega and Haynes define a functional analysis as identifying the relevant, controllable and causal functional relationships that apply to an individual's specific behaviors [Virues-Ortega-Haynes-2005].

1. **Define the target behavior** precisely: what, how often, how long, how intense.
2. **Collect recent episodes** (three to five) by interview and a simple log kept between sessions.
3. **List antecedents** for each episode: situation, people, time, internal states (emotion, sensation, urge, thought).
4. **List consequences** in two columns: short-term (relief, pleasure, escape, attention) and long-term (guilt, health, relationship damage, problem maintained).
5. **Look for the function.** Ask what the behavior achieves right away: removing something unpleasant (negative reinforcement) or adding something wanted (positive reinforcement).
6. **Judge which links are causal.** A causal relationship needs covariation, a logical connection, the cause coming first in time, and no third variable that explains both [Virues-Ortega-Haynes-2005].
7. **Rate importance and changeability.** Choose antecedents and consequences that matter most and that the client can modify [Virues-Ortega-Haynes-2005].
8. **Plan and test.** Change one controlling variable, predict the effect, and track the behavior.

Example (illustrative): Antecedent: alone at night after a critical message, feeling ashamed. Behavior: drinks five beers. Short-term: shame eases, falls asleep. Long-term: hangover, misses work, more self-criticism, more shame. Function: escape from shame. Targets: shame tolerance and self-compassion, evening plan, reply to criticism.

## What to record

The behavior definition, a table of episodes (antecedent, behavior, short-term consequence, long-term consequence), the hypothesized function, the selected targets, and the monitoring measure.

## Guidance

- If you have only asked about triggers → ask "What happened right after? What did it do for you?" Because people judging a case often miss one side: in one study of a hypothetical case, only a minority considered both antecedents and consequences [Virues-Ortega-Haynes-2005].
- If the client says "I just do it" → walk through the last episode minute by minute. Because detailed sequences reveal antecedents the client has not noticed.
- If the behavior relieves distress quickly → name that function respectfully before planning alternatives. Because a replacement must meet the same need or the old behavior will return.
- If several behaviors share one function (drinking, bingeing and scrolling all escape shame) → target the shared function. Because one controlling variable may maintain several problems.

Example phrasing: "It sounds like drinking does something useful for you in the moment. What does it do? And what does it cost you by the next day?" Avoid: "Why do you keep doing that?"

## Common problems

- **Vague target** ("my anger"). Fix: define one observable behavior.
- **Confusing correlation with cause.** Fix: apply the four causal criteria above.
- **Analysis never updated.** Fix: revisit after each new episode log.

**Evidence.** A meta-analysis of within-subject time-series studies found that interventions based on a pre-intervention functional behavioral assessment reduced problem behavior more than interventions not based on one (effect size 0.85) [Hurl-etal-2016]. The abstract reports 19 studies; Persons and Tompkins describe the same meta-analysis as 13 studies of 57 single cases, mostly children or young adults with disruptive behavior [Persons-Tompkins-2022]. Persons and Hong judge functional analysis the formulation strategy with the greatest empirical support, perhaps because it is the most often studied [Persons-Hong-2016]. Evidence in adult CBT outpatients is thinner (emerging evidence).
