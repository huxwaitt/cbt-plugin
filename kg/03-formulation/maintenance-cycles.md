**Bottom line:** A maintenance cycle is a loop in which the person's response to a problem (avoiding, checking, withdrawing, ruminating) brings short-term relief but keeps the problem going. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) works mostly by breaking these loops. Draw them from the client's own examples, name what each response costs in the long run, and pick one loop to target first.

## Components

**Generic loop.** Trigger, then a thought or appraisal, then emotion and body response, then a coping response, then a consequence that feeds back into the appraisal or the emotion. Williams and Garland describe how reduced activity and unhelpful behaviors create vicious circles that maintain depression and anxiety, and treat breaking these circles as the main route to change [Williams-Garland-2002].

**Box, arrow in, arrow out.** Padesky describes a simple co-constructed model: a box for the issue of concern, arrows in for its triggers, and arrows out for what follows and maintains it [Padesky-2020]. It suits single, focused problems.

**The vicious flower.** Moorey built a six-cycle maintenance model for depression because, unlike anxiety, depression had no accepted maintenance diagram [Moorey-2010]. Depression sits at the center, and each petal is a loop that feeds and is fed by it:

| Petal | Example loop |
|---|---|
| Automatic negative thinking | "I'm useless" lowers mood, which brings more such thoughts |
| Rumination and self-attacking | Hours going over failures deepens low mood |
| Withdrawal and avoidance | Stopping seeing friends removes enjoyment and confirms "nobody cares" |
| Unhelpful behavior | Drinking or oversleeping helps briefly and costs later |
| Motivation and physical symptoms | Fatigue leads to doing less, and doing less increases fatigue |
| Mood and emotion | Guilt, shame or anger about being depressed adds to the depression |

Moorey reports that a small sample of trainees found the model very useful for understanding maintenance, and proposes it for conceptualization, socialization, treatment planning and teaching [Moorey-2010]. This is expert consensus and early user feedback, not trial evidence.

## How it keeps the problem going

Common mechanisms:
- **Avoidance and escape** prevent the person from learning that the feared outcome does not happen or is bearable.
- **Safety behaviors** make it look as if the behavior prevented disaster.
- **Withdrawal and inactivity** remove sources of pleasure and mastery.
- **Rumination and worry** keep attention on threat and loss. Persons notes these can be treated as behaviors and targeted directly [Persons-Tompkins-2022].
- **Attention and monitoring** (checking the body, scanning for criticism) find more "evidence."

## Drawing it with the client

1. Start from a recent episode or a five-part model already drawn.
2. Ask "What did you do to cope?" then "What happened next, in the short term?" then "And in the long run?"
3. Draw the arrow back from the long-run consequence to the thought, emotion or problem.
4. For depression, draw the center and add only the petals the client recognizes. Add more as they appear.
5. Ask the client which loop feels most important and most changeable.

- If the client says the coping behavior helps → agree that it helps in the short term, then ask what it costs over weeks. Because loops persist through short-term relief.
- If many loops appear → choose one to start, ideally one the client can change this week. Because the aim is the simplest formulation that guides treatment, not a complete one [Persons-Tompkins-2022].
- If the client feels blamed for their own coping → say that the behavior made sense and that anyone would do it, then look at what it costs. Because a formulation should increase hope, not shame.

Example phrasing: "So staying in bed makes the morning easier, and then by evening you feel worse about the day. Is that a loop we could test?" Avoid: "You're keeping yourself depressed."
