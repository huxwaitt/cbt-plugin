**Bottom line:** The cognitive conceptualization diagram (CCD) links three or more typical problem situations (automatic thought, its meaning, emotion, behavior) to the client's core beliefs, intermediate beliefs, coping strategies and relevant history. Fill it in between sessions from the bottom up, mark every inference with a question mark until the client confirms it, and show clients a simplified version rather than the full form.

## When to use

- After the evaluation and early sessions, once you have several concrete examples of the client's reactions [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- When problems recur across many situations, are long-standing, or seem driven by stable beliefs about the self (for example, personality-related difficulties or chronic depression).
- When cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for a single disorder has stalled and you suspect an underlying belief is blocking change.

Not needed for every client. A five-part model or disorder-specific model is enough for many straightforward presentations [Kuyken-etal-2008].

## Steps

The Beck Institute instructions describe this order [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020]:

1. **Start midway down the page, with situations.** Record three problematic situations that are typical for the client. Choose ones where the client shows a pattern of unhelpful behavior or where automatic thoughts share a theme. If there is more than one theme, include a situation for each.
2. **For each situation, fill four boxes.** Automatic thought(s): what went through the client's mind. Meaning of the automatic thought: what it meant to them. Emotion: what they felt. Behavior: what they did next.
3. **Look across the meanings.** The meanings of automatic thoughts across representative situations point to the core belief. Ask "If that were true, what would it say about you?"
4. **Fill the core belief box.** Record the client's most central negative beliefs about themself during the current episode.
5. **Fill intermediate beliefs.** Record the assumptions, attitudes and rules that help the client cope with the core belief, often as "If I..., then..." statements.
6. **Fill coping strategies.** Record the patterns of behavior the client uses to cope with the core belief.
7. **Fill relevant life history and precipitants.** Record the experiences that contributed to developing and maintaining the core belief, and what triggered the current episode.
8. **Add boxes if needed.** Three situations are not enough for some clients, especially those with several core beliefs.

The full Beck Institute case write-up places the diagram inside a wider document: intake information, history, the diagram, a conceptualization summary (precipitants, maintaining factors, values and aspirations, narrative) and a treatment plan [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020]. Judith Beck's text reproduces this write-up as an appendix [Beck-2021].

**Strength-based version.** The strength-based CCD records adaptive core beliefs, adaptive assumptions and helpful coping from before the current difficulties. Gather the data at evaluation by asking about the best period in the client's life [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].

## What to record

- The completed diagram, dated, with a question mark on every unconfirmed hypothesis.
- The client's confirmation, disagreement or belief rating (0 to 100) for each belief once discussed.
- A short narrative summary linking history, beliefs, coping and current triggers.
- What changed when you revised it, and why.

## Guidance

- If you are filling the top boxes before you have concrete situations → stop and gather examples first. Because the diagram is built from specific information the client provides [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If you want to share the diagram → draw a simplified version, or fold the form in half and start with the lower portion together. Because the full diagram is too confusing for most clients [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If two colleagues would fill the belief boxes differently → treat the beliefs as hypotheses to test, not facts. Because practitioners agree on descriptive content but agreement drops for inferred parts [Kuyken-etal-2005].
- If the client has a history of trauma → keep history boxes brief and factual and check the client's readiness before discussing them. Because longitudinal explanation is added only if needed [Kuyken-etal-2008], and formulation that touches identity and painful history can cause distress that sometimes outlasts therapy [Redhead-etal-2015].
- If discussing history brings up hopelessness, self-harm urges or suicidal thoughts → pause the diagram and assess risk using `02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`. Because risk takes priority over formulation work.

Example phrasing: "I've noticed that in all three situations the thought seemed to mean something like 'I can't cope.' Does that fit, or is it something else?"

## Common problems

- **Diagram written from theory, not data.** Quality varies widely; in a study of 115 practitioners formulating the same case on the Beck diagram, only 44% of formulations were rated at least good enough, and quality rose with experience and accreditation [Kuyken-etal-2005]. Fix: use the client's words and real situations.
- **Too many core beliefs.** Fix: keep the one or two that explain the most situations.
- **No link to treatment.** Fix: for each box, name what you will do about it (see `03-formulation.formulation-to-treatment`).
- **Strengths left out.** Fix: complete the strength-based version too.
