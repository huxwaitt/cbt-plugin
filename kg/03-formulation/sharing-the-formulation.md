**Bottom line:** Build the formulation with the client, not for them: they supply the content, the words and the corrections, and you supply structure and theory. Keep it as simple as the stage of therapy allows, include strengths, present it as a best guess, and check how it lands. Most clients find a shared formulation helpful, but some find it distressing, so watch for that and respond.

## The principle

In collaborative case conceptualization, client and therapist each bring expertise and test the formulation together against the client's experience [Kuyken-etal-2008]. The Collaborative Case Conceptualization Rating Scale (CCC-RS) turns this into observable behaviors: the client is actively engaged and client and therapist ideas are valued equally; the formulation uses the client's own language, metaphors and images and includes relevant cultural aspects; it is as simple as possible for the stage of therapy; and it includes strengths that inform the plan [Padesky-etal-2011]. The scale has good preliminary reliability [Kuyken-etal-2016a]. Padesky argues that co-constructed models in client language lead to greater understanding, empowerment and adherence [Padesky-2020].

## Guidance

- If you are about to draw a formulation → hand the client the pen or ask them to tell you what to write. Because active client engagement is a marker of competent collaborative conceptualization [Padesky-etal-2011].
- If the client uses their own image or phrase ("the black cloud," "my alarm system") → put that phrase in the diagram instead of a technical term. Because the client's own language, metaphors and images are part of competent practice [Padesky-etal-2011].
- If you have a full belief-level diagram in your notes → show only a simplified version, or build the lower half together. Because the full Beck diagram is too confusing for most clients [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If the formulation includes painful history or self-critical beliefs → introduce it gradually, name strengths alongside it, and ask how it feels to see it. Because formulation can be saddening or upsetting, and pacing, sensitivity and protective factors reduce that risk [Macneil-etal-2012].
- If the client looks flat, tearful or goes quiet after seeing it → pause, reflect what you see, and ask directly. Because for some clients distress from formulation lasted beyond the end of therapy, and the authors advise watching for adverse reactions so they can be resolved during therapy [Redhead-etal-2015].
- If the client becomes hopeless, or mentions self-harm or suicide, while discussing the formulation → stop the formulation work and assess risk (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`). Because risk takes priority over formulation.
- If the client disagrees with part of it → treat the disagreement as data and change the diagram. Because the formulation is a hypothesis to be corrected by the client's experience [Kuyken-etal-2008].
- If the client says "So it's my fault" → separate understanding from blame: the coping made sense, and now it can be changed. Because clients describe feeling understood and accepted as a key benefit [Redhead-etal-2015].
- If cultural values shape the problem or how the client relates to the therapist → include them explicitly, in the client's terms. Because incorporating relevant cultural aspects is part of competent conceptualization [Padesky-etal-2011].
- If several sessions have passed → bring the formulation out again and ask what to add, remove or redraw. Because Persons describes the formulation as a living document referred to often and revised frequently [Persons-Tompkins-2022].
- If sessions are by video → share the screen and draw together. Because the same co-construction is possible remotely [Persons-Tompkins-2022].

## Example phrasings

- Opening: "Can we put what we've talked about on paper? You tell me what goes where, and correct me when I get it wrong."
- Tentative offer: "Here's my rough guess about how these fit together. How close is it?"
- Checking impact: "How is it for you, seeing it laid out like this?"
- Linking to strengths: "This box is the part that keeps things stuck. This one is what you already do well, and we'll use it."
- Revising: "Last month we thought checking kept the worry going. What have we learned since?"
- Avoid: "This is your formulation." Avoid: "Your core belief is that you're unlovable, which comes from your childhood."

## Evidence

The evidence is qualitative and small-sample. In interviews with 10 clients after cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for depression or anxiety, formulation helped them understand their problems, feel understood and accepted, feel an emotional shift and move forward; it could also cause distress, for example from an inaccurate formulation or implications for identity [Redhead-etal-2015]. Among 13 clients in CBT for psychosis, reactions to a shared written formulation were complex and changed over time [Pain-etal-2008]. In a small experimental study in psychosis, formulation had little or no impact on symptoms; therapists rated the relationship as improved, and six clients found it at least partly negative [Chadwick-etal-2003]. These findings support collaboration, pacing and monitoring rather than a fixed script (expert consensus).
