**Bottom line:** Beck's cognitive model has layers. Situation-specific automatic thoughts sit on the surface; beneath them are intermediate beliefs (attitudes, rules and conditional assumptions), and beneath those are global core beliefs about the self, others, the world and the future. People develop coping strategies to live with their core beliefs, and these strategies often keep the beliefs intact. Work from the surface down, and treat deeper layers as hypotheses until the client confirms them.

## Components

This structure is set out in Judith Beck's text (Chapters 3 and 17) and organized on the Beck Institute conceptualization diagram [Beck-2021; BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020]. The example below comes from the Beck Institute case write-up of a depressed man called Abe [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].

| Level | What it is | How to spot it | Example (Abe) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Automatic thoughts | Quick, situation-specific words or images that pass through the mind | "What was going through your mind just then?" | "I'll never get a job." |
| Intermediate beliefs: attitudes | Evaluative stances | "It's important to...", "It's terrible to..." | "It's important to be responsible, competent and reliable." |
| Intermediate beliefs: rules | Should and must statements | "I should...", "I must..." | "I should solve problems myself." (held before the depression) |
| Intermediate beliefs: assumptions | Conditional if-then predictions | "If I..., then..." | "If I ask for help, people will see how incompetent I am." |
| Core beliefs | Global, rigid beliefs about self, others, world, future | Meaning of thoughts across many situations | "I'm incompetent, a failure." |
| Coping (compensatory) strategies | Behavior patterns used to manage the core belief | "What do you do so that belief doesn't show or come true?" | Avoids challenges and avoids asking for help |

The Beck Institute groups negative core beliefs about the self into three families: helpless (incompetent, weak, a failure), unlovable (unlikeable, bound to be rejected) and worthless (morally bad, unacceptable) [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020]. Its strength-based version also records adaptive core beliefs held before the current difficulties [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].

## How it keeps the problem going

1. A situation activates a core belief. Persons notes Beck's proposal that the type of event matters: loss and rejection tend to activate beliefs about dependency, while failure tends to activate beliefs about failure and loss of autonomy [Persons-Tompkins-2022].
2. The belief shapes the meaning given to the situation, which appears as automatic thoughts with matching emotions and behavior [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
3. Assumptions and rules guide coping. Abe's assumption "If I try to do hard things, I'll fail" led to avoidance, and avoidance helped maintain his depression [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
4. Once active, a core belief takes in negative information quickly and discounts information that contradicts it, so it feels confirmed [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].

Note the reliability limit: therapists agree on surface content but agree poorly on inferred core beliefs [Kuyken-etal-2008].

## Drawing it with the client

- If the client reports an automatic thought with strong emotion → ask "If that were true, what would it mean about you?" and repeat until the answer becomes a general statement about the self. Because the meaning of automatic thoughts across situations is the route to core belief hypotheses [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If the same meaning recurs across several typical situations → offer it as a tentative core belief and ask the client to rate how much they believe it, 0 to 100. Because hypotheses stay tentative until the client confirms them [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If you hear "should" or "if, then" → write it as a rule or assumption and ask what the client fears would happen if they broke it. Because assumptions and rules are how clients cope with a core belief, and they drive coping behavior [Beck-2021; BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If the client becomes distressed when a core belief is named → slow down, validate, and return to current situations. Because naming core beliefs too early can feel like a verdict rather than a hypothesis.

Example phrasing: "When you thought 'I'll mess this up,' what would that say about you, if it were true?" Avoid: "Your core belief is that you're worthless."

## Treatment targets it implies

- **Automatic thoughts**: identify and evaluate them first; they are the most accessible level (`cognitive.thought-records`).
- **Assumptions and rules**: test the if-then prediction directly with behavioral experiments and drop the coping strategy that blocks the test (`cognitive.modifying-intermediate-beliefs`).
- **Core beliefs**: work on them later, once the client can evaluate thoughts, by building and strengthening a new belief and logging evidence for it; the Beck Institute belief change worksheet rates the old and new beliefs each session [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020] (`cognitive.core-belief-work`).
- **Coping strategies**: reduce them gradually, because each one prevents the belief from being tested.
