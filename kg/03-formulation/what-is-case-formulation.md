**Bottom line:** A case formulation is a working hypothesis, built with the client, that first describes and then explains why this person has these problems now and what keeps them going, so that treatment can be chosen and tested. It works alongside a diagnosis rather than replacing it. Start descriptive, add explanation only as evidence builds, include strengths, and revise it whenever progress data disagree with it.

## What it is

Kuyken, Padesky and Dudley define case conceptualization as a process in which therapist and client work together to first describe and then explain the client's presenting issues using cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) theory. Its main purpose is to guide therapy so that it relieves distress and builds resilience [Kuyken-etal-2008]. In this wiki "case formulation" and "case conceptualization" mean the same thing.

Persons and Tompkins treat the formulation as a hypothesis that ties four elements together in a short narrative or diagram [Persons-Tompkins-2022]:
- **Problems**: everything the client is struggling with, across symptoms, relationships, work or school, health, money, housing, legal issues, leisure and problems with past treatment.
- **Mechanisms**: the psychological (sometimes biological) processes that cause and maintain the problems. This is the heart of the formulation.
- **Origins**: the earlier, distal factors that explain how the person acquired those mechanisms.
- **Precipitants**: the recent events that switched the mechanisms on.

## Why it matters in CBT

A diagnosis and a formulation answer different questions.

| | Diagnosis | Formulation |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Which category fits? | Why this person, why this problem, why now? |
| Tells you | A likely evidence-based protocol and prognosis | The client's own triggers, beliefs, maintaining cycles and strengths |
| Changes over time | Rarely | Expected to change as data come in |

A diagnosis says little about cause, is a weak guide to which intervention to use, and says nothing about how the person experiences the problem. Two people with the same diagnosis can share few symptoms [Macneil-etal-2012]. A diagnosis still matters: it points to an evidence-based general (nomothetic) model and treatment, which the formulation then adapts to the individual (idiographic) case [Persons-Tompkins-2022]. Use both.

Be honest about the evidence. Formulation is widely treated as a core CBT skill, but its contribution to outcomes has not been demonstrated, and experienced therapists can reliably construct only some of its elements [Easden-Kazantzis-2018], mainly the descriptive ones [Kuyken-etal-2008]. See `03-formulation.formulation-evidence`.

## Key details

**Levels.** Kuyken and colleagues describe a formulation that evolves over therapy [Kuyken-etal-2008]:
1. **Descriptive**: the presenting issues in cognitive and behavioral terms (for example, a five-part model of a recent episode).
2. **Cross-sectional explanatory**: triggers and maintaining factors, usually drawn from a disorder-specific model or a functional analysis.
3. **Longitudinal explanatory**: predisposing and protective factors from the person's history, added only if needed.

**Three principles** of the collaborative approach [Kuyken-etal-2008]: collaborative empiricism (both parties bring expertise and test ideas against the client's experience), levels that develop over time, and client strengths included at every level.

**The hypothesis-testing approach.** Persons and Tompkins describe a loop of assessment, formulation and intervention, with the therapist returning repeatedly to assessment to monitor progress and update the formulation [Persons-Tompkins-2022]. Their seven steps are: build a problem list, assign diagnoses, choose an anchoring diagnosis, choose a general model for it, individualize that model, form hypotheses about origins, and describe precipitants. They value the simplest formulation that helps treatment over one that tries to be complete [Persons-Tompkins-2022].

## In the session

- If you are in the first or second session → aim for a descriptive formulation of one or two recent episodes, not a theory of the person's childhood. Because agreement between therapists is good at descriptive levels and poor at inferential ones [Kuyken-etal-2008].
- If you form a hypothesis about a belief the client has not confirmed → write it with a question mark and treat it as tentative. Because the conceptualization diagram instructions say hypotheses stay provisional until the client confirms them [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If the client asks "What is wrong with me?" → give the diagnosis plainly and add the formulation as the personal explanation. Because a diagnosis alone tells the client little about why or what to do [Macneil-etal-2012].
- If progress stalls → collect more assessment data and test a different formulation. Because poor progress suggests the formulation may be wrong [Persons-Tompkins-2022].

Example phrasing: "Let's draw a rough map of what happened last Tuesday. It's our best guess for now, and we'll change it as we learn more."

**What to record:** the problem list, the current working formulation (diagram or one paragraph) with its date, which parts are confirmed and which are hypotheses, and the next planned test of the formulation.
