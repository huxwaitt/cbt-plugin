**Bottom line:** The five-part model maps one specific, recent episode as an environment or situation plus four interacting parts: thoughts, moods, physical reactions and behaviors. It is the usual first, descriptive formulation in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) because clients grasp it quickly and it shows that changing one part can change the others. Build it together, in the client's own words, from a concrete example.

## Components

Christine Padesky and Kathleen Mooney published the model in 1990 (the diagram itself is dated 1986) as a generic model to present in the first or second session. It shows four connected aspects of the person (thoughts, mood, biology and behavior) inside an environment that includes both current and past circumstances [Padesky-Mooney-1990]. Mind Over Mood names the biology part "physical reactions" [Greenberger-Padesky-2016]. Clinicians often call it the "hot cross bun" because of the drawing. The original presentation built the model from intake summaries; it is now often used to map one specific recent episode [Padesky-2020].

| Part | What belongs here | Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Environment or situation | Where, when, who, what happened; wider life stressors, culture, relationships | "When did this last happen? Where were you?" |
| Thoughts | Words, images, memories, predictions | "What went through your mind just then?" |
| Moods | Emotions, named in one word, rated 0 to 100 | "What did you feel? How strong, 0 to 100?" |
| Physical reactions | Body sensations, arousal, fatigue, sleep | "What did you notice in your body?" |
| Behaviors | What the person did or held back from doing | "What did you do next, or stop yourself doing?" |

A UK variant, the Five Areas model, uses life situation, relationships and practical problems; altered thinking; altered emotions; altered physical feelings or symptoms; and altered behavior or activity levels [Williams-Garland-2002].

## How it keeps the problem going

Every part influences every other part. Thoughts shape emotions and body states, these shape behavior, and behavior feeds back into thinking and mood [Williams-Garland-2002]. Example: invited to a party, the client thinks "It will be terrible," feels anxious and physically aroused, and stays home. Staying home brings relief but leaves the prediction untested.

The model describes how a problem is maintained, not why it began. Williams and Garland note that causes are multifactorial (life events, heredity, biology, learning) and that changing any one area tends to change the others [Williams-Garland-2002]. It is a descriptive formulation in the sense of the levels described by Kuyken and colleagues [Kuyken-etal-2008]; explanatory layers come later.

## Drawing it with the client

1. Choose one recent, specific episode that the client rates as typical and distressing. Avoid summaries such as "I'm always anxious."
2. Write the situation first, then fill the other parts in whatever order the client offers them.
3. Write the client's exact words. Rate mood and belief in key thoughts from 0 to 100.
4. Draw arrows between parts and ask the client to explain each link.
5. Ask what the client notices about the whole picture, then summarize.
6. Repeat with a second episode to see whether the pattern holds.
7. Set a small test as homework, such as charting mood and activity for a week, to find out whether the model fits the client's life [Padesky-Mooney-1990].

Padesky's version emphasizes standard questions that get the client to co-construct the model, using client language, and linking client strengths to the plan [Padesky-2020].

- If the client cannot name a thought → ask about images, or ask "What did that situation mean to you?" Because in the cognitive model the interpretation of a situation, expressed in thoughts or images, drives the reaction [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If the client describes a general pattern → ask for "the last time this happened" and work with that. Because a concrete episode gives specific, testable content [Padesky-2020].
- If the client mixes thoughts and feelings ("I felt like a failure") → write "failure" under thoughts and ask what emotion came with it. Because clients need to tell situations, automatic thoughts and emotions apart before the thoughts can be examined [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If the client says the problem is "biological" or "just my situation" → point to biology and environment on the diagram and show how thoughts and behavior join in. Because the model includes biological and environmental views and explains why changing thoughts and behavior can still help [Padesky-Mooney-1990].

Example phrasing: "Let's put what happened on paper so we can both see it. You tell me, I'll write." Avoid: "Here is your model," followed by a finished diagram.

## Treatment targets it implies

- **Thoughts**: identify and test key predictions (`cognitive.identifying-automatic-thoughts`, `cognitive.thought-records`).
- **Behaviors**: reduce avoidance and safety behaviors, test predictions with behavioral experiments, schedule activity (`behavioral.behavioral-experiments`, `behavioral.behavioral-activation`).
- **Physical reactions**: work on sleep, arousal and activity levels.
- **Environment**: problem-solve practical stressors and involve supports.
- **Moods**: track them as the outcome; they usually shift when other parts change [Williams-Garland-2002].

Pick the part the client finds clearest and most changeable first. Move to `03-formulation.maintenance-cycles` when the same loop recurs across episodes.
