**Bottom line:** A formulation the client did not help build is often not understood, not believed and not used, and it can be experienced as a judgment. Build it together in the client's words, offer every link as a guess, include strengths, and ask how it lands.

## What it looks like

- The therapist arrives with a typed diagram and talks through it for ten minutes.
- Boxes use terms like "schema," "safety behavior" or "compensatory strategy" that the client never used.
- The client says "I suppose so" and does not mention the formulation again.
- The client hears "you keep yourself depressed" and feels blamed.
- The therapist does not ask how the client feels about what they have seen.

## Why it happens

- Time pressure: drafting between sessions feels efficient.
- Wanting to look expert.
- Confusing psychoeducation with formulation.
- Not knowing how to hand over control of the pen.

Clients' reactions to formulation are mixed. Some are upset or overwhelmed by formulations in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and cognitive analytic therapy [Kuyken-etal-2008]. In a study in psychosis, six clients found formulation at least partly negative [Chadwick-etal-2003]. Clients in another study reported distress from formulations they saw as inaccurate or that touched their sense of identity [Redhead-etal-2015].

## The fix

- If you have drafted a formulation alone → do not present it; use it to guide your questions and let the client fill the boxes. Because competent conceptualization has the client actively engaged, with client and therapist ideas valued equally [Padesky-etal-2011].
- If you catch yourself using a technical term → swap in the client's phrase or a plain word. Because using the client's own language, metaphors and images is a marker of competence [Padesky-etal-2011].
- If you need to share a belief-level diagram → share a simplified version only. Because the full diagram is too confusing for most clients [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
- If the client seems to accept everything without comment → ask "What doesn't fit?" and "How is it to see this?" Because formulation should be discussed sensitively and adverse reactions watched for [Macneil-etal-2012; Redhead-etal-2015].
- If the client feels blamed → reframe each coping behavior as understandable, then look at its cost. Because feeling understood and accepted is one of the main benefits clients report [Redhead-etal-2015].

Example phrasing: "I've got some ideas, but you know your experience better than I do. Let's draw it together and you correct me." Avoid: "I've worked out what's going on with you."

## Sources note

Based on qualitative client studies, one controlled study in psychosis and expert consensus. The Collaborative Case Conceptualization Rating Scale (CCC-RS) gives an observable standard for collaboration.
