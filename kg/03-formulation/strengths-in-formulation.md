**Bottom line:** A formulation should explain what helps as well as what hurts. Look for strengths from the first session, record them in the formulation, and link at least one strength to each part of the plan. In later therapy, strengths can become a client-built personal model of resilience used for relapse prevention.

## When to use

Kuyken and colleagues recommend working with strengths at every level of conceptualization, from assessment on, because it serves both aims of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): relieving distress and building resilience [Kuyken-etal-2008]. Use this procedure:
- At assessment, alongside the problem list.
- When the formulation feels bleak to the client or therapist.
- In the final phase, to plan how the client will handle setbacks.

## Steps

1. **Ask about strengths directly at assessment.** Ask when the client coped well and what their best period of life was like. The Beck Institute case write-up records "best lifetime functioning, including strengths, assets and resources" [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
2. **Listen for hidden strengths.** Clients often describe strengths as faults. Kuyken's client Beth called it "attitude"; her therapist saw a willingness to take on challenges and stand her ground, which had helped her cope [Kuyken-etal-2008].
3. **Record strengths in the formulation.** Add a protective factors column [Macneil-etal-2012], or complete the strength-based cognitive conceptualization diagram: adaptive core beliefs, assumptions and coping from before the current difficulties [BeckInstitute-Worksheet-Packet-2020].
4. **Link strengths to the plan.** For each target, name the strength the client will use (persistence for graded tasks, humor for exposure, care for others as motivation).
5. **Build a personal model of resilience (later phase).** Padesky and Mooney's four-step strengths-based CBT model: search for strengths in an area where the client already persists; build a personal model of resilience from them, ideally in the client's own metaphor or image; apply it to a problem area; and practice it through behavioral experiments [Padesky-Mooney-2012].
6. **Review.** At each formulation review, ask what the client has done well and add it.

## What to record

A strengths list with examples, values and aspirations, supports the client agreed to involve, which strength is linked to which goal, and the personal model of resilience in the client's words.

## Guidance

- If the client is in acute distress → validate first and ask about strengths briefly and concretely ("How did you get here today?"). Because a strengths question asked too early can feel dismissive.
- If the client says "I don't have any strengths" → ask about an everyday area where they keep going despite difficulty (caring for a pet, a hobby, getting through a hard job). Because Padesky and Mooney start the search where the client already persists [Padesky-Mooney-2012].
- If the client names a strength → ask "What does that say about you?" and write the answer in their words. Because client-generated metaphors and current strengths are central to strengths-based CBT [Padesky-Mooney-2012].
- If the formulation is about to be shared → put at least one strength on the page. Because including protective factors is linked to optimism and the therapeutic relationship [Macneil-etal-2012].

Example phrasings: "When things were going well for you, what were you doing?" "You called it being stubborn. In this situation, it sounds like it kept you going."

## Common problems

- **Strengths listed but never used.** The Collaborative Case Conceptualization Rating Scale (CCC-RS) rates whether strengths inform the treatment plan, not only whether they appear [Padesky-etal-2011]. Fix: tie each strength to a goal.
- **Generic praise.** Fix: name specific behaviors and evidence.
- **Problem focus only.** Fix: discuss aspirations and positive goals, not just symptoms [Padesky-etal-2011].
- **Disorder model without wellbeing model.** Dudley and colleagues argue lasting improvement needs models of wellbeing and resilience as well as disorder [Dudley-etal-2011].

Evidence strength: expert consensus with a reliable competence measure; outcome trials of strengths-focused formulation are lacking.
