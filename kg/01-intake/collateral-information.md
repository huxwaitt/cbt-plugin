**Bottom line:** Collateral information from parents, partners, teachers or other clinicians fills gaps the client cannot fill, such as childhood behavior, how a problem looks from outside, or recent risk. Decide what question the informant can answer, get the client's informed permission (or the legally required permission for a minor), and treat disagreement between informants as information about context, not as a contest over who is right.

## When to use

- **Children and adolescents:** routine. Reports from different informants correlate only modestly (mean r = .28 across 341 studies), partly because children behave differently across settings [DeLosReyes-etal-2015].
- **Adult attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD):** an informant who knew the client as a child, usually a parent or close relative, where possible [DIVA-Site].
- **Communication or cognitive difficulties:** consider asking a family member or carer about symptoms [NICE-NG222].
- **Limited insight or recall,** for example suspected mania, psychosis, cognitive decline or heavy substance use.
- **Other providers:** prescriber, primary care clinician or previous therapist. Persons lists family members and other treatment providers among routine data sources for diagnosis and formulation [Persons-2008].

## Steps

1. **Name the question.** "I'd like to know what your mother remembers about how you were at school." A clear question keeps the contact short and focused.
2. **Discuss it with the client.** Explain who, why, what you will ask and what you will and will not share. Agree topics that stay private.
3. **Get valid permission.**
   - United States (US): under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule, a provider may share information directly relevant to a family member's or friend's involvement in care if the client agrees, or does not object when given the chance [HHS-45CFR164]. For other disclosures, use a written authorization naming the information, the recipient, the purpose, an expiry date or event, the client's signature, and the right to revoke [HHS-45CFR164].
   - Minors (US): a parent or guardian is usually the personal representative, but not where the minor lawfully consents to the service alone; state law then decides parental access [HHS-45CFR164].
   - People who cannot consent: give an explanation, seek assent, consider their preferences and best interests, and get permission from a legally authorized person [APA-Ethics-2017].
   - United Kingdom (UK): 16 and 17 year olds are presumed able to consent; under-16s can consent if Gillick competent [NHS-Consent-Children].
4. **Clarify roles in family work.** At the outset, say who the client is and what relationship you will have with each person [APA-Ethics-2017].
5. **Interview the informant.** Ask for specific examples, when they occur and where. The Cultural Formulation Interview has an informant version for family or caregivers [APA-CFI-Site].
6. **Integrate.** Note where reports agree and differ, and what setting each informant sees.

## What to record

- Who was contacted, relationship to client, date, method.
- The permission obtained (signed authorization or documented oral agreement) and its limits.
- What was shared with the informant and what they reported, labeled as their report.
- How discrepancies were interpreted.

## Guidance

- If the parent and child give different accounts → record both and ask where each sees the behavior. Because low agreement often reflects real differences across contexts [DeLosReyes-etal-2015].
- If an adult client refuses any collateral contact → respect it, note the gap, and revisit once trust grows. Because disclosure needs the client's agreement or authorization outside specific legal exceptions [HHS-45CFR164].
- If a family member calls you unprompted → you may listen, but do not confirm the person is your client without permission. Because confirming treatment is itself a disclosure (expert consensus).
- If an informant reports current danger to the client or others → treat it as a risk disclosure and follow `02-risk.crisis-response`. Because safety overrides routine information gathering.
- If an adolescent asks what you will tell parents → explain the limits before they disclose. "What you tell me stays between us unless I'm worried about your safety or someone else's. If that happens, I'll talk with you first about what I need to share." Because limits of confidentiality are discussed at the outset, including with parents or legal representatives where feasible [APA-Ethics-2017].

## Common problems

- **Informant becomes the main client.** Keep the session focused on the agreed question.
- **Parent expects full access to a teen's sessions.** Agree the confidentiality arrangement with both at the start [APA-Ethics-2017].
- **Treating the informant's report as fact.** It is one perspective; label it as such in the record.
