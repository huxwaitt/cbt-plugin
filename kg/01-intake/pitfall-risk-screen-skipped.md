**Bottom line:** The commonest intake risk failures are not asking directly, asking too late to act, and asking without following up. Ask every client plainly and early, treat any positive answer as the start of an assessment, and record what you asked, what you heard, your judgment and what you did.

## What it looks like

- "Any thoughts of harming yourself?" asked as the client stands to leave.
- Euphemisms ("You're not going to do anything silly?") that invite a "no".
- A self-report form with an endorsed suicide item that nobody reviewed during the session.
- Notes reading "risk assessed, low" with no questions, answers or reasons.
- A client who said "sometimes I'd rather not be here" and was given an appointment in two weeks with no safety plan or crisis numbers.

## Why it happens

- Fear that asking will upset the client or put the idea in their head.
- Discomfort and lack of practice with the conversation.
- Templates that treat risk as a tick box.
- Running out of time.

The fear is not supported by evidence: a review found no study showing a significant rise in suicidal ideation after asking, and talking about suicide may reduce it [Dazzi-etal-2014]. A randomized trial in high school students found no increase in distress or ideation, including among high-risk students [Gould-etal-2005].

## The fix

- If you have not yet asked by the middle of the intake → ask now, plainly. "Sometimes when people feel this low they have thoughts of ending their life. Have you had thoughts like that?" Because the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommends always asking directly about suicidal ideation and intent [NICE-NG222].
- If a form shows an endorsed risk item → review it with the client in the session before anything else. Because an unreviewed answer cannot be acted on, and asking directly is recommended [NICE-NG222].
- If the client says yes → ask about frequency, plans, intent, preparation and past attempts, begin a safety plan, check supports and sources of help, and advise what to do if things get worse. Because this is the recommended response when there is risk of self-harm or suicide [NICE-NG222].
- If risk appears considerable and immediate → refer urgently to specialist services or emergency care the same day, and give crisis contacts. Because urgent referral is recommended for considerable immediate risk [NICE-NG222]. Contacts: United States 988 by call or text, or 911 [Lifeline-988]; United Kingdom 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 mental health option, Samaritans 116 123 [NHS-Urgent-Help].
- If you are documenting → record the estimate of risk and the factors influencing it, not a bare "low" label. Because guidelines ask for the factors [APA-PsychEval-2016; Armstrong-2016], and NICE advises against low, medium or high stratification to predict suicide or decide who gets treatment [NICE-NG225].
- If you are unsure → consult a supervisor or senior colleague before the client leaves. Because the client may not be reachable later, and arranging help matched to need is the standard [NICE-NG222].

## Sources note

Recommendations to ask directly and act on risk are guideline-based [NICE-NG222; APA-PsychEval-2016]. UK guidance also advises against using low, medium or high risk labels to predict suicide [NICE-NG225]. Evidence that asking is safe comes from a literature review [Dazzi-etal-2014] and a randomized trial in schools [Gould-etal-2005]. The failure patterns listed are expert consensus from supervision practice.
