**Bottom line:** Psychological symptoms can be caused or maintained by physical illness, prescribed or over-the-counter medicines, alcohol or drugs, or an unrecognized bipolar disorder. Ask about all four at every intake, use brief validated screens, and refer for medical review when the picture suggests it, before concluding that cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) alone is the answer.

## What it looks like

- Panic-like symptoms treated with exposure while a new stimulant, a supplement or heavy caffeine use goes unasked.
- Depression treated with CBT in a client who had a past episode of high mood and little sleep that nobody asked about.
- Insomnia work that never addresses nightly alcohol.
- Fatigue and low mood with no recent physical check and no primary care clinician on file.
- Treatment that stalls for many sessions before anyone revisits the history.

## Why it happens

- Therapists may not feel competent to ask medical questions and skip them.
- Clients do not volunteer substance use or think medicines are irrelevant.
- The referral says "depression", and the intake confirms rather than tests it.
- No intake form for medications and medical history.

## The fix

- If you have no medication list → ask about all current and recent medicines, including nonprescribed and supplement products, and their side effects. Because this is a recommended part of initial evaluation [APA-PsychEval-2016].
- If the client has no primary care clinician → encourage registration and a physical review. Because establishing that relationship is recommended [Armstrong-2016].
- If you have not asked about substances → use the single-question screens. Because they are brief and accurate in primary care: past-year heavy drinking days (5 or more drinks for men, 4 for women) identified unhealthy alcohol use with 81.8% sensitivity and 79.3% specificity [Smith-etal-2009]; any past-year illegal or nonmedical prescription drug use was 100% sensitive for a drug use disorder [Smith-etal-2010].
- If the client has depression → ask about any history of mood elevation before choosing treatment. Because depression may be part of bipolar disorder [NICE-NG222].
- If symptoms are atypical (sudden onset late in life, neurological signs, weight loss, symptoms tied to a new medicine) → refer for medical assessment and coordinate with the prescriber. Because treatment plans should take physical health problems into account [NICE-NG222].
- If you are not medically trained → stay within scope: screen, document, and refer rather than interpret tests or advise on medicines. Because who may diagnose medical conditions or advise on medicines depends on license.

## Sources note

The domains to assess are drawn from the American Psychiatric Association adult evaluation guideline (summarized by Armstrong) and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) depression guidance [APA-PsychEval-2016; Armstrong-2016; NICE-NG222]. Screen accuracy figures are from primary care validation studies [Smith-etal-2009; Smith-etal-2010] and may differ in mental health settings. The case patterns are expert consensus.
