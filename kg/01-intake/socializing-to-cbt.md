**Bottom line:** Clients new to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) do not know they will be asked to report on their week, set an agenda, give feedback, summarize, and practice between sessions [Beck-2021]. Teach the cognitive model through the client's own recent example rather than a lecture, explain why sessions are structured and why practice matters, and invite the client into an active, collaborative role. Then check understanding and adjust.

## The principle

The cognitive model says that how people interpret a situation shapes how they feel and what they do; thoughts, moods, behaviors and physical reactions interact within the person's environment [Padesky-Mooney-1990; Greenberger-Padesky-2016]. CBT is structured, present focused, time sensitive, collaborative and educative, and it uses action plans (homework) [BeckInstitute-Site]. Socialization means the client understands and agrees to this way of working. Preparing clients for what therapy involves reduces early dropout [Walitzer-etal-1999].

## Guidance

- If you are in the first therapy session → say why you structure the session, describe each part, give the rationale, and give gentle corrective feedback as the client learns it. Because this is how Beck recommends introducing structure [Beck-2021].
- If the client has a recent upsetting moment → use it to draw the model: situation, then thought, then emotion, body and behavior, and ask how each links to the others. Because a personal example makes the model the client's own discovery rather than theory [Padesky-Mooney-1990].
- If the client says "So you're saying it's all in my head" → validate the real situation and add that the meaning they give it also affects how they feel. Because the model includes environment and biology, not only thoughts [Padesky-Mooney-1990].
- If the client shows a negative shift when you describe the structure → ask what went through their mind, compare with what helped before, and propose a trial with a review. Because beliefs about therapy or the therapist can block engagement [Beck-2021].
- If the client asks why homework matters → link practice to their goal and explain that skills grow between sessions. Because both the amount and quality of homework completion relate to CBT outcome [Kazantzis-etal-2016].
- If the client has had nondirective therapy before → name the difference openly and invite a trial. Because some clients expect to talk freely and may object to structure [Beck-2021].
- If pressing for structure seems likely to cost the client's engagement early on → offer to split the session time between their topic and a structured task, then review together whether it helped. Because getting the client to return comes first [Beck-2021].
- If the client agrees to everything but says little → ask for a summary in their own words. Because collaboration, not compliance, predicts outcome (r = .29) [Tryon-etal-2018].
- If the client asks how long therapy lasts → give the typical range for their problem and say you will review progress together. Because guidelines give typical ranges: for depression the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) describes individual CBT as usually 8 sessions (less severe) or 16 sessions (more severe) and notes the person needs to be willing to do homework [NICE-NG222].

## Example phrasings

- Drawing the model: "Let's put Tuesday on paper. The situation was your boss walking past without saying hello. What went through your mind? ... And when you thought 'She's annoyed with me,' what did you feel? ... What did you notice in your body? ... What did you do next?"
- Checking the link: "If you had thought 'She's distracted today,' how might you have felt?"
- Rationale for structure: "We'll start each session by checking in and choosing together what to focus on. It helps us use the time on what matters most to you. Tell me if it ever feels too rigid."
- Rationale for practice: "What we do in here is like a lesson. The change happens when you try it in your week."
- Collaboration: "You're the expert on your life and I know some things about how these problems work. We'll figure it out together."
- Feedback: "Was there anything today that didn't sit right, or that I got wrong?"
- Avoid: "Your thoughts are distorted." It sounds like blame and skips discovery.
- Avoid: "CBT is proven, so just trust the process." It replaces a rationale with authority.

## Evidence

- Preparing clients through role induction and pretraining reduces early attrition and may be especially effective in groups at high risk of dropout (narrative review) [Walitzer-etal-1999]. In a later randomized trial in outpatient alcohol treatment, a single role induction session gave no significant advantage over no preparation, while a motivational interview did [Connors-etal-2002].
- Homework completion relates to outcome at posttreatment (quantity g = 0.79; quality g = 0.78) in a meta-analysis of 17 CBT studies; the quality estimate rests on only 3 studies with a wide confidence interval [Kazantzis-etal-2016].
- Patient-therapist collaboration (r = .29) and goal consensus (r = .24) predict outcome across psychotherapies [Tryon-etal-2018].
- The specific socialization methods above are expert consensus drawn from treatment manuals [Beck-2021; Padesky-Mooney-1990].
