**Bottom line:** Goals the client and therapist agree on, a hopeful but realistic expectation of what therapy will do, and a treatment that respects the client's preferences all predict better outcomes. At intake, ask what the client hopes will change, translate it into concrete goals in their words, ask what they expect therapy to involve, and check how ready they feel to act.

## When to use

- Late in the evaluation session, once the problem list exists.
- Again when goals drift, progress stalls, or the client seems to be attending without engaging.

## Steps

1. **Ask about hopes.** "If our work together went really well, what would be different in three months? What would you be doing that you are not doing now?" The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks clinicians to discuss what people hope to gain from treatment [NICE-NG222].
2. **Make goals concrete.** Turn "feel better" into observable change: "go back to the gym twice a week", "take the bus to work alone". Phrase goals as something present, not as an absence of distress.
3. **Make them meaningful.** Ask why each goal matters. Persons stresses goals that are emotionally meaningful to the client and clearly tied to the interventions [Persons-2008].
4. **Rate and prioritize.** Rate how far the client is from each goal now (0 to 10) and pick one or two to start. These ratings become an idiographic outcome measure you can repeat at reviews (see `08-measurement.idiographic-measures`).
5. **Check expectations.**
   - Outcome: "How much do you think therapy can help with this?"
   - Process: "What do you imagine sessions will be like? What will your part be?"
   - Past therapy: what helped, what did not [NICE-NG222].
6. **Ask about preferences.** Format (individual, group, in person, remote), timing, therapist gender, involving a family member [NICE-NG222].
7. **Gauge readiness.** Ask how important change is and how confident the client feels, each on a 0 to 10 scale, and what would move the number up by one. Motivational interviewing describes four tasks, engaging, focusing, evoking and planning, which fit this conversation [Miller-Rollnick-2023].

## What to record

- Goals in the client's words, with current ratings.
- Expectations of outcome and of the process, and any mismatch you addressed.
- Preferences stated and how they were accommodated or why not.
- Importance and confidence ratings and the client's reasons.

## Guidance

- If the client's goal is "never feel anxious again" → acknowledge the wish and negotiate a goal about doing what matters while anxiety comes and goes. Because concrete, achievable goals support goal consensus, which predicts outcome (r = .24, d = .49 across 54 studies) [Tryon-etal-2018].
- If the client expects to get better just by attending → explain gently that most change happens between sessions. Because unrealistic hopes of improving without doing the work interfere with engagement [Beck-2021].
- If the client doubts therapy can help → validate the doubt, share the evidence for their problem honestly, and propose a short trial with review. Because more optimistic early outcome expectations predict better outcomes (r = .18, d = .36 across 81 samples) [Constantino-etal-2018a].
- If the client prefers a different format or approach → accommodate where you can. Because accommodating preferences was linked to fewer dropouts (odds ratio 1.79) and better outcomes (d = .28) [Swift-etal-2018].
- If importance or confidence is low → explore ambivalence before assigning tasks. Because readiness for change predicts outcome (d = 0.41 across 76 studies) [Krebs-etal-2018].
- If a goal is about changing someone else → reframe it toward what the client can control. "You can't make him listen. What would you like to be able to do when he doesn't?" Because goals should be meaningful to the client and clearly tied to the interventions [Persons-2008], and therapy cannot intervene on someone who is not in the room.

Example phrasing: "What would tell you, in an ordinary week, that therapy had worked?"

Avoid: "Your goal is to reduce your depression score." It is your measure, not their goal.

## Common problems

- **Too many goals.** Keep a full list, but start with one or two.
- **Goals that are symptoms.** Pair each symptom target with a life change the client wants.
- **Collaboration only on paper.** Patient-therapist collaboration also predicts outcome (r = .29) [Tryon-etal-2018]; revisit goals at review points.
