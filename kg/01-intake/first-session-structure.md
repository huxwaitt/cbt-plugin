**Bottom line:** The first contact has four jobs: obtain informed consent (including the limits of confidentiality), understand the problem well enough to screen risk and judge whether cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) fits, give the client a first taste of structured, collaborative CBT, and end with hope and a clear next step. Many services split this into an evaluation session followed by a first therapy session [Beck-2021]. Whatever the format, consent and the risk screen come before depth.

## When to use

- The first meeting with any new client, in person or by video.
- Planning an intake when the "pretreatment" phase (assessment, diagnosis, initial case formulation, treatment plan and consent to it) will span the first few sessions [Persons-2008].

## Steps

1. **Before the session.** Read the referral for risk flags. Send intake forms and baseline questionnaires so session time goes to conversation. For video sessions, confirm the client's location and an emergency contact first.
2. **Open and orient.** Introduce yourself, give the time available and the plan for today. "Today is mostly about me understanding what has brought you here, so we can decide together whether CBT is a good fit."
3. **Consent and confidentiality.** Explain the nature and likely course of therapy, fees, any third parties, and the limits of confidentiality, and invite questions, as early as is feasible [APA-Ethics-2017]. Discuss confidentiality at the outset unless that is not feasible or is contraindicated [APA-Ethics-2017]. When a supervisor holds legal responsibility, a trainee says they are in training and names the supervisor [APA-Ethics-2017].
4. **Client's account first.** Let the client tell the story in their own words for a few minutes, then move to focused questions (see `01-intake.presenting-problem`).
5. **Risk screen early.** Ask directly about suicidal thoughts [NICE-NG222]. Asking has not been found to raise suicidal ideation [Dazzi-etal-2014]. Doing it in the first half leaves time to act on a positive answer.
6. **Prioritized history and measures.** Cover what the decision in front of you needs; collect the rest by form or at the next session.
7. **Goals, hopes and preferences.** Ask what the client hopes to gain and what they have found helpful before [NICE-NG222].
8. **Initial shared understanding.** Summarize, then link thoughts, feelings and actions using one of the client's own examples (see `01-intake.socializing-to-cbt`).
9. **Agree the next step.** Offer CBT with a plan, a further assessment session, or a referral. Describe what sessions will look like and why they are structured [Beck-2021]. Set one small, doable task.
10. **Ask for feedback.** "What was today like for you? Was there anything I got wrong or that bothered you?"

## What to record

- What was explained for consent, that questions were invited, and the client's agreement (written or oral).
- Risk screen questions, answers, your risk judgment and any action taken.
- Presenting problem summary, provisional impressions, baseline scores.
- Goals, preferences, agreed plan and first task.
- Client feedback and anything left to cover next time.

## Guidance

- If the client starts a detailed story before consent → acknowledge it warmly, pause, and cover confidentiality briefly first. Because the confidentiality discussion belongs at the start of the relationship [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If the client discloses current suicidal thoughts at any point → stop the planned sequence and move to a full suicide risk assessment. Because direct inquiry and arranging help matched to need are recommended [NICE-NG222].
- If the client is very distressed or the picture is complex → prioritize safety and engagement and book a second assessment session. Because the pretreatment phase can take several sessions [Persons-2008].
- If the client asks how long CBT takes → give a realistic range tied to their problem and promise a joint review. Because guideline estimates exist: for depression, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) describes individual CBT as usually 8 sessions for less severe and 16 for more severe episodes, with more for complex needs [NICE-NG222].
- If services are court ordered or otherwise mandated → explain the mandate and its confidentiality limits before proceeding. Because this is required before mandated services begin [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If the client seems uneasy about a structured session → name the structure, give the reason, and offer to try it and review. Because clients new to CBT do not know what to expect and a rationale is often enough [Beck-2021].

Example opening: "Before we get into what's going on, I want to explain how confidentiality works here, because there are a few limits you should know about."

Avoid: "I just need to get through these forms first." It tells the client the paperwork matters more than they do.

## Common problems

- **Running out of time before the plan.** About one in five adult clients drops out of therapy, and dropout is higher in younger clients and with trainee therapists [Swift-Greenberg-2012]. A narrative review found that preparing clients for what treatment involves reduces early attrition [Walitzer-etal-1999]. Protect the final part of the session for the plan and feedback (expert consensus).
- **The session becomes a questionnaire.** See `01-intake.pitfall-interrogation-intake`.
- **Risk left to the end.** A positive screen at minute 55 cannot be handled safely. Screen in the first half.
- **No hope offered.** End with a brief, honest statement of what CBT can offer for this problem and what happens next.
