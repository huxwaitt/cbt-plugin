**Bottom line:** An intake that only collects data can lose the client before therapy starts. Use forms for facts, the interview for understanding, and protect time for a first shared picture of the problem, a dose of realistic hope, and an agreed next step.

## What it looks like

- The therapist works down a template, question by question, often typing.
- The client gives short answers; there is little emotion in the room.
- No recent, specific episode is explored, so no link between thoughts, feelings and actions is made.
- The session ends at the risk or history section, with "We'll continue next time" and no plan or rationale.
- The client does not attend the second appointment, or attends but seems unsure why.

## Why it happens

- Service pressure to complete mandatory fields in one sitting.
- Anxiety about missing something, especially in trainees.
- A belief that engagement starts after assessment. Persons describes relationship building as starting with the first telephone call and running through the whole pretreatment phase [Persons-2008].
- No forms sent in advance, so session time goes to factual questions.

The cost is real. About one in five adult clients drops out of therapy, with higher rates among younger clients and those seen by trainees [Swift-Greenberg-2012]. Optimistic early outcome expectations predict better outcomes [Constantino-etal-2018a], and an intake that offers no hope does nothing to build them.

## The fix

- If mandatory fields are many → send forms and questionnaires before the session and review them, rather than reading them aloud. Because interview time is best spent on meaning, risk and engagement.
- If you notice you have asked several closed questions in a row → switch to an open question and a reflection. "That sounds exhausting. What's the hardest part of a day like that?" Because open questions and reflections invite the client's own account (expert consensus).
- If time is running short → move to a summary, one link between thought, feeling and behavior, and a plan; defer the rest of the history. Because preparing clients for what treatment involves reduces early attrition [Walitzer-etal-1999].
- If the client seems flat or disengaged → ask for feedback directly. Because Beck models asking clients what they thought of the session and whether anything bothered them, then reinforcing negative feedback [Beck-2021].
- If you are a trainee → plan with your supervisor which items can wait for session two. Because dropout is higher among clients seen by trainees [Swift-Greenberg-2012].

Before the client leaves, they should be able to say what you understood about their problem, what happens next, and why it might help.

## Sources note

Dropout figures come from a meta-analysis of 669 studies [Swift-Greenberg-2012]; the expectation finding is from a meta-analysis of 81 samples [Constantino-etal-2018a]. The link between interrogative intake style and dropout specifically is expert consensus, not tested directly in trials.
