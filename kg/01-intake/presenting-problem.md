**Bottom line:** First get a short list of every problem the client wants help with, then, for the main ones, establish onset, course, triggers, frequency, severity and impact on daily life. Then walk through one recent, specific episode moment by moment: situation, thoughts and images, emotions with ratings, body sensations, and what the client did. That one concrete example is the raw material for the first formulation.

## When to use

- In the evaluation session, after the client has told the story in their own words.
- Whenever the account stays general ("I'm anxious all the time") and you cannot yet see what happens in a specific moment.

## Steps

1. **Open, then summarize.** "What has brought you here now?" Listen, then reflect back the main themes in two or three sentences and check you have them right.
2. **Build a problem list.** Ask about every area of life, including housing, money, work, relationships, health and legal problems, not only symptoms. Persons builds formulation from a comprehensive problem list across all domains [Persons-2008].
3. **For each main problem, map the shape.**
   - Onset: "When did this start? What was going on in your life then?"
   - Course: steady, worsening, episodic? Previous episodes?
   - "Why now": what made the client seek help at this point.
   - Triggers: situations, people, times, body states that set it off.
   - Frequency and duration: how often, how long each time.
   - Severity: a 0 to 10 or 0 to 100 rating of distress at its worst and on average.
   - Impact: what the client has stopped, avoids, or does with difficulty at work, at home, socially and in self-care. Assessment should weigh severity, duration, course and functional impairment, not just count symptoms [NICE-NG222].
   - What they have tried and what happened, including avoidance and things done "to be safe".
4. **Add a standardized measure.** Use a validated measure of symptoms or functioning to inform and later evaluate treatment [NICE-NG222]; the American Psychiatric Association also suggests quantitative measures of symptoms, functioning and quality of life at initial evaluation [APA-PsychEval-2016; Armstrong-2016].
5. **Get one recent, specific example.** "Can you think of a time in the last week when this was bad? Take me back to it." Then slow it down:
   - Situation: where, when, who, what had just happened.
   - Thoughts and images: "What went through your mind right then? Any pictures in your mind?"
   - Emotions: name each and rate intensity 0 to 100.
   - Body: "What did you notice in your body?"
   - Behavior: "What did you do? What did you feel like doing?"
   - Consequence: what happened next and how it felt afterward.
6. **Reflect it back in five parts.** Thoughts, moods, behavior and physical reactions interact, within the client's life circumstances (environment) [Padesky-Mooney-1990; Greenberger-Padesky-2016]. Say it in the client's words and check fit.

Early formulation is mostly descriptive: helping the client describe the problem in cognitive and behavioral terms comes before explanation of triggers and maintaining factors [Kuyken-etal-2008].

## What to record

- Numbered problem list in the client's words, each with onset, frequency, a severity rating and main impact.
- The "why now" factor.
- At least one specific episode in five parts, with emotion ratings and verbatim key thoughts in quotation marks.
- Coping attempts, avoidance and safety behaviors noticed.
- Baseline questionnaire scores.

## Guidance

- If the client answers in generalities ("it's always bad") → ask for the most recent clear example and slow it down. Because specific episodes give the data a formulation needs, and early formulation is descriptive [Kuyken-etal-2008].
- If the client says "I feel that I'm useless" → reflect it as a thought and ask what emotion went with it. Because separating thoughts from emotions is the first skill of the five-part model [Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
- If the client cannot recall any thoughts → ask about images, about what the moment meant, or replay it in imagination. "If you picture yourself there now, what is going through your mind?" Because automatic thoughts often occur as images or felt meanings rather than words (expert consensus).
- If there are many problems → list them all briefly first, then ask which matters most and which causes most interference. Because a full list prevents missed problems and supports prioritizing [Persons-2008].
- If the client mentions current suicidal thoughts while describing impact → pause the problem interview and complete the risk screen (see `01-intake.risk-screen-at-intake`). Because guidelines call for asking directly about suicidal ideation and intent and arranging help matched to need [NICE-NG222].

Example phrasing: "Let's take just that moment in the kitchen on Tuesday. When he said that, what was the very first thing that went through your mind?"

Avoid: "So basically you have depression." It closes the description before you have it.

## Common problems

- **Example too old or too vague.** Ask for one from the last week or two; it will be richer.
- **Only emotions, no behavior.** Ask directly what the client did next and what they avoided. Behavior is often the easiest target for early change.
- **Ratings feel strange to the client.** Anchor the scale: "0 is none at all, 100 is the most you have ever felt."
- **Staying abstract.** See `01-intake.pitfall-general-not-specific`.
