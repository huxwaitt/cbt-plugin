**Bottom line:** General descriptions ("I'm always anxious", "I have no confidence") tell you a theme but not what happens. Without at least one recent, specific episode slowed down into situation, thoughts, emotions, body and behavior, the first formulation is guesswork and the client cannot see the cognitive model in their own life.

## What it looks like

- Notes say "generalized worry, low self-esteem, relationship issues" with no example.
- The therapist asks "How often do you feel like that?" and gets "All the time."
- Thoughts are recorded as feelings ("feels like a failure") and behavior is missing.
- The therapist offers a diagnostic label or a textbook model instead of the client's own sequence.
- In the next session the client cannot connect homework to anything that happened in the first.

## Why it happens

- Clients in distress summarize; specific memories take effort and can feel exposing.
- Therapists move to explanation too early. Kuyken and colleagues note that therapists agree on descriptive levels of formulation, but reliability is poor at levels requiring more inference, such as beliefs and maintaining factors [Kuyken-etal-2008]. Starting with inference before description adds error.
- Time pressure pushes toward checklists rather than one slow example.

## The fix

- If the client answers with "always" or "never" → ask for the most recent clear time. "When was the last time it was really bad? Take me back to that moment." Because one specific episode supplies the descriptive data early formulation needs [Kuyken-etal-2008].
- If the example is vague → anchor it: day, place, who was there, what happened just before. Because concrete cues help the client recall the moment rather than a summary (expert consensus).
- If the client gives a feeling that is really a thought ("I feel like nobody cares") → reflect it as a thought and ask for the emotion. Because the five-part model separates thoughts, moods, behaviors, physical reactions and environment [Padesky-Mooney-1990; Greenberger-Padesky-2016].
- If no thoughts come → ask about images or the meaning of the moment: "What did that say about you?" Because automatic thoughts often occur as images or meanings rather than words (expert consensus).
- If you are tempted to label → describe first. Because Persons describes the initial formulation as a hypothesis to be tested and revised as information comes in [Persons-2008].
- Before ending the intake → write one episode in five parts with the client and check: "Does this fit what happens for you?"

## Sources note

The five-part structure is from Padesky and Mooney's presentation of the cognitive model [Padesky-Mooney-1990]. The claim that descriptive formulation is more reliable than inferential formulation comes from research reviewed by Kuyken and colleagues [Kuyken-etal-2008]. The specific questioning tips are expert consensus.
