**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) by live video appears about as effective as in person, and telephone CBT shows promise for depression and anxiety [Fernandez-etal-2021; Coughtrey-Pistrang-2018]. Keep the usual session structure, and add three routines: a safety and location check at the start of every session, a privacy check, and a backup plan for dropped connections [Chiauzzi-etal-2020]. Expect to work harder to read emotion and convey warmth, because therapists tend to show lower skill levels in teletherapy [Lin-etal-2025].

## The principle

Remote CBT changes the channel, not the model. The mood check, agenda, action plan review, focused work, summary and feedback all carry over. What changes is that the therapist cannot see the whole room, cannot reach the client in a crisis, and has fewer nonverbal cues. Those gaps are closed with explicit routines agreed at the start of treatment. The American Psychological Association (APA) updated its telepsychology guidelines in 2024, expanding the eight 2013 guidelines to 11: competence, informed consent, data security and transmission, data disposal, documentation, interjurisdictional practice, clinical best practices, testing and assessment, emergencies, supervision and training, and emerging technologies [Perle-etal-2026].

## Guidance

Setup at the first remote session:
- If starting remote work with a new or transferring client → agree in writing the client's usual location, an emergency contact, the nearest emergency service, and a phone number to call if video fails. Because a dropped connection during distress must not leave the client alone, and these steps mirror in-person safety planning [Chiauzzi-etal-2020].
- If the client has a history of suicidal thoughts or self-harm → complete or update a safety plan before remote sessions begin, and confirm access to crisis lines: in the United States 988 (call or text) or 911; in the United Kingdom 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans 116 123. Because in a small review (nine studies) of telemental health delivered to homes, the two studies with safety incidents resolved them with prescribed safety procedures [Luxton-etal-2010]. See `02-risk.safety-planning`.

Start of every session:
- If the session is starting → confirm the client's current location and that they can talk privately, before the mood check. Because emergency response depends on where the client is that day, and in the United States licensure generally follows the client's location [Chiauzzi-etal-2020]. See `14-ethics-law.telehealth-law`.
- If the client cannot speak privately (housemates, partner nearby) → offer yes or no questions, the chat box, headphones, a later time, or a phone call. Because privacy limits what clients disclose, and phone calls can be safer for people living with an abusive partner [Chiauzzi-etal-2020].

During the session:
- If you cannot read the client's emotion on screen → ask more often and directly: "What are you feeling right now, as we talk about this?" Because bandwidth, framing and camera angle distort expressions [Chiauzzi-etal-2020].
- If you notice yourself less warm or less precise than in person → slow down, summarize more, and name empathy out loud. Because observer ratings show lower common-factor skills in teletherapy for most therapists [Lin-etal-2025].
- If you need to set the agenda or work through a worksheet → share your screen or use a shared document, and send the written action plan by secure message afterward. Because visual structure helps the client follow and remember.
- If the connection drops → call the agreed number at once; if you cannot reach a client who was at risk, follow the emergency plan: call the emergency contact, or emergency services for the client's confirmed location. Because an interrupted connection at a critical moment can block a needed intervention [Chiauzzi-etal-2020].

Choosing video or phone:
- If the client prefers phone, lacks bandwidth or feels self-conscious on camera → phone is a reasonable choice for many CBT tasks. Because no evidence clearly favors video over audio-only for outcome [Chiauzzi-etal-2020].
- If treatment needs observation (in-session exposure, checking a body posture, a young child) → prefer video. Because some tasks depend on seeing the client.

## Example phrasings

- "Before we start, can you confirm where you are today, and that you can talk freely?"
- "If we get cut off, I'll call you on your mobile straight away. If I can't reach you and I'm worried about your safety, I'll contact the person we agreed."
- "I can't read faces as well on video, so I may check in more often about how you're feeling."
- "I'll put our agenda on the screen so we can both see it."
- Avoid: starting straight into content without the location and privacy check, even with a familiar client.

## Evidence

- Video: 47 between-group studies found a negligible difference from in-person therapy; CBT for affective disorders showed the strongest results (meta-analysis) [Fernandez-etal-2021].
- Telehealth versus face-to-face for less common conditions: 12 randomized trials with 931 clients showed no difference in symptoms (standardized mean difference 0.05) or alliance after treatment. The authors note this is insufficient evidence of a difference, not proof of equivalence [Greenwood-etal-2022].
- Telephone: 14 studies, median Cohen's d = 0.61 in controlled studies; only 4 reported clinically significant change [Coughtrey-Pistrang-2018].
- Skill: in simulations with 223 therapists, skills were lower in teletherapy than in person; about 35% were teletherapy experts and about 11% struggled markedly (emerging evidence) [Lin-etal-2025].
