**Bottom line:** Homework (between-session practice, called an "action plan" in Beck's current text [Beck-2021]) is how learning in session transfers to daily life, and it adds measurably to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) outcomes [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024]. Tasks get done when they are designed with the client, flow from the session, carry a clear reason, are specific about when and where, and have obstacles planned for in advance [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024; Young-Beck-1980].

## When to use

- During each agenda item, as soon as a useful conclusion or skill emerges, and again at the end of the session to confirm the full plan.
- When a client has a history of not completing tasks: design matters more, not less.

## Steps

1. **Link to the session.** Choose a task that tests, practices or extends what was just learned. Assignments should follow logically from the problems discussed [Young-Beck-1980].
2. **Design it together.** Ask what the client thinks would help, and fit the task to their goals and values [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024]. As therapy progresses the client should design more of each task [Young-Beck-1980].
3. **Give the rationale.** Say what the task will show or build and how that connects to the client's goal [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
4. **Make it specific.** Define how, when, where, how long, how often and whether anyone else is involved [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
5. **Add an if-then plan.** "If it's 7 pm after dinner, then I'll fill in one column." Forming implementation intentions had a large effect on goal attainment in clinical and analogue samples, d = 0.99 across 28 experimental tests after excluding one outlier (meta-analysis) [Toli-etal-2016].
6. **Anticipate obstacles.** Ask the client to picture doing the task and name what might get in the way, then problem-solve [Young-Beck-1980]. Ask "Does it sound useful? Manageable? Clear?" [Young-Beck-1980].
7. **Frame it as a no-lose experiment.** Whatever happens, there is something to learn, including from not doing it [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
8. **Write it down.** Give a written summary with instructions and the reason; keep a copy for the record [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024; Young-Beck-1980].

Typical tasks include thought records, activity scheduling with mastery and pleasure ratings, reviewing session notes, reading, listening to a session recording, and trying a feared or new behavior [Young-Beck-1980].

## What to record

- The exact task, its rationale, and when and where it will happen.
- The if-then plan and the obstacles discussed.
- The client's confidence or reservations, in their words.

## Guidance

- If the client looks doubtful when the task is proposed → ask what would make it more doable and shrink it until they are confident. Because overly demanding or unclear tasks raise pressure and resistance, and client feedback belongs in the design [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
- If the word "homework" brings up school memories or fear of failure → call it an action plan or practice. Because the word itself can trigger evaluation anxiety [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
- If the client cannot say why the task matters → ask them to state the rationale in their own words before leaving. Because a task without a reason competes poorly with daily demands [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the task depends on a skill learned today → rehearse the first step in session. Because starting in the room surfaces confusion while you can still fix it (expert consensus).
- If the client has low literacy, little time or limited privacy → use voice notes, phone reminders, pictures or a single rating instead of written forms. See `13-context-culture-development.intellectual-disability-and-low-literacy`. Because tasks should stay responsive to the client's needs and context [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].

Example phrasings:
- "What would be a small step this week that would tell us whether that prediction holds?"
- "When exactly could you do this, and what might get in the way?"
- "Whether it goes well or not, we'll learn something. If it doesn't happen at all, that's useful information too."
- Avoid: "Try to do some thought records this week." It is vague about what, when and why.

## Common problems

- **Assigned at the last minute.** Tasks rushed into the final minute are often unclear; set them during the agenda item they come from.
- **Therapist-designed only.** Clients engage more with tasks they helped shape [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
- **Too large.** Start with a task the client is confident they can complete, then grow it.

Evidence context:
- Homework versus no homework: a medium causal effect, d = 0.53, from two meta-analyses of controlled comparisons (Kazantzis and colleagues, 2000 and 2010), as summarized in [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
- Completion and outcome: r = 0.26 across 23 studies and 2,183 participants; effects were larger with retrospective and combined client and therapist ratings [Mausbach-etal-2010].
- Quantity and quality: at posttreatment, completion quantity g = 0.79 (15 studies) and quality g = 0.78 (only 3 studies, wide interval) [Kazantzis-etal-2016]. Quality evidence is thin.
