**Bottom line:** The agenda is a short, shared list of what this session will work on, set at the start, named rather than discussed, and limited to one or two target problems [Young-Beck-1980; Beck-2021]. Prioritize by risk, distress, stage of therapy and the chance of progress. Follow it by default, and change it openly and together when something more important appears.

## When to use

- After the mood check and bridge in every treatment session.
- Whenever the session drifts and needs re-anchoring ("Let's check we're still on what matters most today").

## Steps

1. **Invite items.** "What would you like help with today?" Use the client's treatment goals list as a prompt if needed [Beck-2021].
2. **Name, don't discuss.** Turn each item into a short label ("reconnecting with your brother"). Avoid discussing the content of an item until the list is complete [Young-Beck-1980].
3. **Add therapist items.** Usually the action plan review, plus anything planned: a formulation point, a skill to teach, risk follow-up or relapse prevention.
4. **Prioritize together.** Weigh stage of therapy, severity, any suicidal wishes, distress tied to each item, likelihood of progress and how many life areas the item touches [Young-Beck-1980].
5. **Limit and order.** Pick two items, possibly a third, and agree roughly how much time each gets [Beck-2021]. It is usually impossible to cover more than one or two target problems well [Young-Beck-1980].
6. **Keep it brief.** When done well, agenda setting takes about five minutes [Young-Beck-1980].
7. **Revisit when needed.** If an item turns out less important or not yet workable, switch by joint decision after stating the reason [Young-Beck-1980].

Early in treatment, and with more severe depression, behavioral targets tend to be more useful than purely cognitive ones; broader themes come later [Young-Beck-1980].

## What to record

- The items agreed and their order, including any the therapist added.
- Items deferred to next session (these become next week's first candidates).
- Any switch during the session and its reason.

## Guidance

- If the client rambles while naming items → gently interrupt, summarize what you heard as one or two labels, and ask if anything is more important. Because the client usually just needs socializing to "name it, don't tell it" [Beck-2021].
- If the client keeps describing rather than naming items over several sessions → make "jot down agenda topics before session" part of the action plan. Because preparing items in advance shortens agenda setting [Beck-2021].
- If the client says "I don't know" → offer prompts: the goals list, "How would you like the next few days to be better?", "When was the past week hardest?", or the highest scale items. Because a concrete menu is easier than an open question [Beck-2021].
- If the client feels hopeless or overwhelmed → agree that the problems are real, that they can be taken one at a time, and ask which one would help most if worked on today. Because a forced choice moves the client into problem-solving without dismissing the load [Beck-2021].
- If the client still refuses to choose → acknowledge the hopelessness, admit you cannot guarantee success, and ask to try a few minutes on one problem. Because honesty about uncertainty raises willingness to experiment [Beck-2021].
- If the client reacts against agenda setting itself (for example feels "hemmed in") → offer a few minutes of free talk first, then choose together what to work on. Because flexibility early keeps the client engaged, and you can move toward standard structure later [Beck-2021].
- If the client needs to vent about something important to them → allow it, within limits, and return to the agenda afterward. Because flexibility of this kind is part of collaboration [Young-Beck-1980].
- If risk, overwhelming distress or a threat to the relationship emerges mid-session → depart from the agenda and say so; for risk to life, assess now and follow `02-risk.crisis-response`. Because these override the agreed plan [Beck-2021].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's make a quick list first, then pick what matters most. What's one thing you want to make sure we get to?"
- "That sounds important. Can we call it 'the argument with Sam' and come back to the details in a minute?"
- "We have three things here and time for about two. Which would make the biggest difference this week?"
- Avoid: "So, what's been going on?" as the agenda question. It invites a narrative rather than a list.

## Common problems

- **No specific problems agreed.** The session wanders and ends without an intervention [Young-Beck-1980].
- **Peripheral rather than central problem chosen.** Progress stalls because the central concern keeps intruding [Young-Beck-1980].
- **Skipping from problem to problem across sessions.** Persist with one or two problems until a workable solution emerges [Young-Beck-1980].
- **Overloading.** Novices tend to overestimate what fits; keep a clock both of you can see so you track time together [Beck-2021].
- **Silent switches.** Changing topic without explanation can signal to the client that a problem is hopeless [Young-Beck-1980].
