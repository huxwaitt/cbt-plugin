**Bottom line:** Start each session by getting a number and a word for how the client has been, comparing it with last time, and discussing what the change means. Then bridge: a two or three sentence update on the week and on last session's work, which feeds the agenda [Beck-2021; Young-Beck-1980]. Keep it brief, talk about the scores rather than just collect them, and treat any risk signal as the first priority.

## When to use

- At the start of every treatment session, before agenda setting.
- When scores move sharply, or stop moving, and today's plan may no longer fit.

## Steps

1. **Before the session.** Have the client complete the agreed measures (for example the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) or Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7); see `08-measurement.depression-measures`) in the waiting room or online. Reread your notes from last session and the previous scores [Beck-2021].
2. **Ask for the client's own rating.** "Compared with last week, have you felt better, worse or about the same?" Then name the emotion: sadder, more anxious, more irritable [Beck-2021].
3. **Compare with the numbers.** State the direction of change in plain words and ask whether it fits the client's experience.
4. **Ask for the client's explanation.** When mood improves, ask what the client did or thought differently, not only what happened to them. When mood worsens, ask whether thinking or activity played a part [Beck-2021].
5. **Screen risk.** Look at any self-harm item first (for example PHQ-9 item 9). If it is positive or the client hints at risk, the agenda waits [Beck-2021]. See `02-risk.ongoing-risk-monitoring`.
6. **Bridge.** Ask for the week in two or three sentences, anything that went well, anything from last session that stuck, and whether the action plan got done. The Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) manual describes this brief resume of events, homework feedback and current emotional status as the opening of the agenda [Young-Beck-1980].
7. **Convert to agenda candidates.** Elevated scale items and hard moments from the week become possible agenda items [Beck-2021].

## What to record

- Each score, its change from the last session and any item-level flags.
- The client's verbal rating (for example 0 to 100) and named emotion.
- The client's explanation of change, risk screen result and action taken.
- Bridge items carried into the agenda.

## Guidance

- If the client says the forms are irrelevant or a waste of time → ask what the worst part is, explain that the forms save session time, and problem-solve when to do them. Because annoyance often rests on a missed rationale, a practical barrier or an overestimate of the time the forms take [Beck-2021].
- If the client is angry about forms and the alliance is fragile → drop the forms for now and ask for a single 0 to 100 mood rating. Because preserving the relationship matters more than a complete data set this week [Beck-2021].
- If the client describes the week at length → interrupt gently, ask "better, worse or the same?", then "more anxious, sad or angry?" Because details belong in agenda time [Beck-2021].
- If the client cannot label feelings → suggest "identifying feelings" as an agenda item. Because labeling emotions is a skill that can be taught [Beck-2021].
- If the client credits all improvement to luck or other people → ask "Did you also do or think anything differently?" Because it reinforces the cognitive model and implies the client has some control over mood [Beck-2021].
- If an item or remark suggests suicidal thinking → set the agenda aside, assess risk now and update the safety plan; if danger is imminent, do not let the client leave alone and contact emergency services (United States: 911, or the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline by call or text; United Kingdom: 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans 116 123). Because risk to life overrides the agreed agenda [Beck-2021]. See `02-risk.crisis-response`.
- If filling in measures is eating session time → move completion to before the session and use the time to discuss meaning. Because time on the mechanics of completing measures, rather than discussing results, predicted poorer outcome in one study of first sessions for severe depression (emerging evidence) [Malins-etal-2026].
- If scores are flat or worsening over several sessions → raise it openly and review the plan with `04-treatment-planning.managing-non-response`. Because progress feedback reduces poor outcomes most for clients who are not on track (supported by meta-analyses) [Lambert-etal-2018; DeJong-etal-2021].

Example phrasings:
- "Your score went from 17 to 12. Does that match how the week felt?"
- "I'm glad your manager was away. Did you also notice yourself doing anything differently?"
- Avoid: "Just fill these in and we'll get going." It signals the measures do not matter.

## Common problems

- **Collecting without using.** Measurement-based care is underused, with typically under 20% of behavioral health practitioners integrating it into practice [Lewis-etal-2019]. A score never discussed teaches the client that forms are paperwork.
- **Missing the bridge.** Without a link to last session, work restarts each week and action plans lose meaning.
- **Effect size context.** Across 58 studies, progress feedback had a small effect on symptoms overall (d = 0.15) and in not-on-track cases (d = 0.17) [DeJong-etal-2021]. In a meta-analysis of 24 studies of two feedback systems, feedback nearly doubled reliable or clinically significant change rates for clients predicted to do poorly [Lambert-etal-2018].
