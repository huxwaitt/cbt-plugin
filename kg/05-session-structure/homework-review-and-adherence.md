**Bottom line:** Review the action plan near the start of every session, focus on what the client learned rather than whether the form is filled in, and summarize the conclusion [Young-Beck-1980]. If the client did not do it, treat that as information: look for practical obstacles, task difficulty, unhelpful beliefs, distress or a strain in the relationship, and adjust the task rather than blame the client [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].

## When to use

- In the opening part of every session, as a standing agenda item [Beck-2021].
- When completion has dropped, or the client seems to go through the motions.

## Steps

1. **Prepare.** Reread last session's note so you know exactly what was agreed. You are more likely to remember the review if it is a standard agenda item and you reread your notes beforehand [Beck-2021].
2. **Ask openly.** "How did the plan go this week?" Then ask what the client did, how often and how it felt.
3. **Look at quality.** Read the worksheet together or ask the client to walk you through one example. Check whether the skill was used as intended. Quality of homework relates to outcome as strongly as quantity, though few studies have measured it [Kazantzis-etal-2016].
4. **Draw out the learning.** "What did that tell you?" Ask the client to put the conclusion in their own words and write it down.
5. **Summarize and reinforce.** State the conclusion or progress made, and credit the client's effort [Young-Beck-1980].
6. **Link forward.** Decide whether to continue, extend, or change the task, and connect it to today's agenda.
7. **Keep it proportionate.** Do not spend long on details of a task unrelated to the client's distress today [Beck-2021].

## What to record

- What was done (none, partial, full), with the client's own estimate of how much.
- What was learned, in the client's words.
- Barriers identified and the change made to the next task.
- Optionally a standard engagement rating such as the Homework Rating Scale-Revised (HRS-II), which is validated but has no cut-offs [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].

## Guidance

- If the task was done → ask what the client noticed and concluded before giving your view. Because the client's own conclusion is what transfers to the next situation.
- If the task was partly done → explore the part that happened first, then what stopped the rest. Because partial completion still holds data and deserves credit.
- If the task was not done → stay curious and check, in order, practical obstacles, whether the task was too hard or unclear, beliefs it activated ("It won't help", "I'll do it wrong") and emotional distress [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024]. Because each cause needs a different fix.
- If repeated non-completion follows tension in the room → consider an alliance rupture and ask about it directly. Because non-completion can signal opposition or rupture, and the therapist should examine their own part before attributing it to the client [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
- If the client forgot → do a short version of the task in session now and add a cue or reminder for this week. Because doing it in session still yields the learning and tests the task (expert consensus).
- If beliefs such as "If I can't do it perfectly there's no point" block the task → treat the belief as a session target, for example with a behavioral experiment. Because the same belief usually drives the presenting problem. See `15-transdiagnostic-processes.perfectionism`.
- If the client seems to comply without engagement → ask what they made of the task and whether it fits. Because going through the motions limits learning [Young-Beck-1980].

Example phrasings:
- "Let's look at what you tried this week. What stood out?"
- "It didn't happen this week. That's useful to know. What got in the way?"
- "When you thought about doing it, what went through your mind?"
- Avoid: "You didn't do your homework again." It invites shame and defensiveness.

## Common problems

- **Skipping the review.** If therapists do not routinely review tasks, clients may conclude the tasks do not matter [Young-Beck-1980].
- **Counting instead of learning.** Recording "done" without the conclusion loses most of the value.
- **Assuming laziness.** Non-completion has many causes; blame closes the conversation [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
- **Evidence context.** Completion correlates with outcome, r = 0.26 across 23 studies [Mausbach-etal-2010]. In two depression samples, higher adherence predicted better response and remission only after adjusting for session attendance, a reminder that attendance and adherence are entangled [Callan-etal-2019].
