**Bottom line:** A common structural failure in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is drift: the update expands to fill the hour and no problem is worked on to the point of change. The fix is gentle interruption, a short agenda of one or two items, frequent summaries, and a check before the end that the session produced a response, a partial solution or an action plan [Beck-2021; Young-Beck-1980].

## What it looks like

- The client narrates the week in detail; the therapist listens, reflects and asks follow-up questions for most of the session.
- Topics change every few minutes, often following whatever the client mentions last.
- No agenda is set, or it is set and then forgotten.
- The session ends at time with "Let's pick this up next week", no summary and no action plan.
- Across sessions, different problems are touched each week but none is followed through [Young-Beck-1980].
- Notes say "discussed stressors" week after week, and scores have stopped moving.

## Why it happens

- **Therapist cognitions.** Beliefs such as "I shouldn't interrupt her", "He'll get mad if I'm too directive" or "I'll miss something important" stop the therapist from steering [Beck-2021].
- **Socialization gaps.** The client was never told how sessions work, so they do what other therapy or friends taught them: talk through the week [Beck-2021].
- **Engagement issues.** The client may lack goals they want, feel hopeless, or fear what change would bring [Beck-2021].
- **Rewarding feel.** Recaps feel warm and easy for both people, so the drift is reinforced (expert consensus).

## The fix

- If the client launches into detail during the update → interrupt kindly and ask for two or three sentences. "Can I pause you? Give me the headline of the week, then we'll choose what to dig into." Because interruption plus summary is the main tool against unfocused discussion [Beck-2021].
- If you notice you have discussed a problem without intervening → ask yourself "How can I help the client feel better by the end of the session?" and move to a specific thought, behavior or plan. Because describing a problem rarely helps on its own [Beck-2021].
- If the client was never socialized to structure → explain it now with a rationale, and invite feedback on trying it. Because most clients accept structure once they understand why [Beck-2021].
- If you are unsure whether drift is the problem → review a session recording, ideally with a supervisor, and rate the agenda and pacing items. Because these are standard competence items and structuring skill was the competence component most related to outcome in a large depression trial [Young-Beck-1980; Shaw-etal-1999].
- If the client needs some free talk to feel heard → schedule it: a few minutes of open update, then agenda work. Because a planned space prevents the update swallowing the session [Beck-2021].

Example phrasings:
- "I notice we've covered a lot of ground today without landing on one thing. Which of these matters most to work on right now?"
- "Before we move on, what could you try this week about this?"
- Avoid: "And then what happened?" repeated through the session. It keeps the narrative going without a target.

## Sources note

The description of drift and its causes comes from Beck's chapter on problems in structuring sessions [Beck-2021] and the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) manual's list of common novice mistakes [Young-Beck-1980]. The link between structuring skill and outcome rests mainly on one secondary analysis of a large randomized trial [Shaw-etal-1999]; overall competence and outcome links are modest, so treat structure as necessary but not sufficient. The CTRS is described in `05-session-structure.therapist-competence`.
