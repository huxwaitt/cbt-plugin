**Bottom line:** Summarize often and briefly during the session so both of you hold the same picture, and ask the client to say the main points in their own words, ideally writing them down to review during the week [Young-Beck-1980]. End by asking the client to summarize, confirming the action plan and asking for feedback. Thank the client for any criticism, apologize where it fits, and act on it [Beck-2021].

## When to use

- **Capsule summaries:** after the client tells a story, before shifting from understanding a problem to intervening, and when the discussion wanders.
- **Client summaries:** at the end of each agenda item and at the end of the session.
- **Feedback:** at the end of every session, and mid-session when you notice a shift in the client's affect or a nonverbal sign of disagreement [Young-Beck-1980].

## Steps

1. **Capsule summary.** In two or three sentences, restate the key situation, thought, emotion and behavior, then check: "Did I get that right?" Ask the client to correct or fine-tune it [Young-Beck-1980].
2. **Redirect.** Use the summary as a bridge back to the key cognition: "So when she said that, what went through your mind?" [Beck-2021].
3. **Client summary of each item.** "What's the main thing you want to remember from this?" Help the client write it in their own words.
4. **End-of-session summary.** The client names the takeaways; you add anything important that was missed.
5. **Confirm the action plan.** Check the client can say what, when and why. See `05-session-structure.homework-design`.
6. **Ask for feedback.** Ask what was helpful, what was not, and whether you got anything wrong. Invite negative reactions explicitly [Young-Beck-1980].
7. **Respond to feedback.** Thank the client, apologize if you misunderstood or pushed too hard, clarify, and agree what to change next time [Beck-2021].

## What to record

- The client's own summary or key takeaways, in their words.
- The client's feedback, positive and negative, and any change you agreed to make.
- Process scale scores if a formal measure is used.

## Guidance

- If the client nods along to everything → ask them to put the main point in their own words. Because clients, especially when depressed, may signal understanding out of compliance [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the discussion is going in circles → summarize in a few sentences and ask a focusing question. Because frequent summaries are a main tool against unfocused discussion [Beck-2021].
- If the client offers criticism ("You don't get how hard this is") → thank them, apologize for the impression given, ask what you said or did that led to it, and carry the issue to the next agenda. Because positively reinforcing negative feedback keeps it coming and protects the alliance [Beck-2021].
- If the client's face or tone changes during the session → name it and ask what went through their mind. Because negative reactions are often shown only nonverbally and may surface only when invited [Young-Beck-1980].
- If the client is still upset as time runs out → acknowledge it, schedule the issue first next time, and move to something lighter before they leave. Because clients should not leave in peak distress over an unfinished topic [Beck-2021].
- If feedback is always "fine" → try a written rating or ask a specific question ("Was there a moment today that felt unhelpful?"). Because general questions invite polite answers; brief formal scales are an alternative, see `08-measurement.process-measures`.
- If a formal outcome or alliance measure shows the client is off track → discuss it openly the same session. Because feedback systems reduce poor outcomes, most for not-on-track clients (supported by meta-analyses) [Lambert-etal-2018; DeJong-etal-2021].

Example phrasings:
- "Let me check I've got this. Your friend canceled, you thought 'nobody wants me around', felt low and stayed in. Is that right?"
- "What do you want to take away from today? Let's write it down."
- "What did you think of today's session? Was there anything I got wrong, or anything that bothered you?"
- "Thank you for telling me that. I'm sorry it came across that way. What would have helped more?"
- Avoid: "Any questions? No? Great, see you next week." It closes the door on feedback.

## Common problems

- **Therapist-only summaries.** The therapist summarizes everything and the client never practices pulling out the main point.
- **Feedback as ritual.** Asking without changing anything teaches the client that feedback is pointless.
- **Defensiveness.** Explaining or justifying in response to criticism shuts it down; reinforce, then conceptualize [Beck-2021].
- **Evidence context.** Formal progress feedback has a small overall effect on symptoms (d = 0.15 across 58 studies) and a small favorable effect on dropout [DeJong-etal-2021]. Informal end-of-session feedback is a core cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) skill scored on competence scales [Young-Beck-1980], but its separate effect on outcome has not been established (expert consensus).
