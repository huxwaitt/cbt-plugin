**Bottom line:** Competence is how skillfully a therapist delivers cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), as distinct from adherence (whether the prescribed techniques were used at all). It is usually rated from session recordings on the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS, usual pass mark 40 of 66) or the Revised Cognitive Therapy Scale (CTS-R, usual pass mark 36 of 72) [Creed-etal-2021; James-etal-2001]. Competence can be trained, but its link to client outcome is weaker and less consistent than many expect, so rate it alongside client outcome data, not instead of it [Webb-etal-2010].

## What it is

- **Adherence** asks "Did the therapist do CBT?"; **competence** asks "How well, for this client, at this moment?"
- **CTRS.** Developed by Young and Beck in 1980. Eleven items in two parts: general therapeutic skills (agenda, feedback, understanding, interpersonal effectiveness, collaboration, pacing and efficient use of time) and conceptualization, strategy and technique (guided discovery, focus on key cognitions or behaviors, strategy for change, application of techniques, homework). Each item is rated 0 to 6, adjusted for how difficult the client seems [Young-Beck-1980]. Totals run 0 to 66, and 40 or more is the usual benchmark for competence [Creed-etal-2021]. Version dates vary: the Beck Institute hosts this 11-item manual as a 1980 revised draft, while the CTS-R developers date a 13-item version to 1980 and the 11-item version to 1988 [James-etal-2001]. The Beck Institute's 2022 revision (CTRS-R) keeps the same 11 items and expands the criteria for each [BeckInstitute-CTRSR-2022].
- **CTS-R.** A United Kingdom (UK) revision with 12 items: agenda setting and adherence, feedback, collaboration, pacing and efficient use of time, interpersonal effectiveness, eliciting appropriate emotional expression, eliciting key cognitions, eliciting behaviors, guided discovery, conceptual integration, application of change methods and homework setting [James-etal-2001]. Each is rated 0 to 6, total 0 to 72; the Newcastle developers set 36 (an average of 3 per item) as the minimum competence standard [James-etal-2001], and later studies use the same threshold [Bergvall-etal-2024]. The original psychometric paper tested a 14-item draft (including an optional nonverbal item), finding high internal consistency but variable item-level agreement between raters; the 12-item version is the one in current use [Blackburn-etal-2001; Alfonsson-etal-2022].

## Why it matters in CBT

- Structuring skill mattered most in one landmark depression trial: the competence factor most related to outcome reflected the ability to structure treatment, though the overall effect was "not as strong or consistent as expected" [Shaw-etal-1999].
- Across psychotherapies, mean correlations with outcome were r = 0.07 for competence and r = 0.02 for adherence, both not significant and highly heterogeneous; competence effects were larger in depression studies [Webb-etal-2010].
- In routine Swedish psychiatric care, 76% of therapists passed the CTS-R threshold in role plays with a standardized patient, clients improved substantially, and neither competence nor adherence predicted outcome [Bergvall-etal-2024].
- Disorder-specific competence may matter more: a panic-specific scale correlated with outcome (r = 0.29 to 0.35) where the CTS-R did not [Liness-etal-2021].

## Key details

- **Ways to assess competence.** A systematic review found ten distinct methods, from knowledge tests and role plays to rated real sessions and client outcomes; each trades reliability and validity against feasibility [Muse-McManus-2013].
- **Competence frameworks.** Roth and Pilling described an evidence-based method for deriving the competences needed to deliver CBT for depression and anxiety, prompted by England's Improving Access to Psychological Therapies program, now NHS Talking Therapies [Roth-Pilling-2008].
- **Deliberate practice.** Repeated, feedback-driven rehearsal of specific skills just beyond current ability. Time spent deliberately working on skills predicted client outcomes in one practice-based study of 17 therapists [Chow-etal-2015]. A 2025 systematic review judged the evidence too preliminary to confirm or refute benefits for outcomes (emerging evidence) [Diamond-etal-2025].
- **Self-practice and self-reflection (SP/SR).** Therapists apply CBT techniques to themselves and reflect on the experience. Small cohort-controlled pilots found gains in therapist confidence and self-awareness (preliminary) [Scott-etal-2021].

## In the session

- If you avoid structuring because of thoughts like "I'll seem controlling" → write the thought down, test it with one fully structured session, and review the recording. Because therapist cognitions are a common block to structure [Beck-2021].
- If preparing for a rated assessment → record sessions with consent, self-rate on the CTRS or CTS-R, then compare with a supervisor's rating. Because calibrated self-rating shows exactly which items to target.
- If one item is consistently low (for example pacing or homework) → pick that single skill for deliberate practice with role plays and feedback. Because narrow, repeated practice is the core of the method [Chow-etal-2015].
- If competence ratings are high but the client is not improving → review outcome data, the formulation and the alliance. Because rated competence and outcome often diverge [Webb-etal-2010].
- If you supervise → listen to recordings rather than relying on report. Because review of session recordings is "invaluable" for spotting structuring problems [Beck-2021].
