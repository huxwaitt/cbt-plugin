**Bottom line:** Clients learn quickly whether homework matters by whether the therapist asks about it. If tasks are vague and never reviewed, clients may conclude there is no need to do them, and one of cognitive behavioral therapy's (CBT) main routes to change is lost [Young-Beck-1980]. Make action plan review a standing agenda item, reread your notes before each session, and design tasks during the session rather than in the last minute [Beck-2021; Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].

## What it looks like

- "Keep doing the thought records" said as the client stands up.
- The next session opens with a new crisis or topic and the task is never mentioned.
- The therapist cannot remember exactly what was agreed and the note does not say.
- The client brings a completed worksheet and it stays in their bag.
- Over weeks, the client stops attempting tasks and starts apologizing for "not being good at this."

## Why it happens

- **Haste.** In a rush to reach the client's agenda items, therapists skip the review [Beck-2021].
- **No written record.** The task was not written down, so neither person knows what to check.
- **Discomfort.** Therapists may avoid asking for fear of shaming the client or seeming like a teacher.
- **Last-minute assignment.** Tasks tacked on at the end are vague, have no rationale and no plan for obstacles [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].

## The fix

- If you keep forgetting the review → put "action plan review" on every agenda by default and reread last session's note before the client arrives. Because both make it much more likely the review happens [Beck-2021].
- If the task was set in the last minute → move task setting into each agenda item, with rationale, specifics and an obstacle plan. Because collaborative, specific, well-reasoned tasks are more likely to be done [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
- If the client completed a task and you did not look at it → apologize, look at it now, and draw out what they learned. Because reviewing and summarizing the conclusion is what makes the task count [Young-Beck-1980].
- If you hesitate to ask because the client may feel ashamed → ask with curiosity and treat non-completion as useful information. Because blame closes the conversation, while curiosity reveals the barrier [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
- If completion has already collapsed → restart with a small, highly specific task the client designs, and review it first thing next session. Because rebuilding a record of success comes before larger tasks.

Example phrasings:
- "First on our list, as always: how did this week's plan go?"
- "I realize I didn't ask about your worksheet last time. I'm sorry. Can we look at it now?"
- Avoid: "Did you do your homework?" as a yes or no question. It invites a guilty "no" and ends the discussion.

## Sources note

That unreviewed homework teaches clients it does not matter is stated in the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) manual [Young-Beck-1980], which scores reviewing previous homework as part of competent practice. Beck describes therapists skipping review in haste and recommends the standing agenda item [Beck-2021]. The link between completion and outcome is correlational, r = 0.26 across 23 studies [Mausbach-etal-2010]; the design recommendations are summarized by Ryum and Kazantzis [Ryum-Kazantzis-2024].
