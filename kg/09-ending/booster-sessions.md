**Bottom line:** Boosters are a few sessions scheduled after the end of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) to review the relapse prevention plan and practice skills; a continuation phase is a longer series of spaced sessions for people at high relapse risk. Continuation CBT has the stronger evidence: it cut relapse in recurrent depression and panic disorder in randomized trials [Jarrett-etal-2001; White-etal-2013]. Offer a continuation phase to people at higher risk; pre-scheduled boosters for most others are expert consensus.

## When to use

- **Continuation phase:** recurrent depression at higher relapse risk (early onset, unstable remission late in treatment, partial remission) [Jarrett-etal-2001; NICE-NG222], or panic disorder after a good response to CBT, especially with leftover agoraphobic avoidance, which predicted relapse [White-etal-2013].
- **Boosters:** most planned endings, especially when residual symptoms remain or a high-risk event is coming.
- **Children and adolescents:** consider boosters routinely; studies that included them reported larger and better-maintained gains in youth CBT [Gearing-etal-2013].
- **Not a substitute** for a new course when the full problem has returned.

## How it works

Spaced contact keeps skills in use, catches early warning signs, and lets the client solve new problems with support. Pre-scheduling boosters may motivate practice between sessions and ease anxiety about coping alone [Beck-2021].

## Steps

1. **Decide the format with the client** at the end of treatment: none, open-door return, scheduled boosters, or a continuation phase.
2. **Schedule.** Spacing is not standardized. Trials have used 10 sessions over 8 months [Jarrett-etal-2001] and 9 monthly sessions [White-etal-2013]. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) describes group relapse prevention courses with the option of extra sessions over the following 12 months [NICE-NG222]. A common pattern is boosters at growing intervals over the first year, and before known high-risk dates (expert consensus).
3. **Set preparation tasks.** Ask the client to bring notes on what they practiced and how they handled problems since the last session [Beck-2021].
4. **Run the booster session:**
   - Repeat the main outcome measure and compare it with the end-of-treatment score.
   - Review the plan: which tools were used, which warning signs appeared, what was done.
   - Work on one current problem with the usual CBT methods.
   - Look ahead: high-risk situations before the next contact [NICE-NG222].
   - Update the written plan and confirm the next contact or the route back.
5. **At the final booster,** reassess relapse risk and decide whether any further follow-up is needed [NICE-NG222].

## Guidance

- If scores at a booster are close to end-of-treatment levels → keep the session short and practical, and space the next one further out. Because the goal is independent coping, not more therapy [Beck-2021].
- If warning signs have appeared but scores remain below the clinical range → bring the next booster forward and restart daily practice. Because residual symptoms are linked to high relapse rates [Paykel-etal-1995].
- If symptoms have returned to the original level → stop treating it as a booster and agree a new course of treatment or a referral. Because the evidence for continuation sessions comes from people who had already responded, not people in a new episode [Jarrett-etal-2001].
- If the client wants to cancel boosters because they feel well → agree to at least one check-in by phone with a questionnaire. "Let's keep one short check in three months; if all is well, that's the last one." Because NICE asks for relapse risk to be reassessed at the end of relapse prevention work [NICE-NG222].
- If a client with recurrent depression responded to acute CBT but remission was unstable → offer a continuation phase rather than one-off boosters. Because continuation CBT cut 24-month relapse and recurrence from 62% to 37% in this group [Jarrett-etal-2001].
- If the client prefers to stay on antidepressants instead → support that choice. Because continuation CBT and fluoxetine gave similar protection in higher-risk responders [Jarrett-etal-2013].

## Common problems

- **Boosters become open-ended therapy.** Fix: agree the number and purpose in advance.
- **No-shows.** Send a reminder, and if missed, a check-in message with the route back.
- **Boosters without measures.** Without a score the session cannot detect early relapse (see `08-measurement.measurement-based-care`).

## Evidence

- **Depression (adults):** after 20 sessions of acute CBT, 10 continuation sessions over 8 months reduced relapse at 8 months from 31% to 10%; in early-onset depression, 24-month relapse and recurrence fell from 67% to 16% [Jarrett-etal-2001]. In a later trial of higher-risk responders, continuation CBT and fluoxetine were both better than pill placebo over 8 months and nearly identical to each other; after they stopped, neither protected longer than the other [Jarrett-etal-2013]. Supported by randomized trials.
- **Panic disorder:** 9 monthly maintenance sessions after successful CBT reduced relapse from 18.4% to 5.2% and reduced work and social impairment over 21 months [White-etal-2013]. Supported by a randomized trial.
- **Youth anxiety and mood:** a meta-analysis of 53 studies (1,937 youth) found larger effects in studies with boosters, at post-treatment (r = .58 versus .45, a marginal difference) and at 6-month follow-up (r = .64 versus .48) [Gearing-etal-2013]. Evidence is comparative across studies, not from randomized booster trials.
- **Brief adult boosters (a handful of sessions):** direct trial evidence is thin; their use rests mainly on expert consensus.
