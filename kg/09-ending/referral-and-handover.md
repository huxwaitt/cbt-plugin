**Bottom line:** Refer on when the client needs something outside your competence, is not benefiting, needs a higher or specialist level of care, or when you or the client cannot continue [APA-Ethics-2017]. Prepare the client, get consent, send a focused referral, and keep clinical responsibility until the new provider has actually seen the client. For anyone with suicide risk, the handover must be fast and actively followed up [ActionAlliance-2019].

## When to use

- **Competence:** the problem needs skills you lack, such as an eating disorder with medical risk or psychosis [APA-Ethics-2017].
- **No benefit or harm:** after an adequate trial and review [APA-Ethics-2017].
- **Step up or specialist care:** see `04-treatment-planning.stepped-care`.
- **Medication or medical review:** see `12-medical-pharmacology.working-with-prescribers`.
- **Interruption:** you are leaving, retiring, unwell, or the client moves or loses funding. Plan for continuity in advance [APA-Ethics-2017].

## Steps

1. **Discuss it with the client first.** Explain the reason in terms of their goals, not your limits alone. Ask about their preferences for type, format and clinician, since accommodating preferences is linked to less dropout [Swift-etal-2018].
2. **Choose the receiving provider** and check the referral criteria, waiting time and cost before promising anything.
3. **Get consent** for what will be shared and with whom (see Guidance on the legal basis).
4. **Write the referral** (table below). Attach the discharge summary or a current summary.
5. **Make it warm.** Where possible, introduce the client by phone or a joint session, book the first appointment before the client leaves, and give the new provider's details in writing.
6. **Bridge the gap.** Agree what support continues until the first appointment, and give crisis contacts.
7. **Confirm attendance.** Ask the client or provider to confirm the first appointment happened. Follow up if it was missed [ActionAlliance-2019].
8. **Close your episode** once care has transferred, and write the closing summary.

| Referral letter section | Content |
|---|---|
| Reason and question | What you are asking the receiver to do, and urgency |
| Background | Presenting problems, diagnosis if made, brief formulation |
| Treatment so far | Sessions, interventions, response with scores |
| Risk | Current risk, safety plan, protective factors |
| Other care | Medication, prescriber, primary care contact |
| Client view | Their goals and preferences for the next step |
| Consent | What the client agreed to share |

## What to record

- The reason for referral and the client's view.
- Consent given, recipients and documents sent.
- The date of the first appointment and confirmation that it took place.
- What interim support was agreed.

## Guidance

- If the client feels "passed on" → frame the referral as matching care to need. "This isn't about you being too much. Your eating has medical risks that need a team I can't provide." Because referral when a need is outside your competence is an ethical duty [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If the client is leaving inpatient care with suicide risk → aim for an appointment within 24 to 72 hours and no later than 7 days, and keep in contact until they attend. Because suicide rates are highest in the first week after psychiatric discharge and stay raised for up to 3 months [ActionAlliance-2019].
- If the first appointment is missed → contact the client the same day and tell the receiving provider. Because no-show follow-up is a core transition practice [ActionAlliance-2019].
- If you are a covered entity under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and are sharing with another treating provider → share what helps their care, tell the client, and check state law, which can be stricter. Because HIPAA permits disclosure for another provider's treatment without authorization, though psychotherapy notes need written authorization [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-506; HIPAA-45-CFR-164-508].
- If the records come from a federally assisted substance use disorder program → obtain written consent that meets 42 Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) Part 2. Because those records need specific consent [CFR-42-2-33].
- If you are leaving your post → tell clients early, offer handover sessions, and arrange record custody. Because records must support continuity of care when you can no longer provide it [APA-RecordKeeping-2007].
- If the client refuses referral but needs it → document the discussion and keep offering it, and give crisis contacts: US 988 (call or text) or 911 [Lifeline-988]; UK 999, NHS 111 (mental health option) or Samaritans 116 123 [NHS-Urgent-Help; Samaritans-Site]. Because ending still requires suggesting alternative providers [APA-Ethics-2017].

## Common problems

- **A letter is not a handover.** The client sits on a waiting list with no support. Fix: bridge the gap and confirm attendance.
- **Over-long referrals.** Send the summary that answers the question; attach detail only if asked.
- **Ownership gaps.** Nobody follows up a no-show. Fix: agree in writing who does.
- **UK practice:** referrals often route through the general practitioner or a single point of access; check local pathways.
