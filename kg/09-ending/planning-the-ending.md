**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is time-limited and aims to teach the client to be their own therapist, so say this in the first session and plan the ending from there [Beck-2021]. In the last phase, space sessions further apart (expert consensus), discuss how to keep the gains and write them into a relapse prevention plan [NICE-NG222], review progress, and say goodbye deliberately [Norcross-etal-2017]. End on the client's progress and the agreed criteria, not on the calendar alone.

## When to use

- **Session 1:** when you explain how CBT works and agree a provisional number of sessions.
- **Every review point:** when you compare progress with goals and measures.
- **The ending phase:** usually the last few sessions of a course, or as soon as ending criteria are close (see `09-ending.ending-criteria`).
- **Any time ending is forced early** (insurer limit, therapist leaving, client moving). The same steps apply in compressed form.

## Steps

1. **Name the ending at the start.** Explain that CBT is time-limited and that the aim is for the client to learn skills they can use alone [Beck-2021]. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks clinicians to let the treatment manual guide the form, duration and ending of therapy [NICE-NG222].
2. **Build self-reliance throughout.** Help the client credit their own work for each change, not the therapist or luck [Beck-2021]. Keep written summaries of what each session taught; these become the relapse prevention plan.
3. **Open the ending phase explicitly.** Several sessions before the end, review progress against goals and scores and agree the end date [Norcross-etal-2017].
4. **Discuss tapering.** Ask the client to list the advantages and disadvantages of spacing sessions out, then examine the feared disadvantages together [Beck-2021]. Spacing sessions (for example every other week, then a longer gap) is expert consensus rather than trial-tested.
5. **Practice self-therapy between spaced sessions.** The client runs their own short session: check mood, review practice, pick a problem, plan the next step [Beck-2021].
6. **Write the relapse prevention plan** (see `09-ending.relapse-prevention-blueprint`). NICE asks therapists nearing the end of psychological treatment to discuss how to maintain the benefits [NICE-NG222].
7. **Decide on extra protection.** For depression at higher relapse risk, discuss continuing the same therapy for relapse prevention, with at least 4 more sessions [NICE-NG222]. Agree boosters or a route back (see `09-ending.booster-sessions`).
8. **Run the final session.** Review gains, process the client's feelings about ending (positive and negative), frame further growth as expected, credit the client, and say goodbye [Norcross-etal-2017].
9. **Close the episode.** Write the discharge summary and send agreed letters (see `09-ending.discharge-summary`).

## What to record

- The provisional session number and review dates agreed at the start.
- The date the ending phase was opened and the agreed final date.
- Start and end scores, goal status and any residual symptoms.
- The client's reactions to ending and how they were addressed.
- That a written relapse prevention plan was given, plus booster or return arrangements.
- Risk status at the final session and crisis contacts given.

## Guidance

- If a client says "I don't think I can manage without these sessions" → test the prediction with a spaced session. "Let's try a three-week gap and see what you actually do with a setback." Because examining the feared disadvantages of tapering is part of preparing for the end [Beck-2021].
- If a client wants to stop earlier than planned because they feel well → review scores and agree a short ending phase rather than arguing for more sessions. Because routine-care data suggest many clients end once they reach a good-enough level of improvement [Barkham-etal-2006].
- If a client has improved well before the planned number → discuss ending early. Because in NHS Talking Therapies data, 95% of clients who reached reliable and clinically significant improvement did so within 14 high-intensity CBT sessions [Robinson-etal-2020b].
- If CBT is clearly not helping or is causing harm → end and refer rather than continue. Because ethics codes require ending when the client is not likely to benefit or is being harmed [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If the ending is forced by a payer or by your own departure → still offer a pre-ending discussion and suggest other providers. Because this is required unless the client's or payer's actions prevent it [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If you feel reluctant to end with a client you like → name the reluctance in supervision. Avoid: "We'll just keep going and see how things are." Because the ethical test is whether the client still needs or benefits from the service, not the relationship [APA-Ethics-2017].

## Common problems

- **Ending as a surprise.** The client learns the end date in the last session. Fix: name it at the start and at every review.
- **No spacing.** Weekly sessions stop dead, so the client never tests their skills alone. Fix: taper and use self-therapy sessions.
- **Credit goes to the therapist.** "You fixed me" signals low self-efficacy. Fix: ask "What did you do that made the difference?"
- **Residual symptoms ignored.** See `09-ending.pitfall-ending-with-residual-symptoms`.
- **Feelings skipped.** Grief, relief or anger about ending go unspoken. Fix: ask directly in the final sessions [Norcross-etal-2017].
