**Bottom line:** A lapse or setback is a brief return of old symptoms or behaviors; a relapse is the return of the full problem before recovery; a recurrence is a new episode after recovery [Rush-etal-2006; Larimer-etal-1999]. Teaching clients that setbacks are expected and recoverable is a core part of ending, because how a person interprets a lapse shapes whether it becomes a relapse [Larimer-etal-1999].

## What it is

Research terms (from depression research) [Rush-etal-2006]:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Response | Meaningful symptom reduction, but symptoms may remain |
| Remission | Minimal symptoms for at least 3 consecutive weeks |
| Recovery | Remission sustained for at least 4 months |
| Relapse | Return of the episode during remission, before recovery |
| Recurrence | A new episode after recovery |

Clinical terms used with clients:
- **Lapse or slip:** a single return to an old behavior, for example one drink after a period of abstinence [Larimer-etal-1999].
- **Setback:** a period of worse mood or anxiety that the client can manage with their skills. Beck's teaching describes recovery as an uneven line with expected dips [Beck-2021].
- **Relapse (clinical use):** the problem has returned to the level that brought the client to therapy, or the person has given up their goal [Larimer-etal-1999].

## Why it matters in CBT

- **It changes the client's reaction.** In Marlatt's model, a person who blames a lapse on stable, global, internal causes ("I have no willpower") is more likely to give up the goal (the abstinence violation effect); a person who sees it as a failure to cope with one situation is more likely to learn from it [Larimer-etal-1999]. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for many problems uses the same idea.
- **It sets ending decisions.** Ending at response rather than remission leaves residual symptoms. In one cohort, 76% of people with residual depressive symptoms relapsed, against 25% without [Paykel-etal-1995].
- **It guides what to watch for.** NICE (the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) lists factors that raise relapse risk in depression, and names identifying triggers and warning signs as a component of relapse prevention work [NICE-NG222].

## Key details

- The 3-week and 4-month durations come from a task force consensus based largely on logic and clinical experience, not data [Rush-etal-2006]. Treat them as a working standard.
- Lapse and relapse language comes from addiction research [Larimer-etal-1999]. For depression and anxiety, "setback" is usually the more acceptable word.
- A lapse is a moment; relapse is a process. The plan in `09-ending.relapse-prevention-blueprint` aims to catch the process early.
- Risk factors for depressive relapse include recurrent episodes (especially frequent ones or any in the last 2 years), residual symptoms, avoidance and rumination, past severe depression, chronic health problems and ongoing stressors [NICE-NG222].

## In the session

- If the client says "If I feel low again, it means therapy failed" → separate a setback from a relapse. "A bad week is a setback. It's information, not a verdict. The plan is what you do with it." Because in Marlatt's model, reading a lapse as proof of personal failure makes abandoning the goal more likely [Larimer-etal-1999].
- If a client reports a slip after ending → ask what happened in the situation, not what it says about them. "What was going on just before? What would you do differently next time?" Because relapse prevention treats lapses as learning opportunities so that one slip does not become a relapse; this step has not been tested on its own (model-based) [Larimer-etal-1999].
- If you are describing progress → draw an uneven line that trends upward and mark expected dips. Because rehearsing expected setbacks prepares clients to cope with them [Beck-2021].
- If scores have just fallen below the clinical range → explain that this is response or early remission, not recovery yet. Because recovery requires sustained remission [Rush-etal-2006].
- Avoid: "You're cured now." It sets up a single setback to feel like total failure.
