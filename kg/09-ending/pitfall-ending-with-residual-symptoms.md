**Bottom line:** Response is not remission. Residual symptoms strongly predict relapse: in one depression cohort, 76% of people with residual symptoms relapsed, against 25% without [Paykel-etal-1995]. Before ending, list what is left, target it, and offer relapse prevention sessions to people at higher risk [NICE-NG222].

## What it looks like

- The total score fell from severe to just under the clinical cutoff, and the plan is to end next week.
- The client says "I'm much better," but still sleeps badly, avoids some situations or ruminates in the evenings.
- Only one or two good weeks have passed since the drop.
- The discharge summary says "recovered" with no list of remaining symptoms.
- Relapse risk factors (recurrent episodes, past severe depression, ongoing stress) were never discussed.

## Why it happens

- Session limits push ending as soon as scores cross a threshold.
- Both client and therapist feel relieved and want to stop on a high.
- Total scores hide individual items that remain high.
- Residual symptoms feel minor next to the original problem.

## The fix

- If the score has only just dropped below the cutoff → wait until improvement is stable before opening the ending phase. Because remission is defined as minimal symptoms for 3 consecutive weeks, and recovery needs at least 4 months of remission [Rush-etal-2006].
- If items such as sleep, energy, worry or avoidance remain high → add them to the problem list and treat them. "Your mood has lifted a lot. Sleep and the evening brooding are still there, and they're the things most likely to pull you back. Shall we spend the next sessions on them?" Because residual symptoms strongly predicted relapse [Paykel-etal-1995].
- If the client has relapse risk factors such as recurrent episodes (especially frequent ones or any in the last 2 years), past severe depression, avoidance or rumination, or ongoing stressors → discuss continuing therapy for relapse prevention, with at least 4 more sessions. Because NICE (the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) recommends this for people at higher risk [NICE-NG222].
- If remission was unstable late in acute treatment → consider a continuation phase. Because continuation cognitive therapy cut 24-month relapse and recurrence from 62% to 37% in this group [Jarrett-etal-2001].
- If the client chooses to stop anyway → respect it, write the residual symptoms and warning signs into the relapse plan, and schedule a booster. Because acute cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) still carries some enduring protection after it ends [Cuijpers-etal-2013b].

## Sources note

The 76% versus 25% figures come from one small cohort (60 partially remitted patients; 13 of 17 versus 10 of 40 relapsed) using a 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale score of 8 or more to define residual symptoms [Paykel-etal-1995]. The remission and recovery durations rest mainly on expert consensus [Rush-etal-2006]. Evidence is strongest for depression; for anxiety disorders apply the principle and check residual avoidance and safety behaviors.
