**Bottom line:** The blueprint is a short written plan the client takes away: what kept the problem going, what helped, how to keep practicing, which situations and early warning signs to watch, and exactly what to do at each level of setback. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) lists these same components for relapse prevention work [NICE-NG222]. Write it in the client's words, rehearse it, and test it during spaced sessions before therapy ends.

## When to use

- The last phase of any course of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT); start drafting it several sessions before the end so it can be tested.
- Relapse prevention sessions for depression at higher relapse risk (see `09-ending.ending-criteria`).
- Any booster session: review and update the existing plan.

## How it works

It turns what the client learned into a usable script for the moment they feel worse, when recall and problem solving are poorest. It moves the client from relying on the therapist to relying on their own plan [Beck-2021]. Catching early warning signs gives a window to act before a full episode: in bipolar disorder, training people to spot early signs and seek help reduced manic relapses by 30% over 18 months, though not depressive relapses [Perry-etal-1999].

## Steps

Build each section with the client, in their words, from session notes and the formulation.

1. **What brought me to therapy and what kept it going.** One or two lines from the shared formulation (see `03-formulation.sharing-the-formulation`).
2. **What I learned and what helped.** The specific tools, with an example of each working. NICE lists reviewing the lessons learned and what helped [NICE-NG222].
3. **How I will keep it going.** A weekly routine: which practice, when, and a monthly self-check of mood or a repeat questionnaire. NICE asks for concrete plans to maintain progress [NICE-NG222].
4. **High-risk situations ahead.** Known events in the next 12 months, such as life changes and anniversaries of difficult events [NICE-NG222], work peaks or a planned medication change.
5. **My early warning signs.** A few personal, observable signs drawn from past episodes, such as anxiety, poorer sleep, avoidance or rumination [NICE-NG222], or seeing friends less. Ask a trusted person what they notice first.
6. **My setback plan.** A graded plan. A traffic-light format is common practice (expert consensus):
   - Green (well): keep the routine.
   - Amber (warning signs for a week or more): restart the core tools daily, run a self-therapy session, tell a support person [Beck-2021].
   - Red (symptoms back at the old level, or not coping): contact the service, primary care doctor or therapist on a named route back.
   - Crisis (thoughts of suicide or not able to stay safe): follow the safety plan. US: call or text 988, or call 911 [Lifeline-988]. UK: call 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 (mental health option), or Samaritans on 116 123 [NHS-Urgent-Help; Samaritans-Site].
7. **Rehearse.** Imagine a setback, name the likely thoughts ("This proves nothing worked"), and write answers on a coping card [Beck-2021].
8. **Test it.** Use the plan during spaced sessions and revise what did not work.

## Guidance

- If the client can only name vague signs ("feeling bad") → ask about the first week of the last episode. "What changed first: sleep, plans, how often you saw friends?" Because specific, observable signs are the ones that can trigger action [NICE-NG222].
- If the client says "I'll just know if it comes back" → point out that recognition is poorest when mood is low, and agree a monthly check. Because early detection is the purpose of the plan [Perry-etal-1999].
- If the client fears any low day means relapse → write "setbacks are expected" at the top of the plan. Because relapse prevention treats a lapse as a learning event, not proof of failure [Larimer-etal-1999].
- If there is current or past suicide risk → link the red and crisis levels to the safety plan (see `02-risk.safety-planning`). Because the blueprint does not replace safety planning.
- If the client plans to stop antidepressants → put the prescriber's taper plan and "seek help early if symptoms return" in the plan. Because NICE advises this when stopping medication [NICE-NG222].

## Common problems

- **Therapist-written plan.** A tidy handout the client never reads. Fix: client writes; therapist edits.
- **Too long.** Keep it short enough to read when low; put the setback plan on its own card (expert consensus).
- **No route back.** Name the person, number and how to self-refer.
- **Never tested.** Fix: use it during spaced sessions (expert consensus).

## Evidence

- Psychological relapse prevention (CBT, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), interpersonal psychotherapy) reduced 12-month relapse by 22% against controls across 27 comparisons [Clarke-etal-2015]. Supported by randomized trials.
- Over 6 to 18 months, people treated with acute CBT for depression relapsed less than people whose antidepressant was stopped (odds ratio 2.61), and about as often as people kept on medication [Cuijpers-etal-2013b].
- In an individual participant data meta-analysis of 4 trials (714 people), a psychological intervention while tapering antidepressants gave 15-month relapse rates similar to staying on them (hazard ratio 0.86, not significant) [Breedvelt-etal-2021].
- NICE lists these components for relapse prevention work; group CBT or MBCT for relapse prevention usually runs 8 sessions over 2 to 3 months [NICE-NG222]. Guideline-recommended.
- The written blueprint and traffic-light format are expert consensus; trials test whole programs, not the document.
