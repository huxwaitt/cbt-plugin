**Bottom line:** The Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI) is a free, 16-question, semi-structured interview in DSM-5-TR (the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision) that asks the client how they define, explain and cope with their problem and what help they expect. It took about 23 minutes in the field trial and was judged feasible, acceptable and useful. Use it early, for any client, and feed what it yields into both diagnosis and the cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) formulation.

## What it measures

The CFI turns the DSM Outline for Cultural Formulation (cultural identity, cultural explanations of illness, stressors and supports, and the clinician-client relationship) into questions [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2014]. It is not a scale; it gathers the client's own account. The core has four domains [DeSilva-etal-2015]:

| Domain | What you learn |
|---|---|
| Cultural definition of the problem | The client's words for the problem and what worries them most |
| Cultural perceptions of cause, context and support | Their explanation of the cause, what others think, stressors, supports, and the role of cultural identity |
| Cultural factors affecting self-coping and past help seeking | What they have tried, including family, religious and traditional help, and barriers met |
| Cultural factors affecting current help seeking | What help they want now, and concerns about the clinician or the service |

## Format and scoring

- **Core CFI:** 16 open questions with prompts, for all clients and settings [APA-Assessment-Measures; Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2014].
- **Informant version:** the same domains asked of a family member or caregiver, with the client's consent; useful for children, cognitive impairment, acute psychosis, or clients who want family involved [DeSilva-etal-2015].
- **12 supplementary modules:** explanatory model; level of functioning; social network; psychosocial stressors; spirituality, religion and moral traditions; cultural identity; coping and help seeking; patient-clinician relationship; school-age children and adolescents; older adults; immigrants and refugees; caregivers [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2014]. The modules were not field tested [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2014].
- **Time:** mean 23.4 minutes within a 54.1-minute intake in the international field trial (318 patients, 75 clinicians, six countries); it fell by about 4 minutes after a clinician's first use [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2017].
- **No score.** Write a short narrative summary instead.

## Interpreting scores

There are no scores or cutoffs. Use the answers to:
- Check whether the presentation fits diagnostic criteria or reflects a cultural concept of distress, an idiom, or an expected response in the client's context.
- Judge severity and impairment against the client's own roles and norms.
- Plan engagement: who should be involved, what the client expects from treatment, and what may block trust.

The DSM-5 work group named five situations where cultural assessment matters most: diagnostic difficulty because the clinician is unfamiliar with the client's culture, uncertain fit between symptoms and criteria, difficulty judging severity or impairment, disagreement about the course of care, and limited engagement or adherence [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2014].

**Evidence.** Clinicians and patients rated it feasible, acceptable and clinically useful; clinicians rated feasibility lower until they had used it once [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2017]. In a Swedish pragmatic randomized trial of 256 new outpatients, adding the CFI did not significantly change rates of depression diagnosis (33.6% versus 27.9%; prevalence ratio 1.21, 95% confidence interval 0.83 to 1.75) and did not reduce non-definite diagnoses. Among patients whose first language was not Swedish the ratio was higher (1.61) but still not significant [Wallin-etal-2022]. Qualitative and debriefing studies report better rapport, communication and trust, but these effects have not been tested as trial outcomes [Wallin-etal-2024]. Treat it as expert-consensus good practice with feasibility evidence, not a proven way to change outcomes.

## Guidance

- If you are starting any intake → ask the first CFI questions early, before diagnostic checklists: "People understand problems in different ways. How would you describe what's been happening, in your own words?" Because it was field tested at the start of the evaluation [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2014].
- If the client's explanation differs from the CBT model (spiritual, family, medical) → record it in their words and build it into the formulation rather than correcting it. Because culture shapes how problems are defined and experienced [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If the client belongs to a group historically over-diagnosed with certain disorders → slow down before assigning psychosis or personality disorder, and check how others in their community would see the experience. Because clinicians are expected to recognize historical and social prejudice in diagnosis [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If time is short → use the four domains as headings and ask one question per domain, then complete the rest in session two. Because later CFIs took less time [Lewis-Fernandez-etal-2017].
- If you work through an interpreter → brief them first that you want the client's own terms, not translated clinical labels. Because the CFI's value lies in the client's own explanation (see `13-context-culture-development.working-with-interpreters`).
- Avoid: "So that's what we call depression," said straight after the client's own explanation. It replaces their meaning before you have understood it.
