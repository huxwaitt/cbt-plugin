**Bottom line:** A diagnosis tells you which group of people a client resembles, and so which evidence-based protocol, course and risks to expect. A case formulation tells you why this person's problems started and keep going, and so what to change. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) needs both: use the diagnosis to choose the template, and the formulation to fit it to the person. Neither replaces the other.

## What it is

- **Diagnosis** assigns a category from DSM-5-TR (the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) based on symptoms, duration, impairment and exclusions. It is nomothetic: it describes what people in the category share.
- **Case formulation** is an individual hypothesis linking the client's triggers, thoughts, emotions, body sensations, behaviors and history into a maintenance account. It is idiographic.

In Persons' case formulation approach, assessment aims at both: the diagnosis points to an evidence-based treatment, and the formulation individualizes it and guides decisions when progress stalls [Persons-2008].

## Why it matters in CBT

**What diagnosis gives you:**

| Contribution | Example |
|---|---|
| Choice of protocol | Social anxiety disorder points to a disorder-specific cognitive model and protocol |
| Treatment intensity | The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) splits depression into less severe (Patient Health Questionnaire-9 score below 16) and more severe for treatment choices [NICE-NG222] |
| Expected course and comorbidity | Depression prompts screening for anxiety, substances and past hypomania |
| Risk checks | An eating disorder diagnosis prompts medical monitoring |
| Communication and payment | Shared language with prescribers; a code for insurers |

**What diagnosis does not give you:**
- Why symptoms persist for this client (the maintaining cycle).
- Which beliefs, safety behaviors or avoidance to target.
- Strengths, values, culture and context.
- Problems that cross categories, such as perfectionism or self-criticism.

## Key details

**Validity versus utility.** Few diagnoses are separated from each other or from normality by natural boundaries, so most are not "valid" in the strict sense; many still have high utility because they predict outcome and treatment response [Kendell-Jablensky-2003].

**Reliability is uneven.** In the DSM-5 field trials, 23 diagnoses had precise reliability estimates: 5 very good, 9 good, 6 questionable and 3 unacceptable when two clinicians interviewed the same patient separately with usual clinical methods [Regier-etal-2013]. Most adequately tested diagnoses were in the good to very good range, so treat a diagnosis as a well-supported hypothesis, not a fact.

**Disorders overlap.** In a cohort followed for 20 years, one general psychopathology dimension explained the pattern of disorders better than three separate groups [Caspi-etal-2014]. This supports formulating shared processes, not just treating labels.

**Clients' experience.** A review of 78 studies found that receiving a diagnosis can feel validating or stigmatizing, and supports an individualized, collaborative approach to giving it [Perkins-etal-2018].

## In the session

- If a client asks "Do I have a disorder?" → answer honestly, then link it to the formulation: "Your symptoms fit what's called panic disorder. The name tells us which treatment has the best evidence. Our diagram shows how it works for you, and that is what we will change." Because diagnosis selects the protocol and formulation tailors it [Persons-2008].
- If the diagnosis and the formulation point to different targets → follow the formulation for session work, and recheck the diagnosis. Because formulation guides decisions when the standard plan does not fit [Persons-2008].
- If a client fears the label → explore the fear, explain who will see it, and consider whether recording it would harm them. Because clinicians may refrain from making or reporting a diagnosis that would cause harm [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If you are building a tool → keep "possible diagnosis" (needing clinician confirmation) separate from "formulation elements" (thoughts, behaviors, maintaining cycles), and never let one field fill the other. Because they answer different questions [Persons-2008].
- Avoid: "You're a generalized anxiety disorder case, so we'll do that protocol." It skips the person's own maintenance cycle.
