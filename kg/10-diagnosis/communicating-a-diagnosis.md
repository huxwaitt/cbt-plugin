**Bottom line:** Share a diagnosis as a working description, in plain words, linked straight to what it means for treatment and hope. Ask what the client makes of it, expect relief, fear or disagreement, and tell them who will see it. In the United States the client can usually read the diagnosis in their record, so write it as if they will.

## The principle

A systematic review of 78 qualitative studies (2,228 service users, clinicians and carers in 13 countries) found six themes in how diagnosis is experienced: disclosure, information provision, collaboration, timing, stigma, and the diagnosis's practical value for recovery. Experiences ranged from validating to harmful, and the authors recommend an individualized, collaborative, holistic approach [Perkins-etal-2018]. Counselors must help clients understand the implications of a diagnosis [ACA-Ethics-2014].

Diagnosis is not hidden information. Under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), clients have a right to see and copy their designated record set; the provider must act within 30 days, with one 30-day extension allowed, and may deny access only on narrow grounds such as danger to life or physical safety [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-524]. Psychotherapy notes are excluded from that right, but the definition of psychotherapy notes itself excludes summaries of diagnosis, symptoms, treatment plan and progress [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-501]. In the UK, the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) gives a similar right of access (see `14-ethics-law.hipaa-and-gdpr`).

## Guidance

- If you have a working diagnosis → share it in the session where you share the early formulation, as a hypothesis: name, what it means, what it does not mean, what helps. Because diagnosis points to the treatment and formulation makes it personal [Persons-2008].
- If the client seems relieved → acknowledge it and add the limits: "It's a description, not a verdict on who you are." Because diagnosis can feel validating, which helps engagement [Perkins-etal-2018].
- If the client looks frightened or ashamed → slow down and ask what the word means to them before giving more facts. Because stigma is a central theme in how diagnoses land [Perkins-etal-2018].
- If the client disagrees with the diagnosis → ask which parts do not fit, review the evidence together, and keep it provisional if the doubt is reasonable. Because collaboration in reaching a diagnosis shapes how it is experienced [Perkins-etal-2018].
- If the diagnosis will go on an insurance claim → tell the client before submission, what code goes where, and their options (for example, self-pay). Because clients should understand the implications of diagnosis and how reports will be used [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If recording a diagnosis could harm the client and is not required → discuss it with them and your supervisor. Because counselors may refrain from making or reporting a diagnosis that would cause harm [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If you write the diagnosis in notes → use neutral, accurate wording you would read aloud to the client. Because the diagnosis summary is part of the record the client can access [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-501].
- If the diagnosis is serious (bipolar disorder, psychosis, personality disorder) → give written information, invite questions next session, and check that the prescriber or team knows. Because timing and information provision affect how diagnosis is received [Perkins-etal-2018].

## Example phrasings

- "From what you've told me, your experiences fit what's called panic disorder. It's common and it responds well to the treatment we'll do. What do you make of that?"
- "A diagnosis is a name for a pattern lots of people share. The diagram we drew is about how it works for you, and that's what we'll change."
- "Some people find a name a relief; others find it worrying. How does it feel for you?"
- "If we bill your insurance, this code goes on the claim. Let's talk through what that means before we decide."
- Avoid: "You're borderline." It reduces the person to a label and carries stigma.
- Avoid: "Don't worry about the diagnosis, it's just for insurance." It is untrue and undermines trust when the client reads their record.

## Evidence

Evidence on how to communicate a diagnosis comes from qualitative studies and expert consensus, not trials [Perkins-etal-2018]. The access rights are law [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-524], and the duty to explain implications is a professional ethics standard [ACA-Ethics-2014].
