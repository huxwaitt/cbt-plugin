**Bottom line:** Comorbidity is the rule in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) practice: most clients with an anxiety or mood disorder have, or have had, another one. Assess for it on purpose, because routine interviews miss it. Then order the work: risk first, then problems that block therapy, then the problem that causes most interference or drives the others, guided by the client's priorities. A single disorder-focused protocol often improves co-occurring problems too.

## What it is

Comorbidity means meeting criteria for two or more disorders at the same time (current) or across life (lifetime).

- In a US population survey, 45% of people with any 12-month disorder had two or more, and severity rose with the number of disorders [Kessler-etal-2005b].
- In 1,127 outpatients with a principal anxiety or mood disorder, 57% had at least one other current disorder and 81% had one across their lifetime [Brown-etal-2001b].
- In a Dutch cohort, 67% of people with a depressive disorder had a current anxiety disorder; 63% of people with an anxiety disorder had current depression. Where both occurred, anxiety came first in 57% [Lamers-etal-2011].

## Why it matters in CBT

- **Prognosis and risk.** Comorbid clients have more severe and longer-lasting symptoms [Lamers-etal-2011; Kessler-etal-2005b].
- **Missed diagnoses.** Routine assessment misses or mislabels disorders: in one adult clinic, 29% of major depressive disorder diagnoses were not supported on structured interview, and in about half of those an anxiety disorder fitted better; many depression diagnoses later convert to bipolar disorder [Bradford-etal-2024].
- **Shared processes.** Disorders cluster because they share vulnerabilities and maintaining processes; in a cohort followed for 20 years, disorders were explained better by one general psychopathology dimension than by three separate groups [Caspi-etal-2014]. That is why avoidance, worry, rumination and safety behaviors appear across diagnoses (see `15-transdiagnostic-processes.transdiagnostic-overview`).

## Key details

**Common patterns to screen for:**

| If the client has | Also screen for |
|---|---|
| Depression | Anxiety disorders, past hypomania, alcohol or drug use, insomnia |
| Generalized anxiety disorder | Depression, other anxiety disorders, insomnia |
| Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) | Depression, substance use, self-harm |
| Panic disorder | Agoraphobia, depression, alcohol used to cope |
| Any anxiety or mood disorder | Substance use, eating problems, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) |

The anxiety and depression pairings rest on clinic and cohort data [Brown-etal-2001b; Lamers-etal-2011]; the other rows are expert consensus for screening, not prevalence figures.

**Does comorbidity weaken CBT?** Across 148 anxiety treatment samples (CBT, medication and other treatments), overall comorbidity was generally unrelated to effect size; it predicted worse outcomes in mixed anxiety samples but better outcomes in panic and PTSD samples [Olatunji-etal-2010]. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) delivered alongside psychiatric conditions produced insomnia remission in 36.0% versus 16.9% of controls across 37 randomized trials, with small gains in the comorbid condition, larger for psychiatric than medical conditions [Wu-etal-2015]. A transdiagnostic option, the Unified Protocol, showed a moderate effect (g = 0.452) over controls across 19 randomized trials [Carlucci-etal-2021].

**Decision order for what to treat first** (expert consensus built from the problem-list approach [Persons-2008]):

1. Risk to life (suicide, self-harm, harm to others, medical risk in eating disorders). Follow `02-risk.crisis-response` when risk is imminent (US: call or text 988, or 911; UK: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111, Samaritans 116 123).
2. Conditions that make standard CBT unsafe or ineffective until addressed: active mania or psychosis, severe substance dependence needing withdrawal management, medical instability.
3. Therapy-interfering problems (for example, missed sessions from substance use).
4. The disorder that causes most distress or impairment, or that drives the others in your formulation (for example, panic driving the low mood).
5. The client's own priority, where 4 leaves a choice.

## In the session

- If a client meets criteria for two disorders → share both and ask "Which of these gets in the way of your life most right now?" Because agreed priorities make the problem list workable [Persons-2008].
- If one disorder appears to drive the other (panic leading to restricted life and low mood) → treat the driver first and track both with measures. Because across anxiety treatments overall comorbidity was generally unrelated to outcome [Olatunji-etal-2010].
- If insomnia persists alongside depression or anxiety → offer CBT-I directly rather than waiting for it to resolve. Because CBT-I works in comorbid insomnia [Wu-etal-2015].
- If several anxiety and mood problems overlap with no clear driver → consider a transdiagnostic protocol. Because the Unified Protocol targets shared processes across emotional disorders [Carlucci-etal-2021].
- If substance use is severe enough to block learning in sessions or needs medical withdrawal → coordinate with addiction or medical services before or alongside CBT. Because withdrawal from some substances needs medical management and intoxication blocks new learning (see `substance-use.assessment`).
- Record every diagnosis with its code, which one is principal (the main reason for this episode of care) and why, and when you will review the order.
