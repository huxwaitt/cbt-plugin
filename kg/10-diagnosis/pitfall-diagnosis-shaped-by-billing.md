**Bottom line:** A diagnosis on a claim must be the one your assessment supports, made by someone allowed to make it. Inflating severity to get sessions, inventing a disorder to cover relationship or work problems, or softening a diagnosis to spare a client stigma all make the record false. Discuss the payment and stigma problem openly with the client instead of solving it with the code.

## What it looks like

- "Major depressive disorder, recurrent, severe" on a claim for a client whose notes describe mild symptoms, because the payer authorizes more sessions for severe depression.
- A disorder code assigned when the real focus is a relationship or work problem that would be coded with a Z code [CDC-ICD-10-CM-2027].
- Bipolar disorder or a personality disorder recorded as "adjustment disorder" to protect the client, so the next clinician or prescriber is misled.
- An unlicensed staff member, a trainee working alone or a software tool fills in the diagnosis field.
- The client first learns their diagnosis from an insurer letter.

## Why it happens

- Payers link authorization and session limits to diagnosis and severity.
- Clinicians want to help clients get care or avoid stigma, life-insurance or employment consequences.
- Workflows require a code before the assessment is complete.
- Roles are unclear in team, digital or cross-state services.

## The fix

- If your assessment does not support the code a payer wants → record the diagnosis you found and appeal or discuss alternatives with the client. Because reports to health insurers must be accurate, honest and objective [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If the main problem is relational, occupational or social and no disorder is present → use the appropriate Z code and tell the client honestly what insurance may or may not cover. Because a diagnosis requires proper assessment, not convenience [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If a code is needed before assessment is complete → use a provisional or unspecified diagnosis and update it when you know more, rather than guessing a specific disorder. Because a diagnosis must rest on proper assessment [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If a client fears what a diagnosis on a claim could mean → explain who sees it, discuss self-pay as an option, and let them decide before you submit. Because clients should understand the implications of diagnosis and billing [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If a diagnosis would harm the client and payment does not require it → discuss not recording one, rather than recording a false one. Because counselors may refrain from making or reporting a harmful diagnosis, but must not misreport [ACA-Ethics-2014].
- If you do not have diagnostic authority → describe and refer; do not record a diagnosis. Because practice must stay within competence and credentials [ACA-Ethics-2014], and payer coverage of diagnosis is limited to what state law authorizes [CFR-42-410-73].
- Write every diagnosis as if the client will read it, because they can request their record [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-524].

**Example phrasing:** "Your insurer will only pay if there's a diagnosis, and from our assessment what you're dealing with is a relationship problem rather than a mental disorder. I won't put down something that isn't accurate. Let's look at the options together: self-pay, a sliding scale, or another service."

## Sources note

This pitfall rests on professional codes and regulation rather than outcome research. The American Counseling Association code is used here because it addresses diagnosis directly; the codes of psychologists, social workers and marriage and family therapists carry similar duties of accuracy and competence (see `14-ethics-law.professional-codes`). Payer rules and state law vary; check both.
