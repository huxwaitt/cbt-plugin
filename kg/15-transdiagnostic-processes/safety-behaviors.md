**Bottom line:** A safety behavior is an action a person takes in or around a feared situation to prevent, escape or reduce a feared catastrophe or the distress that comes with it [Blakey-Abramowitz-2016]. Because the catastrophe does not happen, the person credits the safety behavior, so the threat belief survives every exposure. In small experiments in social phobia and in panic disorder with agoraphobia, exposure with safety behaviors dropped produced larger falls in belief and anxiety than exposure with them kept [Wells-etal-1995; Salkovskis-etal-1999].

## Components

- **Feared outcome.** A specific prediction: "I'll faint," "They'll see I'm shaking," "I'll get ill and die," "Something bad will happen to my family."
- **Safety behavior.** What the person does to stop it. Types:
  - Overt actions: holding on to furniture, sitting near exits, carrying medication or water.
  - Subtle actions: avoiding eye contact, gripping a glass tightly, speaking quickly.
  - Mental actions: rehearsing sentences, monitoring the heart, counting, praying in a set way, reassuring oneself.
  - Interpersonal actions: asking for reassurance, bringing a companion.
- **Misattribution.** "I didn't faint because I sat down." The non-occurrence of disaster is credited to the behavior rather than to the belief being wrong.
- **Side effects.** Many safety behaviors keep attention on threat or create the very signs the person fears, such as looking odd while rehearsing speech.

## How it keeps the problem going

Safety behaviors keep anxiety going because they prevent the person from benefiting from disconfirming experience [Salkovskis-etal-1999]. Evidence:

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Wells and colleagues, social phobia | 8 patients; one session of exposure alone and one of exposure with fewer safety behaviors, counterbalanced | Exposure with fewer safety behaviors reduced within-situation anxiety and belief in the feared catastrophe more [Wells-etal-1995] |
| Salkovskis and colleagues, panic with agoraphobia | Brief 15-minute exposure with safety behaviors stopped or kept; behavior test repeated within two days | Stopping them gave significantly greater decreases in catastrophic beliefs and anxiety [Salkovskis-etal-1999] |

From an inhibitory learning view, safety signals also block the new learning that exposure aims for, and removing them is one of eight strategies for maximizing exposure [Craske-etal-2014]. Safety behaviors are reinforced the same way avoidance is: they bring quick relief [Krypotos-etal-2015].

There is a real debate. Rachman and colleagues argue that judicious use of safety behavior, especially early in treatment, can help people enter situations and need not block disconfirmation [Rachman-etal-2008]. A later review found the evidence on safety behaviors during exposure limited, mixed and controversial [Blakey-Abramowitz-2016]. Practical reading: aim to drop them, and allow planned, time-limited use only when it is the difference between doing an exposure and refusing it.

## Drawing it with the client

1. Pick a recent feared situation. Ask: "What were you afraid would happen?" Rate belief 0 to 100.
2. Ask: "What did you do to stop that happening, or to cope?" Then ask about mental and subtle actions: "Was there anything you did in your head? Anything you were careful about?"
3. Ask: "What do you think would have happened if you had not done that?"
4. Draw the loop: feared outcome, safety behavior, "it didn't happen," credit to the safety behavior, belief stays high.

- If the client says the behavior is just a habit → ask what they would fear if it were impossible. Because a behavior aimed at preventing a feared catastrophe is a safety behavior whatever its form [Blakey-Abramowitz-2016].
- If the client cannot face the situation without it → plan a staged drop, with the behavior as a time-limited step to remove. Because judicious early use can help engagement [Rachman-etal-2008].
- If exposures are completed but belief does not move → search for subtle and mental safety behaviors before adding more exposure. Because these prevent disconfirmation [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- If the client fears that dropping it is dangerous → frame it as a test. "Let's find out what happens when you leave the water bottle behind for ten minutes. What do you predict?" Because a written prediction that is then tested is what lets the threat belief be disconfirmed [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- Avoid: "Just stop doing that and you'll see it's fine."

What to record: each safety behavior, its feared outcome, belief rating before and after each experiment, and which behaviors remain.

## Treatment targets it implies

- Behavioral experiments comparing the situation with and without the safety behavior, with a written prediction (`behavioral.behavioral-experiments`).
- Exposure with safety signals removed (`behavioral.exposure-principles`).
- Systematic identification and fading (`behavioral.safety-behavior-reduction`).
- Reassurance seeking and checking in health anxiety and obsessive-compulsive disorder are treated as safety behaviors; see the condition models.
