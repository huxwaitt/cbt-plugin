**Bottom line:** Worry and rumination are processes that attach to whatever content is available. Disputing each topic in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) leaves the process untouched and can turn the therapist into a reassurance source. Formulate and target the process, its beliefs and its functions instead [Ehring-etal-2011; Behar-etal-2009].

## What it looks like

- Thought records fill up with a different worry each week: health one week, money the next, a child's safety after that.
- The client leaves sessions relieved, then calls or emails between sessions with a new "what if."
- Worry minutes per day, or hours of rumination, stay flat while individual worries "resolve."
- The therapist feels they are winning each argument and losing the treatment.
- Notes record topics, not triggers, duration or beliefs about worrying.

## Why it happens

- Standard thought testing works well for single hot thoughts, so it is the default tool.
- Repetitive negative thinking (RNT) shares the same process features across content: it is repetitive, intrusive and hard to disengage from [Ehring-etal-2011]. Content changes; the process does not.
- The contemporary worry models share an emphasis on avoidance of internal experience, which content debate does not address [Behar-etal-2009].
- Positive beliefs about worry ("it keeps me prepared") and negative beliefs ("I can't control it") maintain it, and these are rarely asked about when the focus is the topic [Nuevo-etal-2004].
- Abstract, evaluative processing is part of what makes repetitive thought unconstructive; arguing about meaning can keep the client in that mode [Watkins-2008].

## The fix

- If a third session in a row is spent on a new worry topic → stop and map the process: trigger, duration, effect on mood, what the worry is trying to achieve. Because the process is shared across topics [Ehring-etal-2011].
- If the client says worrying helps → elicit and test positive and negative beliefs about worry with experiments such as postponement. Because these beliefs predict worry severity [Nuevo-etal-2004].
- If the client wants the therapist to confirm things will be fine → name the pattern and agree a plan not to reassure. "I've noticed we often end with me helping you feel sure. Shall we see what happens if we work on living with not being sure?" Because the main models of generalized anxiety disorder agree that worry serves to avoid internal experience, which reassurance about content does not address [Behar-etal-2009].
- If rumination asks "why" → switch to concrete "how" questions about one next step. Because concrete processing is linked to constructive outcomes [Watkins-2008].
- If a worry concerns a real, current problem → solve it once, then return to the process. Because rumination impairs problem solving, while practical action helps [Nolen-Hoeksema-etal-2008].

Example phrasing: "We've looked at a lot of different worries together. What stays the same each time is the worrying itself. Could we make that the thing we work on?"

## Sources note

Supported by process research on repetitive negative thinking and by reviews of worry models; no trial directly compares content-focused and process-focused responses within the same protocol.
