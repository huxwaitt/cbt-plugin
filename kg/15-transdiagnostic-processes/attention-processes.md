**Bottom line:** Where attention goes shapes what a person concludes. Heightened self-focused attention appears across almost every disorder studied [Ingram-1990], and anxious people preferentially attend to threat (d = 0.45 across 172 studies) [Bar-Haim-etal-2007]. In cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), the practical work is to notice where attention goes in feared situations, show how it creates misleading evidence, and practice flexible, outward attention, rather than to rely on computerized bias training, whose clinical effects are small and uncertain [Fodor-etal-2020].

## What it is

| Process | Description | Typical presentations |
|---|---|---|
| Self-focused attention | Attention turned inward to sensations, feelings, behavior or an imagined image of how one looks | Social anxiety, depression, panic, generalized anxiety |
| Attentional bias to threat | Fast, often automatic, preferential attention to threat cues | Anxiety disorders, in children and adults [Bar-Haim-etal-2007] |
| Threat monitoring | Deliberate, repeated scanning for danger: the body, faces, the environment | Health anxiety, panic, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) |

Mor and Winquist found that private self-focus (on feelings and thoughts) was more strongly linked with depression and generalized anxiety, while public self-focus (on how one appears to others) was more strongly linked with social anxiety [Mor-Winquist-2002].

## Why it matters in CBT

- **It is shared.** Ingram concluded that self-focused attention has little power to discriminate between disorders because it is raised in all of them [Ingram-1990]. That makes it a useful transdiagnostic target.
- **It manufactures evidence.** A person scanning their chest notices normal heartbeats and reads them as danger. A person monitoring their own voice hears every stumble and assumes others do too. Internal information is mistaken for external fact.
- **It crowds out disconfirming information.** Attention on self or threat means the reassuring parts of a situation (a friendly face, a normal outcome) are missed.
- **It interacts with mood.** Across 226 effect sizes, self-focus was more strongly tied to negative affect in clinical samples, and ruminative self-focus showed stronger links than nonruminative self-focus [Mor-Winquist-2002].

## Key details

- The threat bias appears with stimuli outside awareness as well as with consciously seen threat, so clients may not notice they are doing it [Bar-Haim-etal-2007].
- Cognitive bias modification (CBM), computerized training to shift attention or interpretation, is not a substitute for CBT. Across 49 trials its effects were small and became nonsignificant after adjustment for outliers and publication bias [Cristea-etal-2015]. A later network meta-analysis of 85 trials found consistent but small benefits, with interpretation bias training outperforming sham for anxiety and attention bias training showing benefit only in sensitivity analyses [Fodor-etal-2020].
- The attention training technique (ATT), a brief metacognitive exercise in switching and dividing auditory attention, showed promising effects in a review of 10 small studies (295 participants); larger trials are needed [Knowles-etal-2016].

## In the session

- If a client describes a feared situation → ask where their attention was. "When you were giving the talk, what were you focused on: the audience, or how you felt and looked?" Because self-focus is the common route to biased conclusions across disorders [Ingram-1990].
- If a client uses a sensation or feeling as proof ("I felt so awkward, so I must have looked awkward") → name the process: their attention was on internal information, which is not the same as what others saw. Then test it with feedback or video. Because public self-focus is strongly tied to social anxiety [Mor-Winquist-2002].
- If a client checks their body repeatedly → run a brief experiment: focus on one body area for two minutes and rate how noticeable sensations become. Because turning attention inward heightens awareness of internal states, which can then be read as danger [Ingram-1990].
- If a client asks about apps that "retrain attention" → explain that the evidence for clinical benefit is small and uncertain and that in-session work on attention is better supported. Because CBM effects are small and fragile [Cristea-etal-2015; Fodor-etal-2020].
- If attention is stuck inward during exposure → coach external focus: describe three things you see, what people are doing, what is actually happening. Because exposure works by violating the feared expectation, which the client must notice to learn from [Craske-etal-2014].

Example phrasing: "Let's try the same conversation twice: once watching yourself closely, and once really paying attention to the other person. Then compare how anxious you felt and how it went."
Avoid: "Just don't focus on yourself."

What to record: where attention went in each feared situation (percentage inward versus outward, estimated by the client), the internal cues used as evidence, and results of attention-shift experiments.
