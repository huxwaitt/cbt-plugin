**Bottom line:** Advising a client to push thoughts away is advice to suppress, and suppression is effortful, unreliable and tends to bring the thought back and raise distress [Wegner-etal-1987; Najmi-etal-2009]. Replace it with allowing the thought while engaging in a chosen activity, and test the switch as an experiment.

## What it looks like

- "Whenever it pops up, just tell yourself 'stop' and think of something else."
- A rubber band on the wrist to snap at the thought.
- Homework to "keep busy so the thoughts don't come."
- A trauma survivor told to "put it behind you" and avoid anything that reminds them.
- The client reports trying harder each week, feeling worse each week, and wondering if they are losing their mind.

## Why it happens

- It feels kind and common-sense, and it matches what clients already try.
- Thought stopping was once a taught technique, and some clients arrive having learned it.
- Brief relief in session looks like success.
- The rebound evidence is mixed in size, which makes the risk easy to dismiss: rebound is small to moderate overall [Abramowitz-etal-2001], and clinical groups do not show more recurrence than others [Magee-etal-2012]. But the main cost in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is added distress, not just frequency [Najmi-etal-2009].

## The fix

- If you are about to say "try not to think about it" → offer allowing instead. "Let the thought be there, notice it, and bring your attention back to what you're doing." Because acceptance and focused distraction managed intrusive thoughts better than suppression [Najmi-etal-2009].
- If distraction is used → make it focused and absorbing, chosen in advance, and aimed at continuing life rather than escaping the thought. Because a specific distracter reduced later preoccupation in the original experiments [Wegner-etal-1987].
- If the client believes they must control their thoughts → run an in-session suppression experiment and discuss what happened. Because suppression tends to produce the state it aims to prevent [Wenzlaff-Wegner-2000].
- If the client was taught thought stopping elsewhere → acknowledge it was offered in good faith, explain the reason for the change, and agree a two-week trial of allowing. Because thought stopping is a form of suppression, and suppression mainly raises distress in OCD [Najmi-etal-2009].
- If the thoughts are of harming self or others → do not treat them only as intrusions to allow; assess intent, plans and history the same session (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`, `02-risk.harm-to-others`), and consult a supervisor if you cannot tell an unwanted, resisted obsessional intrusion (`ocd.cbt-model`) from risk. Because allowing is the right response to intrusions but not to suicidal or violent intent. In the US, call or text 988 or call 911 in an emergency; in the UK, call 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123.

Example phrasing: "Trying hard not to think about something is like trying not to notice a sound in the room. What if we let the thought be background noise while you get on with things?"

## Sources note

Supported by experimental research on suppression and by a direct comparison of suppression, focused distraction and acceptance in OCD; the size of the rebound effect varies across studies.
