**Bottom line:** Worry is repetitive, mostly verbal thinking about possible future threats, an attempt at mental problem solving on an issue whose outcome is uncertain [Borkovec-etal-1983b; Ehring-etal-2011]. It persists because it feels useful, because it dampens emotional imagery and body arousal, and because people come to fear the worry itself [Borkovec-Inz-1990; Behar-etal-2009]. Target the process and the beliefs about worry, not each worry topic.

## Components

- **Trigger.** An uncertain situation, a news item, a body sensation, a quiet moment, an intrusive "what if."
- **The chain.** "What if I make a mistake? Then they'll fire me. Then I can't pay rent." Each step raises a new threat; few chains reach a decision.
- **Verbal form.** Worry is mainly thought in words rather than images. In generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), successful therapy shifted clients' balance of thought and imagery toward that of nonanxious people [Borkovec-Inz-1990].
- **Beliefs about worry.** Positive beliefs ("Worry helps me prepare") keep it going. In Wells' metacognitive model, negative beliefs that worry is uncontrollable or dangerous create "worry about worry," and this meta-worry predicts worry severity [Nuevo-etal-2004].
- **Relation to rumination.** Worry and rumination are highly correlated and share process features (repetitive, abstract, mainly verbal). The main difference is content: worry looks forward to threats, rumination looks back at losses and symptoms [Ehring-etal-2011].

## How it keeps the problem going

Behar and colleagues reviewed five contemporary models of GAD (avoidance, intolerance of uncertainty, metacognitive, emotion dysregulation and acceptance-based) and found they share an emphasis on avoiding internal experiences such as thoughts, beliefs and emotions [Behar-etal-2009]. A later model, contrast avoidance, adds a sixth route:

| Model | Maintaining route |
|---|---|
| Avoidance model (Borkovec) | Verbal worry suppresses emotional imagery and arousal, so fear is not processed [Borkovec-Inz-1990; Borkovec-Hu-1990] |
| Intolerance of uncertainty model (Dugas) | Uncertainty feels unacceptable, so the person worries to reduce it [Behar-etal-2009] |
| Metacognitive model (Wells) | Positive beliefs start worry; negative beliefs about uncontrollability and danger escalate it [Nuevo-etal-2004] |
| Emotion dysregulation and acceptance-based models (two models, grouped as emotional and experiential) | Intense emotion is experienced as aversive and worry is used to manage it [Behar-etal-2009] |
| Contrast avoidance model (Newman, later than the review) | Worry creates and sustains negative emotion so that a sudden sharp rise in distress would feel smaller [Newman-Przeworski-2024] |

In one experiment, speech-anxious people who worried before imagining a feared scene showed less heart rate response but more subjective fear than those who thought neutrally. The authors concluded worry may block emotional processing and preserve the fear [Borkovec-Hu-1990].

## Drawing it with the client

Map one recent episode: trigger, first "what if," the chain, how long it lasted, mood and body after, and what the worry was trying to achieve. Then ask what the client believes about worrying.

- If the client says worry keeps them prepared → test it. Ask how many worried-about events happened and whether the worry helped with those that did. Because positive beliefs about worry help start and maintain it [Nuevo-etal-2004].
- If the client says "I'll lose my mind if I can't stop" → record this as a negative belief about worry and plan to test it with postponement experiments. Because beliefs that worry is uncontrollable and dangerous escalate it [Nuevo-etal-2004].
- If the worry is about a real, current problem with a possible action → move to problem solving and choose a step. Because worry is an attempt at mental problem solving that rarely reaches resolution [Ehring-etal-2011].
- If the worry is about a remote, uncontrollable possibility → do not try to prove it will not happen; work on tolerating not knowing. Because in the intolerance of uncertainty model it is the uncertainty, not the particular outcome, that drives the worry [Behar-etal-2009]. See `15-transdiagnostic-processes.intolerance-of-uncertainty`.
- If worry seems to keep a feared image or scenario at bay → consider imaginal exposure to that image. Because verbal worry may block emotional processing [Borkovec-Hu-1990].

Example phrasing: "We could spend the session on whether this particular thing will happen, and next week there would be a new one. Shall we look at what the worrying itself is doing for you?"
Avoid: "That's very unlikely, you don't need to worry about it."

What to record: worry triggers, minutes per day, percentage of the day spent worrying, positive and negative beliefs about worry with 0 to 100 belief ratings, and the Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ), a 16-item measure of trait worry [Meyer-etal-1990].

## Treatment targets it implies

- Beliefs about worry: verbal challenge and behavioral experiments such as postponement (metacognitive targets).
- Intolerance of uncertainty: graded experiments in uncertainty.
- Avoided imagery and emotion: imaginal exposure and emotion-focused work.
- Practical problems: problem solving (`behavioral.problem-solving-therapy`).
- Choose among these with the client's formulation; `gad.worry-models-compared` compares the evidence.
