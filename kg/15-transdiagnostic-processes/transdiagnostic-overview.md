**Bottom line:** Many disorders are kept going by the same small set of processes: avoidance, safety behaviors, repetitive negative thinking (worry and rumination), biased attention, intolerance of uncertainty, perfectionism, self-criticism and difficulty regulating emotion. Harvey and colleagues identified cognitive and behavioral processes, such as selective attention and repetitive negative thinking, that are implicated across four or more disorders [Harvey-etal-2004; Dalgleish-etal-2020]. In cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), formulating at the level of process helps when a client has several diagnoses or none, but it adds to the disorder-specific model with trial evidence rather than replacing it.

## What it is

A transdiagnostic approach cuts across diagnostic boundaries or sets them aside [Dalgleish-etal-2020]. The word is used in two senses:

- **Transdiagnostic processes.** Cognitive, behavioral and emotional processes found in several disorders, in both clinical and nonclinical samples. Harvey and colleagues reviewed processes of attention, memory, reasoning, thought and behavior, and identified a set implicated across four or more disorders, such as selective attention, overgeneral memory and repetitive negative thinking [Harvey-etal-2004; Dalgleish-etal-2020].
- **Transdiagnostic treatments.** Protocols that apply the same treatment principles across disorders without tailoring the protocol to the diagnosis [Dalgleish-etal-2020]. See `15-transdiagnostic-processes.transdiagnostic-treatments`.

Dalgleish and colleagues separate "soft" approaches, which keep diagnoses and look for shared processes, from "hard" approaches, which replace diagnoses with new frameworks [Dalgleish-etal-2020]. Routine CBT is mostly soft: the diagnosis points to an anchoring model, and processes explain what keeps this client's problems going.

## Why it matters in CBT

- **Comorbidity is common.** In the Dunedin cohort, followed for 20 years from adolescence to midlife, one general psychopathology dimension (the p factor) explained the structure of disorders better than three separate factors. Its authors argue this is why causes and treatments specific to one disorder are hard to find [Caspi-etal-2014].
- **One process, many problems.** Rumination is associated with depression, anxiety, binge eating, binge drinking and self-harm [Nolen-Hoeksema-etal-2008], and it is the worked example of a transdiagnostic risk factor in Nolen-Hoeksema and Watkins's model [Nolen-Hoeksema-Watkins-2011]. Across anxiety, depression, eating and substance-related disorders, rumination showed a large association with symptoms, and avoidance and suppression a medium to large one [Aldao-etal-2010].
- **Shared mechanisms within a disorder family.** Fairburn and colleagues proposed that anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and atypical eating disorders share core maintaining mechanisms, with four extra processes in some patients: clinical perfectionism, core low self-esteem, mood intolerance and interpersonal difficulties [Fairburn-etal-2003].
- **Treatments built on shared processes work.** Across 24 randomized trials, transdiagnostic treatments outperformed control conditions for anxiety and depression, though trial quality was generally poor [Newby-etal-2015].

Limits to keep in mind:
- **Same process, different outcomes.** One transdiagnostic risk factor can lead to several disorders (multifinality), and two people with the same factor can develop different symptoms. Moderators such as environment and other vulnerabilities shape which disorder appears [Nolen-Hoeksema-Watkins-2011].
- **Content still matters.** Dalgleish and colleagues list a neglect of mental content among five challenges for the field, alongside theory, setting diagnoses aside, research methods and joined-up science [Dalgleish-etal-2020].
- **Most process evidence is correlational.** Treat each process as a hypothesis to test in this client, not a fact about them.

## In the session

- If a client meets criteria for two or more disorders → ask which habits run through all of them (for example worry before work and rumination after it) and draw one shared loop. Because shared processes help explain why disorders co-occur [Caspi-etal-2014; Harvey-etal-2004].
- If a disorder-specific model fits the main problem → anchor on it and add a process only where it explains something the model misses. Because routine practice keeps diagnoses and uses processes to individualize, the soft approach [Dalgleish-etal-2020].
- If the client asks why their problems keep changing shape → name the shared habit in their words. "The worry about work and the going over of arguments at night look different, but both are your mind trying to fix something by thinking about it over and over." Because one transdiagnostic process can show up as several different symptom pictures (multifinality) [Nolen-Hoeksema-Watkins-2011].
- If you suspect a process → collect a recent example, its short-term payoff and its long-term cost, and rate it before and during treatment (see `15-transdiagnostic-processes.assessing-transdiagnostic-processes`). Because process-disorder links are mostly correlational and need testing case by case [Nolen-Hoeksema-Watkins-2011].

What to record: each process identified, one concrete example, the measure or rating used, and which presenting problem it maintains.
