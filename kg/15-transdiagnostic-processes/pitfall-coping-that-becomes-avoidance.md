**Bottom line:** A coping skill becomes a safety behavior when the client uses it to prevent a feared catastrophe or to escape distress during exposure. Then the non-occurrence of disaster is credited to the skill and the threat belief survives [Salkovskis-etal-1999; Blakey-Abramowitz-2016]. Judge each strategy by its function, and plan for it to be dropped during exposures.

## What it looks like

- "I did the supermarket, but only because I did my breathing the whole time."
- The client counts, prays, grips a bottle of water or repeats "I'm safe" throughout an exposure.
- A partner or the therapist always comes along.
- Subjective distress falls within sessions, but belief in the feared outcome (for example "I'll faint") stays high.
- Gains collapse when the strategy is unavailable: no phone, no companion, no time to breathe first.

## Why it happens

- The same act can be adaptive coping or a maintaining safety behavior. Hofmann and Hay argue that its function decides which [Hofmann-Hay-2018], and function is easy to miss.
- Therapists teach skills early to help engagement, which can be reasonable: judicious early use of safety behavior can help some clients approach feared situations [Rachman-etal-2008]. The problem is never planning its removal.
- Evidence on safety behaviors during exposure is mixed, which makes it tempting to leave them in [Blakey-Abramowitz-2016].
- Clients and therapists both find lower distress reassuring, though exposure works by violating the feared expectation rather than by keeping distress low [Craske-etal-2014].

## The fix

- If a client credits a skill for getting through an exposure → ask "What would have happened if you hadn't done it?" and rate belief 0 to 100. Because a strategy used to prevent a feared outcome blocks disconfirmation [Salkovskis-etal-1999].
- If the answer is a catastrophe → design the next exposure without the strategy and frame it as a test of the prediction. Because removing safety signals is one of the strategies that strengthens exposure [Craske-etal-2014].
- If the client cannot start without the strategy → allow it for an agreed, short phase, with a written date to drop it. Because judicious early use can help engagement [Rachman-etal-2008].
- If you teach relaxation or breathing → say in advance that the aim of exposure is learning that the feared outcome does not happen or can be handled, not staying calm. Because inhibitory learning depends on expectancy violation [Craske-etal-2014].
- If the strategy serves a value or daily functioning (for example a hearing aid, a medication taken as prescribed) → keep it. Because function, not form, defines a safety behavior [Hofmann-Hay-2018].

Example phrasing: "Breathing helped you get started, which is great. Next time, let's find out what happens if you let the anxiety be there without it. What do you predict?"

## Sources note

Supported by experimental studies in panic and social anxiety and by inhibitory learning research; whether planned, faded safety behaviors harm long-term outcomes remains debated.
