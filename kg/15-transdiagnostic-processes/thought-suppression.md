**Bottom line:** Thought suppression is deliberately trying not to think about something. It tends to backfire: people cannot hold the thought away, and it often returns more often afterward (the rebound effect) [Wegner-etal-1987]. Meta-analysis shows a small to moderate rebound [Abramowitz-etal-2001], and in obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) the main harm of suppression is more distress rather than more intrusions [Najmi-etal-2009]. In cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), replace suppression with allowing the thought, or with focused, absorbing activity, and test this with the client.

## What it is

In the original "white bear" experiments, people asked not to think of a white bear for 5 minutes could not do it, and when later allowed to think of it they had more bear thoughts than people who had been free to think of it from the start [Wegner-etal-1987]. Suppression is a popular form of mental control that can bring about the very state of mind a person hoped to avoid [Wenzlaff-Wegner-2000]. Suppression has been proposed as a cause or maintaining factor in depression, generalized anxiety disorder, specific phobia, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and OCD [Purdon-1999].

Common clinical forms:
- "I just push it out of my head."
- Snapping a rubber band or shouting "stop" internally (thought stopping).
- Constantly checking whether the thought is still there.
- Avoiding reminders so the thought will not come.

## Why it matters in CBT

- **It feeds intrusions and distress.** The effort to suppress keeps the thought primed, and each return is read as a sign the thought is important or uncontrollable.
- **It adds a second problem.** The client now fears both the thought and their failure to control it.
- **It belongs to the avoidance family.** Across anxiety, depression, eating and substance-related disorders, suppression had a medium to large association with symptoms [Aldao-etal-2010].

## Key details

What the evidence does and does not show:

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Rebound after suppression is small to moderate and varies with the target thought and how frequency is measured | [Abramowitz-etal-2001] |
| Clinical samples did not show larger rebound than nonclinical ones, though few clinical studies existed | [Abramowitz-etal-2001] |
| People with psychopathology did not have more recurrence during suppression than people without; samples with OCD symptoms showed slightly less | [Magee-etal-2012] |
| In OCD, suppression mainly increased distress; focused distraction and acceptance worked better | [Najmi-etal-2009] |
| Giving people a specific distracter thought reduced later preoccupation | [Wegner-etal-1987] |

So the honest message is not "suppression always makes thoughts multiply." It is "suppression is effortful, unreliable and tends to raise distress, and better options exist."

## In the session

- If the client says "I try not to think about it" → ask what happens to the thought and their distress when they try, and what it means to them when the thought comes back. Because suppression tends to bring about the state it aims to prevent [Wenzlaff-Wegner-2000].
- If the client believes they should be able to control thoughts → run a white bear experiment in session: two minutes trying not to think of a chosen neutral image, counting intrusions, then two minutes allowing it. Discuss the result without promising a particular outcome. Because rebound is real but varies in size [Abramowitz-etal-2001].
- If intrusions are distressing (OCD, trauma memories) → teach allowing the thought to come and go while continuing the task, and use focused, engaging activity rather than pushing away. Because acceptance and focused distraction outperformed suppression for intrusive thoughts in OCD [Najmi-etal-2009].
- If someone has taught the client thought stopping → explain kindly why you are suggesting a different approach. Because thought stopping is a form of suppression, which tends to raise distress [Najmi-etal-2009].
- If suppression targets thoughts of self-harm or suicide → do not simply advise allowing them; assess risk the same session (`02-risk.suicide-risk-assessment`). Because suicidal thoughts need direct risk assessment and a safety plan (`02-risk.safety-planning`), not only a coping strategy. In a crisis in the US, call or text 988 or call 911; in the UK, call 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123.

Example phrasing: "What if the goal is not to stop the thought, but to let it be there while you get on with what matters? Shall we try a small experiment to see what happens?"
Avoid: "Just don't think about it."

What to record: the suppressed thought, methods used, estimated daily frequency and distress (0 to 100) before and after switching strategy, and the client's beliefs about controlling thoughts.
