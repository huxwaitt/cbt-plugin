**Bottom line:** Avoidance is any action, overt or mental, that keeps a person away from something they expect to be threatening or painful. It brings quick relief, so it is reinforced, and it stops the person learning that the feared outcome is unlikely or bearable [Krypotos-etal-2015; Craske-etal-2014]. Judge each avoidance by its function, not its form: the same act can be avoidance for one client and healthy coping for another [Hofmann-Hay-2018].

## Components

| Form | What it looks like | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Behavioral avoidance | Not entering, leaving early, putting off | Skipping meetings, not opening mail, staying in bed |
| Cognitive avoidance | Steering the mind away | Distraction, pushing thoughts away, worry in words instead of images |
| Experiential avoidance | Unwillingness to stay in contact with unwanted thoughts, feelings, sensations or memories, plus efforts to change or escape them [Hayes-etal-1996] | Drinking to numb, not talking about a loss, refusing to feel anxious |
| Social and nonsocial avoidance in depression | Withdrawing from people or from tasks | Not returning calls, abandoning hobbies |

In building an avoidance scale for depression research, Ottenbreit and Dobson found four reliable factors in a student sample (behavioral or cognitive, crossed with social or nonsocial), and the subscales related to depression and anxiety scores [Ottenbreit-Dobson-2004]. Worry can itself work as cognitive avoidance: worry is mainly verbal thought rather than imagery, and Borkovec and Inz proposed that in generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) it works as avoidance of emotional imagery and its body sensations [Borkovec-Inz-1990].

## How it keeps the problem going

1. **Relief rewards the avoidance.** Escaping a feared cue lowers distress right away, which makes avoidance more likely next time [Krypotos-etal-2015].
2. **No new learning.** The person never finds out that the feared outcome does not happen or can be handled. Exposure works best when it violates the feared expectancy, which avoidance prevents [Craske-etal-2014].
3. **Loss of reward.** In depression, withdrawal removes contact with pleasure, achievement and people, which lowers mood further. This is the rationale for behavioral activation (`behavioral.behavioral-activation`), and avoidance scores track depression severity [Ottenbreit-Dobson-2004].
4. **The feared feeling gets bigger.** Trying not to have a feeling or thought keeps attention on it. Across disorders, avoidance and suppression show medium to large associations with symptoms [Aldao-etal-2010].

Not every avoidance is a problem. Hofmann and Hay argue that some avoidance, used flexibly, can be effective coping that increases a person's sense of control over a threat; what matters is its function [Hofmann-Hay-2018].

## Drawing it with the client

Ask four questions about one recent example: "What did you stay away from?" "What did that do for you right then?" "What did it cost you later?" "What did you not get to find out?"

- If the client says avoiding "works" → agree that it works in the short term, then ask what it has cost over months. Because the relief is real and is what keeps the loop running [Krypotos-etal-2015].
- If you cannot tell whether an act is avoidance → ask what the client fears would happen if they could not do it, and whether they could choose not to. Because function, not form, decides whether it maintains the problem [Hofmann-Hay-2018].
- If avoidance is of feelings, memories or thoughts rather than places → map it as experiential avoidance and ask what the person does instead of feeling. Because experiential avoidance is the unwillingness to stay in contact with private experiences [Hayes-etal-1996].
- If the client avoids many things → list them from easiest to hardest and pick one the client values. Because exposure and activation need a concrete, chosen starting point [Craske-etal-2014].
- If the client feels blamed → normalize first. "Anyone would steer away from something that feels this bad. The question is what it is costing you now." Because avoidance is a learned response that relief keeps reinforcing, not a failing of character [Krypotos-etal-2015].
- Avoid: "You just need to stop avoiding."

What to record: the avoided target, the feared outcome, the short-term payoff, the long-term cost, and a 0 to 100 rating of how much the client avoids it.

## Treatment targets it implies

| Avoidance type | Main approach | See |
|---|---|---|
| Feared situations and cues | Exposure designed for expectancy violation, with safety signals removed and practice varied [Craske-etal-2014] | `behavioral.exposure-principles` |
| Withdrawal in depression | Behavioral activation | `behavioral.behavioral-activation` |
| Feelings, memories, thoughts | Acceptance and willingness work, emotional exposure | `15-transdiagnostic-processes.emotion-regulation` |
| Worry as cognitive avoidance | Shift from verbal worry to concrete processing | `15-transdiagnostic-processes.worry` |

Measurement: the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-II (AAQ-II) is widely used for experiential avoidance and showed good reliability [Bond-etal-2011]. Its items load more strongly on general distress than on acceptance, so do not read a high score as proof of avoidance [Wolgast-2014]. Pair it with idiographic ratings of the client's own avoided targets.
