**Bottom line:** Therapists are poor judges of who is failing to improve, so treatment continues by default long after it should have been reviewed. Clinicians rarely predict deterioration, while simple session-by-session measures flagged 85% of deteriorating clients by the third session [Hannan-etal-2005]. Fix it by setting review dates at planning, graphing scores every session, and acting on a not-on-track signal within the next session.

## What it looks like

- The client is at session 18 of a planned 12, and nobody can say when the plan changed.
- Scores on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) or the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) are collected but not looked at, or not collected at all.
- Session notes say "good session" and "client engaged" while scores stay flat.
- The therapist feels the work is "deepening" and expects a breakthrough soon.
- The agenda has drifted into weekly catch-up conversation; core tasks such as exposure have stopped.
- Supervision discusses the client's story, not the outcome data.

## Why it happens

- Self-assessment bias: in one survey, a quarter of clinicians rated their skill at the 90th percentile compared with peers, none rated themselves below average, and clinicians overestimated improvement and underestimated deterioration in their own clients [Walfish-etal-2012].
- Clinical judgment alone misses most clients who are getting worse [Hannan-etal-2005].
- Therapist drift: a shift from "doing" therapy to "talking" therapy, often driven by the therapist's own anxiety about pushing behavioral change [Waller-2009].
- The relationship feels good, and ending or changing course feels like rejection.
- Services without review rules let sessions continue by default.

## The fix

- If you are setting up a plan → put review dates on the calendar now (for example at session 6 and at the planned end). Because the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) expects a review if there is no response after 4 to 6 weeks of psychological therapy [NICE-NG222].
- If you are collecting measures → graph them and look at them with the client every session. Because NICE recommends considering routine session-by-session outcome monitoring [NICE-NG222], and progress feedback gives a small benefit (d = 0.15) and less dropout [DeJong-etal-2021].
- If a client is flagged as not on track → raise it in the next session and in supervision. Because in a trial in English stepped-care services, alerts plus supervision review reduced end-of-treatment symptoms for not-on-track patients [Delgadillo-etal-2018].
- If no response persists after you fix adherence and life factors → review the diagnosis and change the plan. Because NICE sets this order: address causes, then review the diagnosis, then change treatment [NICE-NG222].
- If you notice sessions have become chat → reinstate the agenda and the core task. "I've noticed we've moved away from the exposure practice. Can we look at what's getting in the way?"
- If you catch yourself explaining flat scores by the client's motivation → treat lack of progress as information about the treatment, not the client. Because stepped care is meant to self-correct when outcomes are poor, and sees dropout as a prompt to review care [Bower-Gilbody-2005].

## Sources note

The prediction and self-assessment findings each come from a single study, so read them as illustrations of the bias rather than precise rates [Hannan-etal-2005; Walfish-etal-2012]. Feedback effects are small on average and vary by system and setting [DeJong-etal-2021]. The trial evidence for alerts plus supervision is from publicly funded stepped-care psychological services in England [Delgadillo-etal-2018].
