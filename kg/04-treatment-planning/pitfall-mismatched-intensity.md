**Bottom line:** Intensity mismatch happens in both directions: complex or severe problems routed to brief low-intensity help and left there, or mild problems given long, open-ended therapy. Guidelines already name presentations that need high intensity from the start, and matching complex cases directly to high intensity improved depression outcomes in a randomized trial [Delgadillo-etal-2022]. Decide the level from severity, impairment, risk, complexity and preference, and revisit it at every review.

## What it looks like

**Under-treatment**
- A client with severe, long-standing posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and dissociation is given an online program.
- A client with moderate to severe obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) completes a second round of guided self-help with no change.
- Social anxiety is treated with a group course by default.
- Borderline personality disorder is offered 6 sessions of generic cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

**Over-treatment**
- A client with mild, recent low mood is placed straight into 20 sessions of weekly therapy and a long wait, though a brief guided program would likely suffice.
- High-intensity therapy continues long after goals are met.

## Why it happens

- Service rules that send everyone to step 2 first regardless of presentation.
- Long high-intensity waits make low intensity the only quick option.
- Stepping up usually means a new therapist and another wait, so it is put off [Thew-etal-2025].
- The clinician does not check the guideline for that condition.
- Over-treatment comes from habit, a wish to be thorough, or the absence of an ending plan.

## The fix

- If the presentation is one a guideline treats at high intensity first → start there. Examples: individual CBT designed for social anxiety rather than group by default [NICE-CG159]; more intensive CBT with exposure and response prevention, or medication, for OCD with moderate impairment [NICE-CG31]; computerized trauma-focused CBT for PTSD only when symptoms, especially dissociation, are not severe [NICE-NG116]; no brief interventions under 3 months for borderline personality disorder outside a structured service [NICE-CG78]. Because these guidelines judged lower-intensity formats less effective or less safe for these presentations.
- If depression is more severe → weigh the advantages of options with more therapist contact before offering guided self-help. Because the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) flags guided self-help as a lower-contact option for more severe depression [NICE-NG222].
- If depression is less severe → consider the least intrusive, least resource-intensive option first, usually guided self-help. Because NICE ranks it first for less severe depression [NICE-NG222].
- If a client in low intensity has not improved after an adequate trial → step up rather than repeat. Because stepped care relies on monitoring and moving people up when they do not improve [Bower-Gilbody-2005].
- If your service has a validated way to predict complexity at assessment (the trial used a machine-learning tool built on intake data) → route predicted complex cases straight to high intensity. Because this raised reliable and clinically significant depression improvement from 45.1% to 52.3% in a cluster randomized trial [Delgadillo-etal-2022].
- If goals are met in high intensity → plan the ending rather than adding sessions.
- Explain the choice: "Based on how long this has gone on and how much it affects your work, I'd recommend starting with one-to-one therapy rather than the shorter program."

## Sources note

NICE guidance comes from a stepped-care system; US services rarely have formal steps, but the same matching logic applies. The stratified care trial cost about 104.5 pounds more per patient, mainly because more people received high intensity, and the benefit was clear only for depression, not anxiety [Delgadillo-etal-2022].
