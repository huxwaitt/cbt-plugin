**Bottom line:** Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has few absolute contraindications, but it is the wrong next step when something else must come first (acute risk, medical illness, mania, intoxication), when another treatment is the guideline first choice (family therapy for adolescent anorexia, structured programs for borderline personality disorder, environmental changes and medication for adult attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or ADHD), or when you lack the competence. Comorbid personality disorder, substance use or a risk score alone are not reasons to refuse treatment. Decide among four options: proceed, adapt, defer while other care comes first, or refer.

## When to use

- At the end of assessment, before agreeing a plan.
- When a new problem appears mid-treatment (for example mania, relapse into heavy drinking, a new medical diagnosis).

## Steps

1. **Does something need to come first?**
   - Imminent risk of harm: follow the crisis pathway before any planning (US: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, call or text 988, or 911; UK: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111, Samaritans 116 123; see `02-risk.crisis-response`).
   - Possible medical cause, or medical instability (for example in an eating disorder): get medical assessment.
   - Signs of mania or hypomania in bipolar disorder: liaise with or refer to specialist care, urgently for mania or severe depression [NICE-CG185].
   - Harmful drinking with depression or anxiety: treat the alcohol use first and reassess after 3 to 4 weeks of abstinence [NICE-CG115].
2. **Is another treatment the guideline first choice?** Check the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and US guidance.
   - Children and young people with anorexia: family therapy for anorexia first; CBT for eating disorders only if that is unacceptable, contraindicated or ineffective [NICE-NG69].
   - Borderline personality disorder: no brief interventions under 3 months outside a structured service; consider comprehensive dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) for women when reducing recurrent self-harm is the priority [NICE-CG78].
   - Young people who self-harm often and have marked emotional dysregulation: consider DBT adapted for adolescents [NICE-NG225].
   - Adult ADHD: environmental changes, then medication if impairment persists; a structured psychological intervention, which may include CBT, if medication is declined, not tolerated or ineffective, or alongside it for residual impairment [NICE-NG87].
3. **Is there an equally recommended alternative the client prefers?**
   - Depression: interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT), behavioral activation, counseling, short-term psychodynamic therapy, problem solving and others are first-line options [NICE-NG222]; the American Psychological Association (APA) found comparable effects across several therapy models [APA-Depression-Guideline-2019].
   - Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD): eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) is offered for non-combat trauma after 3 months [NICE-NG116].
   - Chronic primary pain: acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) or CBT for pain [NICE-NG193].
4. **Do you have the competence?** Specialist protocols need specific training and supervision. If not, refer or arrange supervision.
5. **Decide and record:** proceed, adapt, defer, or refer, with the reason and the client's view.

## What to record

- The specific reason CBT is deferred, adapted or not offered, and the evidence or guideline behind it.
- Alternatives discussed and the client's choice.
- Referral made, to whom, and interim support while waiting.
- Risk status and crisis contacts given.

## Guidance

- If the client has depression and a personality disorder diagnosis → offer depression treatment, considering combined medication and therapy, and extend duration up to a year if needed. Because NICE says not to withhold depression treatment for this reason [NICE-NG222].
- If the client has PTSD and uses alcohol or drugs → do not exclude them on that basis alone; consider trauma-focused CBT alongside substance use care. Because NICE advises against exclusion and APA strongly recommends this combination [NICE-NG116; APA-PTSD-Guideline-2025].
- If someone proposes using a risk scale to decide whether to offer therapy → decline. Because NICE says not to use risk tools to decide who is offered treatment [NICE-NG225].
- If the client lives with ongoing danger (for example current domestic violence) → prioritize safety and stability, follow local safeguarding procedures (see `02-risk.safeguarding`), and adapt the timing of trauma-focused work. Because NICE asks clinicians to weigh the safety and stability of the person's circumstances [NICE-NG116].
- If PTSD and severe depression co-occur and the depression blocks trauma work or raises risk → treat the depression first. Because NICE makes this the exception to treating PTSD first [NICE-NG116].
- If you are referring elsewhere → present it as matching, not rejection: "From what you've told me, a program called DBT fits this pattern better than standard CBT. Here's how we'd get you there, and here's what we'll do until then." Avoid: "CBT won't work for someone like you." Because NICE asks that people be kept in touch with and told how to get help while they wait [NICE-NG222].

## Common problems

- **Excluding instead of adapting.** Many presentations (psychosis, intellectual disability, older age) call for adapted CBT, not none.
- **Referring without a bridge.** Waiting lists are long. Agree interim contact and crisis steps.
- **Offering CBT outside competence** because nothing else is available. Seek supervision and say so in the record.
