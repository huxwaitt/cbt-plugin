**Bottom line:** Goals that cannot be observed, are not timed, or belong to the therapist make progress invisible and weaken collaboration. Agreement on goals and active collaboration each have a medium-sized link with outcome across dozens of studies [Tryon-etal-2018]. The fix is to co-write a few specific, measurable, timed goals in the client's own terms and check them at every review.

## What it looks like

- The plan says "improve mood," "manage anxiety" or "build self-esteem," with no baseline or target.
- Goals are phrased as the absence of feelings ("not feel anxious"), which no one can achieve.
- The therapist wrote the goals after the session; the client cannot recall them.
- The client agrees to everything but homework is rarely done, or sessions drift onto topics the client cares about more.
- At review, neither person can say whether therapy is working.
- Goals depend on someone else changing ("my boss to respect me").

## Why it happens

- Diagnosis-led planning: the therapist copies the symptom target from the protocol instead of asking what the client wants their life to look like.
- Time pressure: writing precise goals takes effort, so a placeholder stays forever.
- Deference: clients often accept the expert's agenda without saying it is not theirs.
- Avoidance: vague goals protect both people from facing a lack of progress.
- Uncertainty about how to measure personal goals, so none are measured.

## The fix

- If a goal names a feeling or a symptom → translate it into what the client would do: "If anxiety had less hold on you, what would you be doing next month that you can't do now?" Because a goal built from a target activity, a quantity and a time period can be measured and reviewed [Bovendeerdt-etal-2009].
- If you drafted the goals → read them back and invite edits: "These are my words. What would you change so they're yours?" Because client and therapist agreement on goals predicts better outcome (r = .24 across 54 studies) [Tryon-etal-2018].
- If the client cannot name goals → start from values or a typical day: "What would a good Tuesday look like?" Then pick one concrete step.
- If a goal relies on other people → reframe to the client's own action: "What would you like to do when he criticizes you?"
- If there are many goals → keep 2 to 4 active and park the rest with a review date.
- Attach a measure to every goal (a standardized scale for the main problem plus a frequency count or a 0 to 100 rating) and record the baseline.
- Revisit goals at each formal review, and record changes and the reason. The case formulation approach sets goals with the client and seeks their consent to the treatment plan [Persons-2008].
- Ask for importance: "From 0 to 10, how much does this goal matter to you?" Rework anything rated low (a practical rule of thumb, not a validated cutoff).

Example rewrite. Before: "Reduce social anxiety." After: "By week 12, speak at least once in each weekly team meeting, rating anxiety at 50 or below on most occasions."

## Sources note

The goal consensus and collaboration figures come from meta-analyses of correlational studies, so they show association, not proof that better goal-setting causes better outcome [Tryon-etal-2018]. The four-part goal method was developed in rehabilitation and is applied here by analogy [Bovendeerdt-etal-2009]. The UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) depression guideline asks clinicians to discuss what people hope to gain from treatment and describes collaborative care as jointly identifying problems and agreeing goals [NICE-NG222].
