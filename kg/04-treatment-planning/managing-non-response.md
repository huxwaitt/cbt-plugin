**Bottom line:** Measure every session, because clinicians rarely predict who will fail without data, while simple tracking flagged most deteriorating clients by session 3 [Hannan-etal-2005]. If there is no response after 4 to 6 weeks, check fixable causes first (attendance, homework, life stressors, other conditions), then review the diagnosis and formulation, then change the plan, for example by switching therapy or adding medication [NICE-NG222], intensifying, or referring. Take not-on-track cases to supervision; feedback with supervision review improved their outcomes in a randomized trial [Delgadillo-etal-2018].

## When to use

- Scores are flat, worse, or swinging, or goals are not moving.
- A scheduled review arrives, usually at 4 to 6 weeks of psychological therapy [NICE-NG222].
- The client says therapy is not helping, or you notice you are dreading sessions.

## Steps

1. **Define non-response in advance.** Use the measure tied to the main goal. In NHS Talking Therapies, the National Health Service (NHS) program in England, reliable improvement means at least a 6-point drop on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) or a 4-point drop on the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [Thew-etal-2025]. Less than that by the review is non-response.
2. **Track and graph every session** and look at the trend with the client. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks clinicians to consider routine session-by-session outcome monitoring [NICE-NG222].
3. **Ask the client directly.** "Your scores have stayed about the same for four weeks. What's your sense of how this is going?"
4. **Check fixable causes** [NICE-NG222]:
   - Attendance and homework completion.
   - Personal, social or environmental stressors (housing, debt, relationship conflict, caregiving).
   - Physical illness or other mental health conditions.
   - Medication problems (missed doses, side effects) if combined.
   - Alliance: disagreement on goals or tasks, or an unrepaired rupture.
5. **Check fidelity.** Are you still doing the core tasks (exposure, behavioral activation, experiments), or has therapy drifted into talking? Therapist drift away from behavioral change is common [Waller-2009].
6. **Review the diagnosis and formulation.** Consider missed or comorbid conditions [NICE-NG222]. Revisit the maintenance cycle: is the target the process that actually keeps the problem going [Persons-2008]?
7. **Take it to supervision** with the graph and your hypotheses.
8. **Change the plan with the client.** For depression after psychological therapy alone, NICE options are switching to another psychological therapy, adding a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), or switching to an SSRI alone [NICE-NG222]. Other levers: increase session frequency, step up intensity, add family or couple sessions.
9. **Set a new review date.** If two well-delivered changes fail, discuss referral for a specialist opinion (expert consensus; for chronic depression NICE offers specialist referral after further-line options fail [NICE-NG222]).

## What to record

- The measure trend and the date non-response was identified.
- Causes checked and findings.
- Revised formulation, if any.
- Supervision discussion and advice.
- The changed plan, the client's agreement and the next review date.

## Guidance

- If scores are worsening or risk is rising → review risk the same session and update the safety plan before anything else; if there is intent, a plan or imminent danger, follow the crisis pathway (US: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, call or text 988, or 911; UK: 999, NHS 111, Samaritans 116 123; see `02-risk.crisis-response`). Because NICE asks for monitoring of suicidal ideation and harms throughout treatment [NICE-NG222].
- If you feel the client is improving but the scores disagree → trust the scores enough to discuss them. Because clinicians overestimate their improvement rates and underestimate deterioration [Walfish-etal-2012].
- If homework is rarely done → explore barriers before changing the treatment (see `05-session-structure.homework-review-and-adherence`). Because NICE lists adherence problems among the first things to check when treatment is not working [NICE-NG222], and nonadherence must be handled before a treatment is judged to have failed [Persons-2008].
- If a client on an antidepressant has not responded → discuss adding cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) with the prescriber. Because CBT added to medication doubled response in treatment-resistant depression [Wiles-etal-2013], and the American Psychological Association (APA) lists adding psychotherapy as an option [APA-Depression-Guideline-2019].
- If the client feels hopeless that "nothing works" → say that other treatments can still be tried and may work: "This approach hasn't shifted things yet. That tells us something useful, and there are other options we can try." Check for suicidal thinking at the same time. Because NICE asks clinicians to give exactly this reassurance after non-response [NICE-NG222], and hopelessness is a risk signal.
- If you are tempted to put non-response down to low motivation → treat it as a signal to review the care instead. Because stepped-care principles treat poor progress and dropout as prompts to review treatment, not as proof of poor motivation [Bower-Gilbody-2005]. Avoid: "You're not trying hard enough."

## Common problems

- **Waiting too long.** Reviews slip and 20 sessions pass without change. Set the review at planning.
- **Changing too much at once,** so you cannot tell what helped. Change one main thing per review.
- **Ignoring feedback data.** Progress feedback has a small but reliable benefit (d = 0.15 overall; 0.17 for not-on-track cases) and reduces dropout [DeJong-etal-2021], but only if you act on it.
- **Never ending.** If repeated changes fail, refer or end with a plan rather than continuing indefinitely [Persons-2008].
