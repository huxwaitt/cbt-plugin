**Bottom line:** A problem list names every difficulty you and the client can see, in plain words, across symptoms and life areas. Goals turn the problems the client most wants to change into specific, measurable targets with a time frame and a measure attached. Order the work by safety first, then by what drives the other problems, then by what matters most to the client. Client and therapist agreement on goals is associated with better outcome [Tryon-etal-2018].

## When to use

- After assessment (and diagnosis, where your license allows), before the first active intervention. In the case formulation approach the problem list and diagnosis come first, then the initial formulation and goals, then a treatment plan the client consents to [Persons-2008].
- At every formal review, because problems resolve, new ones appear and priorities shift (see `04-treatment-planning.managing-non-response`).

## Steps

1. **Start with the client's words.** Ask what they hope to gain and what they think is contributing to their difficulties. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks for both before choosing depression treatment [NICE-NG222]. "If our work goes well, what will be different in your week?"
2. **Add what you observed** in life areas the client may not have raised: relationships, work or study, physical health, money, housing, legal issues, leisure. NICE asks for an assessment of need covering physical health, coexisting mental health problems and barriers to treatment [NICE-NG222].
3. **Write each item as a short problem statement:** what happens, when, and the impact. Example: "Avoids buses and supermarkets because of panic attacks; has missed 6 work days this month."
4. **Screen for problems that must come first** (risk, medical causes, substance use); see Guidance.
5. **Pick 2 to 4 priorities** for the first phase. Park the rest on the list with a review date.
6. **Write each priority as a SMART goal** (specific, measurable, achievable, realistic or relevant, timed), built from up to four parts: target activity, support needed, quantity, and time period [Bovendeerdt-etal-2009]. Example: "Within 8 weeks, travel to work by bus alone on 4 of 5 days."
7. **Attach a measure to each goal:** a standardized symptom measure for the main problem and, where useful, an idiographic rating such as frequency per week or a 0 to 100 rating.
8. **Agree the plan and record consent.** Read the list and goals back, invite changes, and ask the client to rate how much each goal matters (0 to 10).

## What to record

- The numbered problem list in the client's words, with the priority order and the reason for it.
- Each goal with its baseline value, target value, measure and review date.
- Baseline scores on standardized measures.
- The agreed approach, expected number of sessions and first review point.
- Any disagreement about priorities and how you resolved it.
- Risk items and where the safety plan is recorded.

## Guidance

- If the client reports current suicidal thoughts, self-harm or risk to others → list safety as problem 1, complete a safety plan, and keep checking risk at each session; if there is intent, a plan or imminent danger, follow the crisis pathway first (US: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, call or text 988, or 911; UK: 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111, Samaritans 116 123; see `02-risk.crisis-response`). Because NICE asks clinicians to monitor suicidal ideation during treatment, especially in the early weeks [NICE-NG222].
- If posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression co-occur → usually target PTSD first, but treat depression first if it is severe enough to block trauma work or there is a risk of harm. Because depression often improves once PTSD is treated successfully [NICE-NG116].
- If harmful drinking co-occurs with depression or anxiety → address the alcohol use first, then reassess mood and anxiety after 3 to 4 weeks of abstinence. Because the alcohol misuse may be driving much of the depression and anxiety [NICE-CG115].
- If one problem plausibly keeps several others going (for example, avoidance driving low mood and lost work) → propose it as the first target and explain the link from the formulation. Because the case formulation approach derives goals and the plan from hypothesized mechanisms that link the problems (expert consensus) [Persons-2008].
- If the client's priority differs from yours and there is no safety issue → start with theirs, or agree an order out loud. Because goal consensus correlates with outcome (r = .24 across 54 studies), although the studies cannot show cause [Tryon-etal-2018].
- If the goal is a feeling ("stop being anxious") → ask what they would be doing if it were achieved: "What would you be doing that anxiety stops you doing now?" Because behavioral targets can be counted and reviewed [Bovendeerdt-etal-2009].
- If the goal depends on someone else changing → reframe it around the client's own actions: "What would you like to do differently when your partner criticizes you?" Because a goal needs a target activity the client can perform and count [Bovendeerdt-etal-2009].
- If there are more than 4 pressing problems → choose those that are most distressing, most impairing or most likely to move others. Say: "Let's start with these two and come back to the list at session 6." Because unaddressed personal, social and health factors are a listed reason treatment stalls [NICE-NG222].

## Common problems

- **Vague or therapist-owned goals.** See `04-treatment-planning.pitfall-vague-or-imposed-goals`.
- **Practical problems ignored.** Debt, housing or caregiving overload can block therapy. Put them on the list and plan signposting or problem solving.
- **The list is never revisited.** Review it at set points and record changes [Beck-2021].
