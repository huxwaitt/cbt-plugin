**Bottom line:** Set a provisional number of sessions from the guideline dose for the condition and severity, tell the client, and review it against measures rather than running on by default. Meet at least weekly; in a randomized trial, twice-weekly sessions of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or interpersonal psychotherapy for depression beat weekly sessions [Bruijniks-etal-2020]. Plan three phases (engage and formulate, active change, consolidate and prevent relapse) and space the last sessions out.

## When to use

- When you write the treatment plan, after goals are agreed.
- At each scheduled review, to confirm, shorten or extend the plan.

## Steps

1. **Pick the provisional dose from the guideline** for the condition and severity. National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) examples for adults:

| Presentation | Usual dose |
|---|---|
| Less severe depression, individual CBT | 8 sessions [NICE-NG222] |
| More severe depression, individual CBT | 16 sessions [NICE-NG222] |
| Generalized anxiety disorder | 12 to 15 weekly 1-hour sessions [NICE-CG113] |
| Panic disorder | 7 to 14 hours, weekly 1 to 2 hour sessions, within 4 months [NICE-CG113] |
| Social anxiety disorder | Up to 14 sessions of 90 minutes over about 4 months (Clark and Wells), or 15 of 60 minutes plus one of 90 (Heimberg) [NICE-CG159] |
| Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), trauma-focused CBT | 8 to 12 sessions, more for multiple traumas [NICE-NG116] |
| Bulimia nervosa, CBT for eating disorders | Up to 20 sessions over 20 weeks [NICE-NG69] |
| Anorexia nervosa, CBT for eating disorders | Up to 40 sessions over 40 weeks [NICE-NG69] |
| Psychosis, CBT | At least 16 planned sessions [NICE-CG178] |

NICE adds that more sessions may be needed for coexisting mental or physical health problems, complex social needs or residual symptoms [NICE-NG222], and asks clinicians to let the treatment manual guide form, duration and ending [NICE-NG222].

2. **Set frequency.** Weekly is the default. Consider twice weekly at the start: NICE advises it for the first 2 to 3 weeks of CBT for anorexia and suggests considering it in the first phase for bulimia [NICE-NG69]; it may also be considered in borderline personality disorder [NICE-CG78].
3. **Set session length:** usually about 1 hour; 90 minutes where the protocol specifies it, as in the Clark and Wells protocol for social anxiety [NICE-CG159].
4. **Map the phases.** Early: assessment, socialization to CBT, formulation and goals. Middle: the core interventions for the main target. Late: consolidation, a relapse prevention plan and spacing sessions out [Beck-2021]. For bulimia NICE describes spreading appointments further apart toward the end [NICE-NG69].
5. **Schedule reviews.** NICE asks for a review of how treatment is working 2 to 4 weeks after it starts, and a fuller discussion if there is no response after 4 to 6 weeks of psychological therapy [NICE-NG222]. Put both on the calendar, and another near the planned end.
6. **Plan the ending and boosters.** For depression at higher relapse risk, if the client wants to continue therapy, NICE advises at least 4 more sessions of the same therapy focused on relapse prevention [NICE-NG222]. For PTSD, plan boosters around significant dates such as anniversaries [NICE-NG116].

## What to record

- Provisional number of sessions, frequency and length, and the guideline or protocol they come from.
- Dates of planned reviews and the measure used at each.
- Any reason for departing from the usual dose.
- Changes to the plan and why.

## Guidance

- If the client has moderate to severe depression and can attend → consider twice-weekly sessions. Because in a 200-person trial, twice-weekly CBT or interpersonal psychotherapy (up to 20 sessions) cut depression scores more by month 6 (d = 0.55) and halved dropout (16 versus 32) [Bruijniks-etal-2020].
- If you must choose between more sessions and more frequent sessions → favor frequency. Because in a meta-regression of 70 depression studies, total sessions had little link to effect, but two sessions a week instead of one was associated with g = 0.45 more; this is correlational and needs replication [Cuijpers-etal-2013a].
- If the client has improved well before the planned number → discuss ending early. Because NICE expects fewer sessions if the person recovers sooner [NICE-CG113], and dose-response curves flatten [Robinson-etal-2020a].
- If the client has complex needs, multiple traumas or an unstable living situation → plan extra sessions and time to build trust. Because NICE recommends extending duration for complex PTSD presentations [NICE-NG116].
- If the client asks "How long will this take?" → give the range and the review point: "For this kind of problem, people usually need about 12 to 16 sessions. We'll check your scores every week and review together at session 6." Because NICE expects people to be told the likely length and expected outcomes of a treatment when they choose it [NICE-NG222].

## Common problems

- **Open-ended drift.** Sessions continue with no review date. Fix: put the review on the calendar when you set the plan.
- **Every 2 weeks from the start** because of capacity. Weekly therapy speeds improvement compared with less frequent schedules [Robinson-etal-2020a]. If every 2 weeks is unavoidable, say so in the plan.
- **One dose for all.** Routine-care studies put the useful range at about 4 to 26 sessions (4 to 6 for low-intensity guided self-help), varying by setting and population [Robinson-etal-2020a]. Base yours on the condition, severity and progress.
- **Abrupt endings.** Taper the last sessions and plan boosters (see `09-ending.booster-sessions`).
