**Bottom line:** Stepped care starts with the least intensive treatment likely to help, monitors outcome, and steps up when the person is not improving [Bower-Gilbody-2005]. In NHS Talking Therapies, the National Health Service (NHS) program in England (formerly IAPT, Improving Access to Psychological Therapies), low-intensity work is about 5 to 8 brief sessions of guided self-help and high-intensity therapy runs up to 12 to 20 sessions [Thew-etal-2025]. Stepped care has a moderate advantage over usual care but is not proven to be the best model, so start some clients at high intensity, and step up promptly when measures stall [VanStraten-etal-2015; Delgadillo-etal-2022].

## What it is

Stepped care is a way of organizing services. It rests on two principles [Bower-Gilbody-2005]:
- **Least restrictive first:** begin with the treatment needing the least specialist time that is still likely to help.
- **Self-correcting:** monitor outcomes and step people up when they do not improve. Dropout is a reason to review care, not proof of poor motivation.

It assumes brief treatments work as well as longer ones for some people, free up resources, and are acceptable to clients and clinicians [Bower-Gilbody-2005].

## Why it matters in CBT

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) ranges from a self-help workbook with short phone support to 20 specialist sessions. Matching dose to person drives waiting times, cost and outcome. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) asks clinicians to use the least intrusive, most resource-efficient treatment that fits the person's clinical needs, or one that has worked for them before [NICE-NG222].

## Key details

**The NICE model for generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)** [NICE-CG113]:

| Step | Who it is for | What is offered |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | All known or suspected GAD | Identification, education, active monitoring |
| 2 | Not improved after step 1 | Low intensity: self-help, guided self-help (5 to 7 sessions of 20 to 30 minutes), psychoeducational groups (6 weekly 2-hour sessions, about 12 per therapist) |
| 3 | Not improved after step 2, or marked impairment | High-intensity CBT or applied relaxation (12 to 15 weekly 1-hour sessions), or medication |
| 4 | Complex, treatment-refractory, very marked impairment or high self-harm risk | Specialist and multi-agency care, crisis or inpatient services |

**NHS Talking Therapies (England).** Step 2 is delivered mainly by psychological wellbeing practitioners; step 3 offers CBT and other high-intensity therapies [Thew-etal-2025]. "Caseness" means a Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score of 10 or more or a Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) score of 8 or more. Reliable improvement means a drop of at least 6 PHQ-9 points or 4 GAD-7 points. Reliable recovery means starting above caseness, improving reliably, and ending below caseness on both measures [Thew-etal-2025]. These definitions are as reported in a 2025 study; the service manual itself could not be opened at review.

**Starting higher.** Some guidance skips step 2:
- Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD): low intensity (up to 10 therapist hours, including exposure and response prevention) only for mild impairment or by preference; more intensive CBT or a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) for moderate impairment; both for severe impairment [NICE-CG31].
- More severe depression: weigh carefully the advantages of options with more therapist contact before offering guided self-help [NICE-NG222].
- Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD): supported computerized trauma-focused CBT only if symptoms, especially dissociation, are not severe [NICE-NG116].

**Evidence.** A meta-analysis of 10 randomized trials (4,580 patients) found a moderate advantage of stepped care over usual care for depression at 6 months (d = 0.34), but only limited evidence that it should be the dominant model [VanStraten-etal-2015]. In a cluster randomized trial with 951 patients, stratified care (a machine-learning tool at assessment matched predicted complex cases straight to high intensity) raised reliable and clinically significant improvement in depression from 45.1% to 52.3%. It cost about 104.5 pounds more per patient because more people received high intensity, and anxiety outcomes did not differ significantly [Delgadillo-etal-2022]. Stepping up usually means a new therapist and another wait; in a small non-randomized pilot, dual-trained therapists blending both intensities used about 4 fewer sessions and finished 121 days sooner [Thew-etal-2025].

## In the session

- If low-intensity care brings no reliable improvement after an adequate trial → discuss stepping up rather than repeating it. Because stepped care only works if it self-corrects [Bower-Gilbody-2005].
- If the presentation is complex (severe impairment, multiple problems, PTSD, moderate to severe OCD) → consider high intensity first. Because matching complex cases directly improved depression outcomes [Delgadillo-etal-2022].
- If a client feels "demoted" by low intensity → explain the logic: "Many people get what they need from a shorter, guided program. We'll check your scores every session, and if it isn't helping enough, we move to longer one-to-one therapy." Because stepped care assumes brief treatment is acceptable to clients, and acceptance cannot be taken for granted [Bower-Gilbody-2005].
- If stepping up → frame it as information, not failure: "The scores tell us this format isn't enough for you. That's useful, and the next step is more intensive therapy." Avoid: "You didn't really engage with step 2." Because stepped care treats poor progress and dropout as a prompt to review care, not as a lack of motivation [Bower-Gilbody-2005].
- If a client in any step reports suicidal intent or a plan → stop the stepping decision and follow the crisis pathway (US: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, call or text 988, or 911; UK: 999, NHS 111, Samaritans 116 123; see `02-risk.crisis-response`). Because NICE places high risk of self-harm at step 4, with specialist and crisis services [NICE-CG113].

The US has no national stepped-care pathway; the nearest common model is collaborative care (see `04-treatment-planning.combining-with-medication`).
