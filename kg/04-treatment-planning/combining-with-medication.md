**Bottom line:** For depression (mostly moderate to severe in the trials), psychotherapy such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) plus an antidepressant outperforms either alone at the end of treatment, and psychotherapy alone equals medication alone [Cuijpers-etal-2020a]. Combined treatment is also favored for severe obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and adding CBT helps people who respond poorly to medication [NICE-CG31; Wiles-etal-2013]. Non-prescribing therapists do not advise on starting, changing or stopping medication; they share measures with the prescriber.

## When to use

- The client has more severe depression, severe OCD, or a partial response to medication.
- The client is already on medication when CBT starts, or wants to stop it.
- The client is older, socially isolated, has significant physical health problems, or has chronic depression not responding to specialist care, where the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) suggests collaborative care [NICE-NG222].

## Steps

1. **Ask about medication at intake:** name, dose, prescriber, how long, adherence, side effects, plans. Get consent to contact the prescriber.
2. **Lay out the options with the guideline.** For more severe depression, NICE ranks CBT plus an antidepressant first in its table of options [NICE-NG222]. The American Psychological Association (APA) recommends CBT or interpersonal psychotherapy plus a second-generation antidepressant if combining [APA-Depression-Guideline-2019], and the American College of Physicians suggests CBT plus a second-generation antidepressant for moderate to severe depression (conditional) [Qaseem-etal-2023].
3. **Choose a sequence.**
   - Simultaneous: start both, or start medication and begin CBT as soon as possible [NICE-NG222].
   - Augmenting: add CBT after a poor response to medication. In the CoBalT trial (CBT as an adjunct to medication for treatment-resistant depression in primary care), adding CBT to usual care for treatment-resistant depression doubled response at 6 months (46% versus 22%) [Wiles-etal-2013].
   - Sequential: CBT after acute response to medication, to prevent relapse. Across 17 trials this cut relapse or recurrence risk (risk ratio 0.84) [Guidi-Fava-2021].
4. **Agree roles in writing:** who prescribes, who monitors which measure, how often you will communicate, and who handles risk.
5. **Share outcome data** (for example the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, PHQ-9) with the prescriber at agreed points, especially at the review NICE expects 2 to 4 weeks after any treatment starts, and at non-response [NICE-NG222].
6. **Review at non-response** using the NICE options: switch or add a psychological therapy, add or switch to a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), or change medication [NICE-NG222].

## What to record

- Current medication, dose, prescriber and consent to share information.
- The agreed sequence and the reason.
- Each communication with the prescriber and what was shared.
- Client reports of side effects, missed doses or wish to stop, and that you advised them to discuss this with the prescriber.

## Guidance

- If the client wants to stop an antidepressant → encourage them to talk to the prescriber first. Because NICE advises tapering, and stopping abruptly or missing doses can cause withdrawal symptoms [NICE-NG222]. "That's an important decision. Could you raise it with Dr. Lee before changing anything? I can share your progress scores if that helps."
- If the client starts an antidepressant during CBT → set expectations: effects are usually seen within 4 weeks, with a first review usually within 2 weeks (1 week if aged 18 to 25 or at particular risk of suicide) [NICE-NG222]. Monitor risk closely in the early weeks, and tell the prescriber the same day about new or rising suicidal thoughts, agitation or self-harm. Because NICE warns of more suicidal thoughts and self-harm early in antidepressant treatment for these groups [NICE-NG222].
- If the client has panic disorder or generalized anxiety and asks for a benzodiazepine → refer the question to the prescriber. Because NICE advises against benzodiazepines for panic disorder, and for generalized anxiety except briefly in a crisis [NICE-CG113].
- If the client with social anxiety has only partly responded to an SSRI after 10 to 12 weeks → offer individual CBT in addition. Because NICE recommends this combination at that point [NICE-CG159].
- If medication and CBT are both stopping → plan relapse prevention for both. Because in a panic disorder trial CBT gains held up after treatment ended, while imipramine gains, including those from combined treatment, faded after discontinuation [Barlow-etal-2000].
- If the client has depression and a personality disorder diagnosis → consider combined treatment rather than withholding either. Because NICE suggests medication plus a psychological treatment for this group [NICE-NG222].

## Common problems

- **Working in silos.** The prescriber and therapist never talk, so the client gets mixed messages. Collaborative care fixes this with a shared plan. NICE lists its parts: shared assessment, symptom measurement, a medication plan, a case manager, structured psychological treatment, integrated physical and mental care, joint working and supervision [NICE-NG222]. In US collaborative care, a behavioral health care manager, primary care provider and psychiatric consultant work as one team with the patient [AIMS-Center-Site]. A Cochrane review of 79 trials found better depression and anxiety outcomes than usual care (standardized mean difference about 0.34 for short-term depression outcomes) [Archer-etal-2012]; in the IMPACT (Improving Mood-Promoting Access to Collaborative Treatment) trial of older adults, 45% versus 19% halved their symptoms at 12 months [Unutzer-etal-2002].
- **Scope creep.** Therapists recommending doses or drugs. Prescribing authority depends on license and state or country; stay within yours.
- **Assuming combined is always better.** For less severe depression, NICE advises against routine first-line antidepressants [NICE-NG222], and psychotherapy alone was more acceptable than medication alone [Cuijpers-etal-2020a]. Adding psychotherapy to antidepressants had a moderate benefit overall (g = 0.43), shown for major depression, panic disorder and OCD [Cuijpers-etal-2014a].
