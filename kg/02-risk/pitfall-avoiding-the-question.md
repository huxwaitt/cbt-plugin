**Bottom line:** Not asking, or asking in a way that signals the wanted answer, is a common way risk is missed (expert consensus). Asking directly does not increase suicidal ideation [Dazzi-etal-2014], and the questions need no special training to ask [C-SSRS-Site]. Ask plainly, use the word "suicide", and follow hints rather than steering away from them.

## What it looks like

- No suicide question at intake because the client "doesn't seem depressed".
- "You're not having any thoughts of hurting yourself, are you?"
- "Any thoughts of self-harm?" used as the only question, which blurs suicide and self-injury.
- The client says "Sometimes I wonder what the point is" and the clinician moves on to homework.
- Risk is asked about only on a form, never in conversation.

## Why it happens

Clinicians fear that asking will plant the idea, upset the client, damage rapport, or open a situation they will have to manage. Some feel unskilled or worry about time. Negatively framed or vague questions feel gentler, but they make a "no" the easy answer. Hints such as hopelessness, feeling trapped or being a burden are recognized warning signs [Lifeline-988], and warning signs indicate acute risk over minutes to days [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024], so steering away is costly.

## The fix

- If you fear planting the idea → ask anyway. Because studies that asked participants about suicide found no significant increase in ideation [Dazzi-etal-2014].
- If the client hints ("what's the point", "everyone would be better off") → reflect it and ask directly whether they are thinking of suicide. Because hopelessness and burdensomeness are warning signs [Lifeline-988].
- If you ask → use a neutral, direct question with the word "suicide", then ask about a wish to be dead, active thoughts, intent, plan and behavior in order. Because the graded C-SSRS (Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale) order captures severity [Posner-etal-2011].
- If the client denies current thoughts → ask about the worst time ever. Because lifetime worst-point ideation carries predictive information [Posner-etal-2011].
- If you feel unskilled → rehearse the questions in supervision and use the C-SSRS screener as a script. Because its developers state that no mental health training is needed to ask the screener questions [C-SSRS-Site].
- If time is short → ask anyway, and schedule the full assessment rather than skipping the question (expert consensus).

Example phrasing: "When people feel as stuck as you've described, they sometimes think about suicide. Have you had thoughts like that?"

Avoid: "You wouldn't do anything stupid, would you?"

## Sources note

Evidence that asking is safe comes from a review of studies [Dazzi-etal-2014]. The question order follows the validated C-SSRS [Posner-etal-2011]. Advice on tone and wording is expert consensus.
