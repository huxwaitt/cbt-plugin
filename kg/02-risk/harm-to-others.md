**Bottom line:** Ask about violent thoughts as directly as you ask about suicide: who, how often, intent, plan, preparation, access to weapons or other means, history and triggers. Structured tools predict violence only low to moderately well (median positive predictive value 41% for violent offending), so use them to organize judgment, not to decide outcomes [Fazel-etal-2012]. If a client makes a serious threat against an identifiable person, most US states impose or permit a duty to warn or protect, so consult and act the same day [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022]. If harm is imminent, call 911 (US) or 999 (UK).

## When to use

- The client names a person they want to hurt, or voices revenge or rage.
- Violent ideation, intrusive harm images, or command hallucinations to harm.
- Past violence, recent escalation, new access to weapons, or a third party reports a threat.

Intrusive, unwanted harm thoughts in obsessive-compulsive disorder are ego-dystonic and feared; assess them, but do not treat them as threats (see `ocd.assessment`).

## Steps

1. **Ask directly.** "Have you had thoughts of hurting anyone? Who? What have you thought of doing?"
2. **Characterize the ideation**: target, frequency, intensity, intent, plan, preparation (acquiring means, surveillance of the target), and timeline.
3. **History**: past violence (a key historical factor), weapons access, substance use, and supervision or legal status.
4. **Current drivers**: intoxication, psychosis, mania, grievances, recent losses, relationship breakdown, and what is stopping the client.
5. **Characterize the violence**: impulsive and reactive versus planned and predatory, which call for different management [Wortzel-etal-2020].
6. **Stratify by severity and timing** (acute and chronic) and link each level to actions [Wortzel-etal-2020]. Specialists may add a structured professional judgment tool such as the Historical, Clinical, Risk Management-20 (HCR-20) Version 3, whose ratings showed areas under the curve of about 0.70 to 0.77 for violent recidivism in 100 Australian forensic psychiatric patients [Brookstein-etal-2021].
7. **Plan**: treat drivers, remove or secure weapons voluntarily, increase contact, and build a safety plan for other-directed violence (warning signs, coping steps, people to contact, means safety) [McGarity-etal-2021], and give crisis contacts the client can use when urges rise (US 988, call or text [Lifeline-988]; UK National Health Service (NHS) 111 mental health option).
8. **Decide on disclosure** if a serious threat exists (below), consult, and document.

## What to record

The client's words about the target and intent, the factors above, your formulation and risk level with reasons, the plan, any disclosure (to whom, what, when, legal basis), and consultation.

## Guidance

- If the client threatens serious physical violence against a reasonably identifiable person → consult a supervisor or legal adviser the same day and follow your state's duty-to-warn or protect rule. Because more than half of states make the duty mandatory, about a third make it permissive, a few have no duty or an unclear rule, and the trigger is usually such a threat [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022].
- If disclosure is needed → share the minimum necessary with someone able to prevent the harm, which can include the intended victim and police. Because the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) permits good-faith disclosure to prevent a serious and imminent threat [HIPAA-45-CFR-164-512], and the American Psychological Association (APA) Ethics Code permits disclosure as allowed by law to protect others from harm [APA-Ethics-2017].
- If the duty can be met clinically → consider voluntary hospitalization or an intensified treatment plan, which some states accept as discharging the duty. Because statutes list several routes, not only warning [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022].
- If the client leaves angry with a threat pending → notify police or warn the target as your state law allows, and document why. Because duty-to-protect routes are set by state law [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022], and a recorded rationale shows why you acted (expert consensus).
- If the anger is reactive and the client is engaged → map the triggers and functions of the violent urges, then build an other-directed violence safety plan with warning signs, coping steps and people to contact. Because a safety plan grounded in the function of the violence helps clients disrupt the pattern [McGarity-etal-2021].
- If a structured tool rates the client "low risk" → keep using clinical judgment. Because tools identify low-risk people fairly well (median negative predictive value 91%) but are not supported as sole determinants of decisions [Fazel-etal-2012].

Example phrasing: "When you say he'll get what's coming, I need to understand that. Are you thinking of hurting him?"

## Common problems

- Not asking because it feels accusatory. Frame it as routine.
- Warning without consultation or clinical steps; or the opposite, not acting because confidentiality feels absolute. Inform clients of these limits at intake (see `14-ethics-law.confidentiality-and-limits`).
- Assuming one state's rule applies everywhere. Laws differ, and some states have no duty [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022]. The US statutes followed the 1976 California Tarasoff ruling [NCSL-Duty-to-Warn-2022]. The UK has no equivalent statute; disclosure to protect others is a public-interest decision under professional regulator guidance and data protection law, so consult before disclosing (expert consensus).
