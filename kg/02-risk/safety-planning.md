**Bottom line:** A safety plan is a short, prioritized, written list of what the client will do, in order, when a suicidal crisis starts, made with the client in their own words. The Stanley-Brown version has seven steps, from recognizing warning signs to making the environment safer and naming reasons for living [Stanley-Brown-SPI-Site]. With follow-up calls it was associated with about half the odds of suicidal behavior in a large cohort study [Stanley-etal-2018], though guideline bodies still rate the trial evidence as limited [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024].

## When to use

After any suicidal crisis, attempt or significant ideation, before an emergency department (ED) or inpatient discharge, and for anyone with intermediate or high acute or chronic risk [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024]. Use it instead of a no-suicide contract.

## How it works

Suicidal crises rise and fall. A plan prepared while calm gives the client a sequence of rehearsed steps to ride out the peak and reach help, and it reduces access to lethal means during the window of highest danger [Stanley-Brown-2012]. Restricting access to means works partly because many people do not switch to another method, and those who do often use a less lethal one [Yip-etal-2012].

## Steps

Start by asking the client to tell the story of the most recent crisis, map how risk rose and fell, give the rationale, then build the plan together [Stanley-Brown-SPI-Site]:
1. **Warning signs**: the thoughts, images, moods, situations or behaviors that signal a crisis is starting.
2. **Internal coping strategies**: things the client can do alone to take their mind off the thoughts.
3. **People and social settings for distraction**.
4. **People to ask for help**, told explicitly about the crisis.
5. **Professionals and services**: the clinician, after-hours line, nearest emergency department, and crisis lines. US: call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org [Lifeline-988]; veterans and service members can dial 988 then press 1, or text 838255 [VCL-Site]; 911 for immediate danger. UK: Samaritans 116 123, free and open 24 hours [Samaritans-Site]; National Health Service (NHS) 111; 999 for immediate danger.
6. **Making the environment safer**: a lethal means conversation (below).
7. **Reasons for living** (optional).

Then discuss where the plan will be kept, who will see it, how likely the client is to use it, and barriers. Review and revise it at follow-up [Stanley-Brown-SPI-Site].

**Lethal means conversation (non-method level).** Ask, without judgment, what means the client has thought about and what is accessible at home. Agree concrete, voluntary steps: secure storage, temporary removal to a trusted person, or less on hand, decided with the client and family. Coordinate with prescribers on safer prescribing. Record what was agreed and who will do it. The US Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guideline suggests reducing access to lethal means (weak recommendation) and calls lethal means safety collaborative and voluntary [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024].

## Guidance

- If the client offers generic coping ideas → ask what actually helped last time and write their words. Because plans built from the client's own crisis are more usable [Stanley-Brown-2012].
- If the client cannot name anyone to contact → list services first and make building one contact a treatment goal. Because the plan must work on the worst night, not the ideal one (expert consensus).
- If the client is reluctant to change access to means → explore the reasons, offer temporary and partial options, and involve a trusted person with consent. Because voluntary, collaborative steps are the standard of lethal means safety [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024].
- If the client rates the likelihood of using the plan as low → find the barrier and revise now. Because a plan that will not be used gives false reassurance [Stanley-Brown-SPI-Site].
- If you work in a digital tool or electronic record → get written permission before reproducing or changing the Stanley-Brown form. Because the developers require permission for wording changes, translations and electronic use [Stanley-Brown-SPI-Site].

## Common problems

- The plan is written by the clinician and handed over. Build it with the client, in session.
- The plan is filed and never reviewed. Revisit it at each risk check and after any crisis.
- A plan replaces assessment or treatment. It is one component of care.

## Evidence

In a cohort comparison at nine Veterans Health Administration EDs (1,640 patients), the safety planning intervention plus at least two follow-up calls was associated with less suicidal behavior over six months (3.03% vs 5.29%; odds ratio 0.56) and roughly double the odds of attending outpatient care (odds ratio 2.06) [Stanley-etal-2018]. A meta-analysis of six studies (3,536 participants) found a relative risk of suicidal behavior of 0.57 (number needed to treat 16) but no effect on ideation [Nuij-etal-2021]. A related crisis response plan cut attempts compared with a contract for safety in a small randomized trial (hazard ratio 0.24) [Bryan-etal-2017]. Despite this, the 2024 VA/DoD guideline found insufficient randomized evidence to recommend for or against safety planning to reduce attempts [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024], and the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) uses "consider" wording for safety plans for people who self-harm, reflecting the lack of high-quality randomized evidence [Mughal-etal-2023]. Treat it as expected routine care with emerging, mostly non-randomized support.
