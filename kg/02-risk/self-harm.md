**Bottom line:** Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is deliberate damage to one's own body without intent to die, most often used to change an unbearable internal state or to affect other people [Nock-2010]. It is common in adolescents and young adults [Swannell-etal-2014] and raises the risk of later suicidal behavior [Ribeiro-etal-2016], so ask about suicidal intent for every episode. Respond with calm curiosity, assess function, and treat with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)-informed or dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) skills, not contracts or punishment.

## Diagnostic essentials

- **Terms differ by country.** US practice separates NSSI from suicide attempts; the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) records non-suicidal self-injurious behavior separately from attempts [Posner-etal-2011]. UK guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) uses "self-harm" for any intentional self-poisoning or injury, whatever the apparent purpose [NICE-NG225].
- **Diagnosis.** The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) lists NSSI disorder as a condition for further study, not an official diagnosis, and added symptom codes for current and past suicidal behavior and NSSI that can be recorded with any diagnosis [APA-DSM5TR-2022]. Self-injury occurs across many diagnoses and without one.
- **Functions.** Intrapersonal: to reduce aversive emotions or thoughts, or to produce a wanted state (for example to feel something). Interpersonal: to gain support or to escape unwanted demands [Nock-2010]. These are reinforcement processes, which is why the behavior persists despite its costs.

## Course and prevalence

Pooled lifetime prevalence in community samples is about 17.2% in adolescents, 13.4% in young adults and 5.5% in adults [Swannell-etal-2014]. Self-injury is most prevalent in adolescence and young adulthood [Nock-2010]. Prior self-injurious thoughts and behaviors roughly double the odds of a later suicide attempt (odds ratio 2.14), though they predict poorly for any one person [Ribeiro-etal-2016].

## Differential and comorbidity

- Suicide attempt versus NSSI: decided by intent, which can be mixed or change within one episode. Ask every time.
- Self-harm in eating disorders, substance use, psychosis (for example command hallucinations) and autism needs condition-specific assessment.
- Common co-occurring problems: depression, anxiety, trauma, emotion dysregulation, and personality disorder features.

## CBT approach in brief

**Assess:** onset, frequency, recent episodes, triggers, function, urges, what stops the client, suicidal intent per episode, whether wounds needed or received medical care, and who knows. Do not ask for method detail beyond what is needed for medical safety.

**Respond:**
- If a client shows or discloses self-injury → stay calm, thank them, and ask what it does for them. Because the behavior serves a function that treatment must replace [Nock-2010].
- If any episode involved intent to die or the client is unsure → complete a suicide risk assessment and safety plan. Because self-injury raises later suicide attempt risk [Ribeiro-etal-2016].
- If wounds may need care → arrange medical assessment the same day. Because physical safety comes before therapy work (expert consensus).
- If you assess a client after self-harm and safety concerns remain → arrange follow-up within 48 hours, ideally with the same clinician. Because repeat self-harm is most likely in this period, and UK guidance sets this aftercare standard [Mughal-etal-2023].
- If a service wants to rate the client low, medium or high risk from a tool → assess needs and formulate instead. Because NICE advises against using tools or global stratification to predict repetition or to decide care [NICE-NG225].
- If the client is a minor or an adult at risk → consider safeguarding duties. See `02-risk.safeguarding`.

**Treat:** functional or chain analysis of recent episodes, then replacement skills matched to function (emotion regulation and distress tolerance for intrapersonal functions; assertiveness and problem solving for interpersonal ones), cognitive work on self-criticism, and planned urge management. For adults, NICE recommends considering a CBT-informed intervention of about 4 to 10 sessions [Mughal-etal-2023]. For young people who self-harm often, DBT for adolescents (DBT-A) can be considered [Mughal-etal-2023].

Evidence: in adults, CBT-based therapy may reduce repetition (low certainty) [Witt-etal-2021a]. In adolescents, DBT-A reduced repetition (30% vs 43%; odds ratio 0.46; high certainty, 4 trials) [Witt-etal-2021b]. Across 18 trials, DBT reduced self-directed violence (d = -0.32) [DeCou-etal-2019].

## Risk notes

- Example phrasing: "A lot of people who hurt themselves do it to get through feelings that seem unbearable. What does it do for you?"
- Avoid: "You need to promise to stop before we can work together." Making care conditional increases concealment (expert consensus).
- Avoid visible shock or disgust; avoid long descriptions of injuries in session or notes.
- Record each episode's intent, function, medical severity, and the plan.
- Crisis contacts: US 988 (call or text) [Lifeline-988] or 911; UK Samaritans 116 123 [Samaritans-Site], National Health Service (NHS) 111, or 999.
