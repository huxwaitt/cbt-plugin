**Bottom line:** Suicide risk assessment is a structured clinical conversation about ideation, plan, intent, past behavior, access to lethal means, risk factors, warning signs and protective factors, ending in a formulation and a plan. No risk factor, tool or risk category predicts who will die by suicide much better than chance [Franklin-etal-2017]. So the output is a shared understanding of what is driving risk now and what will reduce it, never a label that decides whether someone gets care [NICE-NG225].

## When to use

- At intake, and whenever a screen is positive (for example Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) item 9 above zero or any yes on the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) screener).
- When warning signs appear: talk of wanting to die, hopelessness, feeling trapped or a burden, rising substance use, agitation, sleep change, withdrawal, rage or extreme mood swings [Lifeline-988].
- At high-risk transitions: after a crisis, loss, hospital or emergency department (ED) discharge, or a change in care.

## Steps

1. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, using the word "suicide" (see `02-risk.asking-about-suicide`).
2. Ideation now and at its worst: frequency, duration, controllability, deterrents. In adolescent suicide attempters, worst-point lifetime ideation on the C-SSRS predicted attempts during follow-up, while another ideation scale did not [Posner-etal-2011].
3. Plan, preparation and intent. Establish whether a plan exists, whether preparatory steps were taken, and how strong the intent is. You do not need graphic detail.
4. Past behavior: attempts, interrupted or aborted attempts, preparatory acts, self-harm.
5. Access to lethal means, including anything the client has thought about using.
6. Risk factor domains: self-directed violence history, psychiatric conditions and treatment, current symptoms, social determinants and adverse events, lethal means, physical health, demographics [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024].
7. Protective factors: reasons for living (such as children), coping skills, connectedness, psychosocial stability, treatment engagement [MIRECC-TRM-2024].
8. Collateral information where consent and safety allow.
9. Formulate "why now". Defeat and entrapment drive ideation, while access to means, capability, planning, impulsivity, mental imagery, exposure and past behavior govern the move to action [OConnor-Kirtley-2018]. Pain plus hopelessness, low connectedness and capability is a compact alternative frame [Klonsky-etal-2021].
10. Rate acute and chronic risk separately if your setting requires it [Wortzel-etal-2014], and tie each level to actions. High acute risk (intent to die and unable to stay safe without outside help) typically means hospitalization; intermediate acute risk (ideation, but able to stay safe) means considering hospitalization or frequent contact, reassessment, safety planning and lethal means counseling [MIRECC-TRM-2024].
11. Agree the plan with the client: safety plan, lethal means steps, contact frequency, treatment targets.

## What to record

Ideation, plan, intent, behavior, means access, risk and protective factors, warning signs, the formulation in two or three sentences, any risk level with its rationale, the plan, and who was consulted. See `02-risk.risk-documentation`.

## Guidance

- If a client denies ideation but warning signs are present → name what you notice and ask again later in the session. Because warning signs mark acute risk over minutes to days [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024].
- If a client denies current ideation → still ask about worst-ever ideation and past attempts. Because worst-point ideation carries predictive information [Posner-etal-2011].
- If the service asks for a risk category → record it with the drivers and the actions it triggers. Because a bare label has poor predictive value [Carter-etal-2017].
- If intent or preparation is present → move to `02-risk.crisis-response` the same session and consult. Because intent with inability to stay safe defines high acute risk [MIRECC-TRM-2024].
- If the client cannot agree a plan or you cannot judge safety → do not end the session; get same-day consultation or emergency evaluation (US 911, UK 999), and offer 988 (US) or Samaritans 116 123 (UK) as a bridge. Because high acute risk calls for action before the client leaves [MIRECC-TRM-2024].

## Common problems

- Treating a "low risk" rating as reassurance. With 56% sensitivity, roughly 44% of later suicides occurred in people not classed high risk [Large-etal-2016] (see below).
- Checklist interviewing that loses the alliance. Ask in a conversational order and reflect what you hear.
- Assessing once at intake and never again. Risk is dynamic; see `02-risk.ongoing-risk-monitoring`.

### Why prediction fails, and what to do instead

A meta-analysis of 365 studies found that risk factors predicted suicidal thoughts and behaviors only slightly better than chance, with no improvement over 50 years [Franklin-etal-2017]. Prior self-injurious thoughts and behaviors roughly double the odds of a later attempt, yet give areas under the curve of only 0.60 to 0.62 [Ribeiro-etal-2016]. "High-risk" categories had 56% sensitivity and 79% specificity [Large-etal-2016], and a pooled positive predictive value for suicide of 5.5% [Carter-etal-2017]. The UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) therefore advises against using risk tools or global low, medium or high stratification to predict suicide or to decide who is treated or discharged [NICE-NG225] [Mughal-etal-2023]. In the US, The Joint Commission (TJC) expects risk stratification, while the Department of Veterans Affairs and Department of Defense (VA/DoD) guideline finds no evidence favoring any one stratification method [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024]. Reconcile the two by stratifying only to choose actions and resources, never to withhold care.
