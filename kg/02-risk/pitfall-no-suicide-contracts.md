**Bottom line:** No-suicide contracts have no evidence that they prevent suicide and no evidence that they protect clinicians from liability [Rudd-etal-2006a] [Lewis-2007]. In a randomized trial, a crisis response plan reduced suicide attempts compared with a contract for safety [Bryan-etal-2017]. Replace the promise with a collaborative safety plan and a commitment to treatment.

## What it looks like

- "Can you promise me you won't do anything before next week?" as the last line of a risk conversation.
- A signed "no-harm agreement" in the chart, recorded as the risk management plan.
- Continuing therapy made conditional on the client never self-harming or never having suicidal thoughts.
- The client says "yes" quickly to end the conversation, and the clinician feels reassured.

## Why it happens

Contracts are quick, feel like action, and seem to document that risk was addressed. They became widespread without evidence that they work [Rudd-etal-2006a]. In one survey of 420 mental health practitioners, 85% used verbal no-suicide assurances, 76% verbal agreements and 37% written contracts, and the authors warned of potential harm to clients and clinicians [Edwards-Sachmann-2010]. Clients may agree out of fear of hospitalization or to please the clinician, so a "yes" says little about safety and can make later disclosure harder.

## The fix

- If you are about to ask for a promise → instead ask, "What will you do if the thoughts get strong tonight?" and build the answer into a written safety plan. Because safety plans give concrete steps rather than an assurance [Stanley-Brown-SPI-Site].
- If the service form asks for a contract → record the safety plan and lethal means steps in its place. Because the safety plan is the better-supported alternative to contracts [Matarazzo-etal-2014].
- If you want the client's commitment → ask for a commitment to treatment: to attend, to use the plan, and to tell you honestly when things get worse. Because a commitment to treatment statement was proposed as the practice alternative to no-suicide contracts [Rudd-etal-2006a].
- If an existing client has an old contract on file → discuss it openly and replace it with a safety plan. Because research does not support contracts as a way to prevent suicide [Lewis-2007].
- If self-harm continues during therapy → treat it as information for a chain analysis, not a broken agreement. Because punishing disclosure drives concealment (expert consensus).

Example phrasing: "Rather than ask you to promise, I'd like us to write down exactly what you can do, and who you can reach, when the thoughts get strong. Then we can check how it worked next time."

Avoid: "If you hurt yourself again, I won't be able to keep seeing you."

## Sources note

Evidence against contracts comes from reviews [Rudd-etal-2006a] [Lewis-2007], practice surveys [Edwards-Sachmann-2010] and one randomized trial in US Army soldiers (97 participants; attempts 5% with a crisis response plan vs 19% with a contract; hazard ratio 0.24) [Bryan-etal-2017]. The trial is small and in one population, but no evidence supports contracts.
