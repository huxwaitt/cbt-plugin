**Bottom line:** Ask about suicide directly, using the word, in a calm and matter-of-fact tone, and move from passive wishes to active thoughts, plans, intent and behavior. Asking does not plant the idea: studies of asking about suicide found no significant increase in suicidal ideation [Dazzi-etal-2014]. How you respond to a "yes" (steady, thankful, curious) strongly shapes whether the client keeps telling you the truth (expert consensus).

## The principle

Many clients do not volunteer suicidal thoughts, and many expect alarm or judgment. A direct, normalized question gives permission to answer honestly. The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) shows the useful order: a wish to be dead, then active thoughts of suicide, then method thinking, intent, plan and any preparatory or suicidal behavior [Posner-etal-2011] [C-SSRS-Site]. Its developers state that no mental health training is required to ask the screener questions [C-SSRS-Site]. The aim of the first conversation is understanding, not reassurance or persuasion.

## Guidance

- If you are about to ask → introduce it as routine, then ask plainly with the word "suicide". Because euphemisms such as "hurting yourself" blur suicidal and non-suicidal acts and are easy to deny (expert consensus).
- If the client answers "no" too quickly → slow down, ask about a wish to be dead or to not wake up, and ask about the worst time in their life. Because passive wishes and past peak ideation are part of the severity picture [Posner-etal-2011].
- If the client says yes → thank them, stay calm, and ask how often, how strong, and what stops them acting. Because a calm response keeps disclosure going and the answers shape the plan (expert consensus).
- If the client has thought about how → establish whether there is a plan, intent, preparation, and access to the means, without asking for graphic detail. Because intent, plan and preparation define severity and drive the next step [C-SSRS-Site].
- If the client reports intent, a worked-out plan with intent, or any suicidal or preparatory behavior in the past three months → stop routine work and move to `02-risk.crisis-response` (crisis contacts: US 988 [Lifeline-988] or 911; UK 999, National Health Service (NHS) 111 or Samaritans 116 123 [Samaritans-Site]). Because these answers trigger behavioral health consultation and safety precautions on the C-SSRS triage [C-SSRS-Site].
- If the client is hopeless about therapy → reflect the hopelessness and ask about reasons for living and what has helped before. Because hopelessness is a warning sign [Lifeline-988] and an identified reason for living (such as children) is one feature that separates intermediate from high acute risk [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024].
- If you notice behavior changes the client has not mentioned → describe what you see and ask what it means. Because warning signs are person-specific changes in thoughts, feelings and behavior [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024].
- If the client fears hospitalization or loss of control → explain honestly what you would do: hospital is typically for someone who intends to die and cannot stay safe even with help, while people with suicidal thoughts who can stay safe are usually helped through outpatient care and a plan made together. Because that is how acute risk levels map to care [VA-DoD-Suicide-CPG-2024], and fear of consequences drives concealment (expert consensus).

## Example phrasings

- "Many people who feel as low as you describe have thoughts of suicide. Have you had thoughts of killing yourself?"
- "Have there been times you wished you were dead, or wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?"
- "When was the worst it has ever been? What was happening then?"
- "You said you have thought about how. Have you made a plan, or done anything to get ready?"
- "On the days the thoughts are strongest, what stops you from acting on them?"
- "Thank you for telling me. That took courage, and it helps us work out how to keep you safe."
- "I'd like us to make a plan together for when these thoughts get strong. Is that okay?"
- Avoid: "You're not thinking of doing anything silly, are you?" It signals the answer you want.
- Avoid: "You wouldn't do that to your family, would you?" It adds guilt and invites concealment.
- Avoid: "Promise me you won't hurt yourself." No-suicide promises do not keep people safe (see `02-risk.pitfall-no-suicide-contracts`).

## Evidence

Reviews of studies that asked participants about suicide found none with a significant increase in ideation, and some suggest benefit in treatment-seeking groups [Dazzi-etal-2014]. The graded question order is validated as part of the C-SSRS [Posner-etal-2011]. Advice on tone and wording is expert consensus drawn from safety planning practice, which starts from a collaborative account of the client's most recent suicidal crisis [Stanley-Brown-SPI-Site].
